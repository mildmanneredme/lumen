11: ## Chapter 1
12: 
13: The crawl at the bottom of the screen said BREAKING, which in the taxonomy of modern cable news meant anything from a nuclear detonation to a celebrity's new haircut. Adrian Marsh reached for his coffee, already cold, and watched the helicopter footage circle a glass tower in San Francisco's South of Market district like a vulture that had spotted something interesting.
14: 
15: "Corporate espionage," the anchor said, her voice carrying the practiced urgency of someone who delivered catastrophe with the same cadence as weather reports. "Federal agents have arrested a suspect in what authorities are calling one of the most sophisticated breaches of a major technology company in recent years."
16: 
17: Adrian set the mug down. It was Monday evening. He was in his living room in Berkeley, in the craftsman bungalow he'd bought with his early-stage equity — the modest payout from rounds one and two, before the real money arrived for the people who stayed. The house was modest. His colleagues in the Computer Science department found it suspiciously so for a man who had been present at the founding of a company whose worth now dwarfed some fairly successful countries. What they didn't understand was the difference between being present at the founding and being present at the payday. Adrian had been pushed out long before the paydays came.
18: 
19: It had happened gradually — it always did. First the board expanded. Then the "growth advisors" arrived, men in expensive shoes who spoke about users as "addressable markets" and ethics as "brand risk." Adrian fought back. He argued for constraints on the behavioral prediction models, for transparency reports, for opt-out mechanisms that would have cost the company a fraction of its advertising revenue. He was told he was out of touch. He was told the market wouldn't wait. He was told, in a particularly memorable board meeting, that his concerns were "academically interesting but commercially irrelevant." His influence shrank with each funding round, each new executive hire, each quarterly report that proved the money people right and the ethics people expendable. By the end, he wasn't fired. He was simply no longer invited to the meetings that mattered.
20: 
21: He took the gracious exit. Cashed out his early equity — enough to be comfortable, nothing close to what the later rounds generated. Some nights, alone in the kitchen with nothing but the hum of the refrigerator, he let himself calculate what those later stakes would be worth today. The number was grotesque. The anguish was not that he had walked away on principle. The anguish was that he had been pushed out before he'd even had that chance. He'd given most of the proceeds away. Kept the house, the books, and a quiet, persistent guilt that no amount of philanthropic tax deductions could extinguish.
22: 
23: On screen, the footage cut to a perp walk. A young man, mid-twenties, East Asian, slight build, the kind of person you'd picture leaving a university lab after hours, still wearing his badge. He was being escorted from the building in handcuffs. And he was smiling. Not broadly, not triumphantly, but with a faint, unsettling curl at the corners of his mouth, as though he had finished something important and the handcuffs were merely an administrative detail. That expression bothered Adrian more than terror would have. Terror he could have understood. This was something else.
24: 
25: "The suspect, whose name has not been released, allegedly accessed proprietary AI systems at NovaMind Technologies—"
26: 
27: Adrian's hand stopped halfway to the remote.
28: 
29: NovaMind. He knew NovaMind. Everyone in his field knew NovaMind. They were one of the companies building what the industry euphemistically called "foundation models," artificial intelligence systems so large and so general-purpose that they functioned less like software and more like infrastructure. The electrical grid of the cognitive age. NovaMind's specialty was behavioral prediction: feed the model enough data about a person's digital footprint and it could forecast their decisions with an accuracy that made free will feel like a construct to put our minds at ease.
30: 
31: Adrian had written about this. He had, in fact, written compulsively about this — as if the writing could retroactively justify the years of fighting a tide he was never going to turn. He'd watched the generation of entrepreneurs who replaced him and saw a class corrupted beyond recovery, drunk on the power these systems gave them, blind to what that power was doing. He couldn't change them. He'd tried, and they'd shown him the door with a severance package and a polite suggestion that he "pursue his passion for education."
32: 
33: So he did. The next generation, the students filing into his seminars each fall with their laptops and their earnestness — they still had a chance. Teaching was his way of fighting back. It was the one lever he believed he could still pull. Three peer-reviewed papers, a book chapter, and an op-ed in *The Atlantic* that his department chair had called career-limiting. His argument was straightforward: systems like NovaMind's didn't just predict behavior — they created the conditions for moulding it. People became clay. The algorithms shaped them in whichever direction their operators desired, subtly, persistently, and without any form of detection. When you can model a population's decision-making at scale, the distance between prediction and manipulation is measured in one board meeting.
34: 
35: "—materials seized from the suspect's residence include academic papers and theoretical frameworks related to AI ethics and behavioral modeling, some connected to researchers at Bay Area universities—"
36: 
37: Adrian sat forward.
38: 
39: The anchor continued, but her words had become noise. On the lower third of the screen, a graphic listed the seized materials under the heading EVIDENCE. Most of it was technical: source code, network schematics, internal architecture documents that had no business being outside NovaMind's servers. But there, fourth on the list, displayed in the same font used to describe stolen proprietary algorithms:
40: 
41: *Theoretical frameworks: behavioral prediction ethics (Marsh, et al., 2024)*
42: 
43: His name. On national television. In connection with a federal arrest.
44: 
45: Adrian stared at the screen. His academic work. Mentioned on national television. Connected to someone in handcuffs.
46: 
47: *It's published work. It's public. Anyone can read it.* The thought arrived like a reflex, defensive and immediate. He turned it over, testing its weight. It was true. His papers were in undergraduate textbooks. His frameworks were cited in policy documents and congressional briefings. The connection between his work and this arrest was probably superficial — a researcher whose name appeared in a bibliography, nothing more.
48: 
49: But the man on screen had been smiling. And there was something about that smile, something about the calm certainty of it, that unsettled Adrian in a way he couldn't quite articulate. It was the expression of someone who believed in what they had done. Not a thief. A convert.
50: 
51: He wondered what the crime actually was. What had this young man accessed, and why? The anchor was talking about "proprietary AI systems" and "behavioural prediction models," the same vague language NovaMind's PR department used to obscure the specificity of what their technology could do. Adrian knew that specificity. He'd written about it. The question gnawing at him was not whether this man had read his work — of course he had, if AI ethics papers were in the seized materials — but what he had done with it, and what that said about NovaMind's systems being worth breaching in the first place.
52: 
53: His phone buzzed. A text from Lena Chen, his department chair:
54: 
55: *Seeing this. You okay?*
56: 
57: Then another, from his graduate student, Marcus:
58: 
59: *Prof Marsh, your name is literally on CNN right now*
60: 
61: Then three more in rapid succession: colleagues, the university's PR office, and his mother in Portland, who watched cable news with the devotion of a medieval pilgrim.
62: 
63: Adrian silenced the phone and turned back to the screen. The helicopter footage was recycling. The same glass tower, the same orbital patience. But now the graphic had changed. Below the evidence list, the anchor was reading a statement from NovaMind's Chief Security Officer:
64: 
65: "We are cooperating fully with federal authorities. The breach targeted our most sensitive research division, including systems under development for government and defense applications. We believe the suspect acted as part of a larger network. We have not ruled out threats to national security, and the organizational sophistication of this breach suggests it was not an isolated act."
66: 
67: A larger network.
68: 
69: Not a lone wolf. Not a disgruntled employee. A *network*.
70: 
71: Adrian picked up his phone and began composing a reply to Lena. He typed something flippant — *Well, at least they spelled my name right* — and deleted it. He stared at the blank text field. He typed something carefully serious — *I'm concerned about the implications and will be consulting with legal counsel* — and deleted that too. Too formal. Too revealing. Too much like a man who had something to worry about. He sat with his thumbs hovering over the keyboard for a full thirty seconds, then typed: *Just saw it. Weird, right?* He hit send. It revealed nothing. That was the point.
72: 
73: He was thinking about the young man in handcuffs. The faint smile. The slight build. The expression of someone who had finished what he came to do.
74: 
75: He was thinking about the phrase *government and defense applications* and what that meant when spoken by a company whose core technology was predicting human behavior.
76: 
77: And he was thinking, though he would not admit this to anyone, not to Lena, not to Marcus, certainly not to his mother, that the most troubling thing about seeing his name on that screen was not the connection to a criminal investigation.
78: 
79: It was that he knew exactly what he would have gone for. If someone had asked him — hypothetically, academically, in the safety of a lecture hall — which systems at NovaMind were the most dangerous, which architectures concentrated the most power with the least oversight, which single points of failure could unravel the whole apparatus, he would have pointed them to the same places this young man had apparently gone. The knowledge sat in his chest like something swallowed wrong. Not guilt, not yet. Just the uneasy awareness that his work had described the terrain, and someone had walked into it.
80: 
81: He didn't know the motive. He didn't know the method. He didn't know if the man in handcuffs had read a single word he'd written or had arrived at the same conclusions independently — as anyone paying close enough attention eventually would. But the connection was there — in the seized materials, on national television — and Adrian could feel the shape of what was coming. Phone calls. Questions. The polite, methodical curiosity of people whose job it was to trace lines between academics who wrote about vulnerabilities and young men who exploited them.
82: 
83: Adrian Marsh had spent his career explaining how the machine worked.
84: 
85: Someone had been listening.

89: ## Chapter 2

91: The contract arrived on Tuesday at 3:47 a.m. Greenwich Mean Time, which was precisely the hour Raven was most productive. Not because she was a night owl in any romantic sense. She simply couldn't sleep. Her mind, which a series of expensive London psychiatrists had described as "atypical," operated on a schedule that bore no relationship to the rotation of the earth. She slept when it let her. She worked when it didn't.
92: 
93: Tonight, it didn't.
94: 
95: She was in her flat in Bermondsey, south London, a converted warehouse space she'd chosen for its acoustics. Not music - Raven was indifferent to music, one of the few sensory inputs that failed to register on her neurological switchboard — but ambient sound. The building hummed at 50 hertz, the resonant frequency of the UK electrical grid, and this steady drone functioned for her the way white noise machines functioned for normal people. Just not Raven.
96: 
97: The flat was organized with a precision that would make a spartan proud. Minimalist. Clinical. This was necessity. Every object lived in a designated location because the alternative was a kind of cognitive static that made sustained thought impossible. The kitchen surfaces were bare. The bookshelves were alphabetized by author, then by publication date, then by spine color within tied dates. The tools of her profession were stored in a climate-controlled cabinet in the back room that she kept locked with a biometric seal, not out of paranoia, but because the idea of someone touching them without her knowledge produced a physical discomfort in her chest that she had never successfully explained to another human being.
98: 
99: The contract came through the usual channel: a modified Telegram client — stripped of metadata logging, rerouted through compromised relay servers in jurisdictions with no cooperation treaties, messages set to self-destruct after a single read. The original developers would not have recognized what their code had become. Raven checked it on a dedicated laptop that never connected to any network associated with her real identity. The laptop itself was a ThinkPad she'd bought for cash at a market in Shenzhen during her last visit to see family, or rather, to walk through the city where her family lived without actually seeing them, a distinction her therapist had once called "significant" before Raven stopped seeing therapists.
100: 
101: The message was terse. They always were.
102: 
103: *Target: Male. Currently in federal custody, San Francisco County Jail, 850 Bryant Street. Holding cell 4-C, pre-trial isolation. Arrested on espionage charges. Initial hearing scheduled for Friday morning. Requirements: (1) Target must be extracted from custody prior to hearing for questioning. (2) A substitute of matching description will be provided and must appear at the hearing in target's place. (3) Target and substitute must subsequently be eliminated. Method: must appear natural or accidental. No forensic trace. No witnesses. Coordination with legal counsel on-site — contact details to follow. Payment terms: triple standard rate. Confirm acceptance.*
104: 
105: Raven read it three times. Not because she needed to — her recall was, in clinical language, "exceptional to the point of diagnostic interest" — but because this was not a standard contract. This was an operation. Extraction, substitution, a hearing, and then elimination — four stages, each with its own failure modes, each dependent on the one before. She didn't know why the target needed to be extracted rather than simply killed. She didn't know who was paying, or why they needed a double at the hearing. The intermediary system, four layers of cutouts between her and whatever power was orchestrating this, ensured mutual anonymity with the reliability of a Swiss watch.
106: 
107: She didn't need to know why. The "why" was someone else's puzzle. Hers was the "how."
108: 
109: The "how" was always the interesting part.
110: 
111: She'd been this way since childhood. At seven, in the flat in Bermondsey where her grandmother watched her while her parents worked double shifts at the restaurant, she had disassembled the lock on the back door — not to escape, not out of rebellion, but because the mechanism fascinated her. Twelve components. She laid them out on the kitchen table in the order she'd removed them, memorized the sequence, and reassembled the lock before her grandmother woke from her afternoon nap. Then she did it again, faster. And again. By the fourth attempt, she could do it in under ninety seconds with her eyes closed. Her grandmother found her sitting at the table, perfectly still, and asked what she'd been doing. "Practising," Raven said. She did not specify what.
112: 
113: Raven pulled up a second laptop, this one for research, routed through a chain of VPNs that would make a signals intelligence analyst weep, and began her assessment.
114: 
115: San Francisco County Jail. 850 Bryant Street. She'd never worked inside it, which was good. No two jobs were the same — there were always different ways to approach them, and that was the point. She pulled architectural plans from a public records request the City of San Francisco had fulfilled in 2019 as part of a lawsuit about conditions. Americans and their Freedom of Information obsession — it was, from her professional perspective, genuinely endearing. The plans showed a standard mid-century detention facility, retrofitted multiple times, with the predictable vulnerabilities of any building that had been patched and updated across six decades of changing codes and budgets.
116: 
117: Pre-trial isolation. Cell 4-C. She'd need the internal layout of that wing specifically. The public plans wouldn't have that resolution. But the jail had been subject to a Department of Justice inspection in 2022, and the inspection report, again public record, included photographs of individual cells, measurements for ADA compliance, and a detailed description of the medical transport protocols.
118: 
119: Medical transport. That was the opening.
120: 
121: Raven smiled. It was a small, private expression that never quite reached her eyes, less an indication of happiness than of a circuit in her brain completing a connection. The DOJ report noted that inmates experiencing acute medical distress were transferred to the San Francisco General Hospital emergency department via a dedicated transport corridor — a loading bay on the facility's south side, staffed by two corrections officers and one medical orderly. The route between the jail and the hospital was eleven minutes by ambulance. Eleven minutes of vulnerability.
122: 
123: She began to think. The operation had four stages, and each one had to work perfectly or the entire thing collapsed. This was not a simple elimination. This was choreography.
124:

126: 
127: Stage two: extraction. During the medical transport — those eleven minutes of vulnerability — the target would be intercepted and removed. The substitute, a man of matching build and approximate resemblance, would take his place. The substitute had been provided by the client; Raven didn't need to source him. She needed to choreograph the switch. The transport corridor. The eleven-minute window. Two corrections officers to manage. This was the most exposed stage, and Raven would handle it personally.
128: 
129: Stage three: the hearing. The substitute would appear at the initial hearing, coached on the basics but operating under the guidance of a syndicate lawyer who would manage the legal proceedings. The substitute would enter a plea, answer minimal questions, and maintain the illusion of continuity. He wouldn't know the details of the real operation — he was a tool, not an insider.
130: 
131: Stage four: elimination. After the hearing, the substitute would die in custody. The real Daniel Yoon would vanish from the public record, and the man left in his place would become its final entry. The client wanted the case closed before anyone could compare the two stories.
132: 
133: The plan assembled itself in her mind as a sequence of obligations. A transfer. A hearing. A death recorded under the wrong name. She did not think about the men who would pass through it. She thought about the report she would send when it was finished.

146: 
147: By Friday, he would be gone from the public record. Someone else would be standing in his place.

151: ## Chapter 3
152: 
153: "The first generative model that attracted mainstream attention," Adrian said, advancing the slide, "was not, as most people assume, a large language model. It was a generative adversarial network — a GAN — developed for image synthesis in 2014. The fundamental architecture involved two neural networks in competition: one generating images, the other trying to detect which images were fake. Can anyone tell me why this adversarial structure matters?"
154: 
155: Silence. Forty-three undergraduates in Introduction to AI Systems, a Tuesday afternoon section that met in Wheeler Hall's tiered lecture room, and not one of them was thinking about generative adversarial networks. Adrian could see it in the way they sat — leaning forward instead of back, phones in laps instead of bags, the electric, barely contained energy of people who wanted to talk about something other than what they were being taught.
156: 
157: A hand went up. Third row, left side. A sophomore named Priti who usually asked good questions.
158: 
159: "Professor Marsh — is it true the suspect in the NovaMind case was a student here?"
160: 
161: Adrian paused. The name had been released that morning. Daniel Yoon. He searched his memory and found a dim image: a quiet kid in one of his sections two years ago, maybe three. Sat near the back. Submitted his assignments on time. Asked a sharp question once about the ethical implications of recommendation algorithms that Adrian had used in a subsequent lecture without attribution. Bright. Nothing remarkable beyond that.
162: 
163: Adrian set the clicker down on the podium. He could feel the room tilt toward him, forty-three pairs of eyes waiting to see how he'd handle this.
164: 
165: "It's a fair question, Priti," he said. "But this isn't the place for it. If you want to discuss it, come to office hours. Right now we've got GANs to get through. Slide fourteen."
166: 
167: A murmur of disappointment. Another hand.
168: 
169: "But did you know him? Daniel Yoon? They said he had copies of your papers—"
170: 
171: The room went still. Adrian looked at the student who'd asked — a junior he didn't recognise — and then at the podium, where his lecture notes sat in their neat stack.
172: 
173: "I did," he said, quieter now. "He was in one of my sections a couple of years back. Bright kid. Quiet. Asked good questions." He stopped. The image surfaced unbidden — Yoon sitting near the back of a room not unlike this one, hunched over his laptop, always a little apart from the others. What had happened between that student and the one on the news? What turns a sharp, quiet kid into someone who breaks into one of the most powerful companies on the planet?
174: 
175: The silence stretched. Nobody moved.
176: 
177: "Right," Adrian said, blinking himself back. "Let's get back on track. Generative adversarial networks. The discriminator function."
178: 
179: He advanced to slide fifteen. The room settled, reluctantly, into the shape of an actual lecture. Adrian talked about discriminators and generators and the mathematical elegance of adversarial training, and he was good at it — he could feel the room begin to engage despite itself, drawn in by the architecture of his argument as he hit his stride.
180: 
181: Then he saw the two men standing at the glass panel beside the lecture hall door.
182: 
183: One was mid-forties, medium height, dressed in a suit that communicated law enforcement as unmistakably as a white coat. He'd actually chosen his tie that morning. It was burgundy. The other was younger, bigger, standing slightly behind — arms crossed, face blank, the posture of someone whose job was to watch.
184: 
185: Adrian looked at the clock. Twenty minutes left in the session.
186: 
187: "Well," he said, "fair to say we're getting a bit distracted today, so let's wrap it up. I want you to read through the rest of the deck before Thursday. And remember..."
188: 
189: "All the semester's decks are available online," the class droned in unison, already closing laptops and shoving notebooks into bags.
190: 
191: "Doesn't hurt to look ahead every now and again," Adrian said, a flicker of his teacher persona surfacing through whatever else was pulling at his attention.
192: 
193: Adrian gathered his notes, powered down the projector, and walked to the door.
194: 
195: The man in the burgundy tie was waiting in the corridor. Up close, he was well dressed in a way that felt anachronistic — pressed suit, polished shoes, an attention to appearance that most detectives had abandoned a generation ago. The last of the old guard. Not the FBI agents Adrian had met at conferences, who tended toward the aggressively nondescript. This man had standards. Adrian appreciated the effort.
196: 
197: "Professor Marsh? I'm Detective James Morrow, San Francisco Police." He held up a badge wallet. "This is my colleague, Detective Santos." The younger man nodded once, said nothing. "Is there somewhere we could talk?"
198: 
199: Adrian nodded obligingly and gestured to his office down the hall. The institutional reflexes of an academic who had spent twenty years in a system built on the assumption that cooperation was a virtue kicked in before his survival instincts could mount a counterargument.
200: 
201: "So how can I help you gentlemen, interested in taking a course on AI?" he joked, trying to cut through the atmosphere. Both detectives snickered and walked in after Adrian. Santos took a position near the wall, standing. Adrian moved behind his desk and sat — not a conscious decision, but the desk was wide and solid and it put something between him and the room.
202: 
203: Morrow entered the office and performed the subtle environmental scan that was the law enforcement equivalent of a professor surveying a student's bookshelf: the rapid, trained eye cataloging the room for information about its occupant. Adrian watched him note the bookshelf — three shelves of AI and machine learning texts, two shelves of philosophy, one shelf that was entirely Ursula K. Le Guin, which usually prompted questions from visitors but apparently did not register as evidence. Morrow settled into the chair across Adrian's desk. The desk was organized in the manner of a man who believed in organization but had been gradually losing the war against entropy: neat stacks of papers that had begun to lean, a coffee mug holding pens that should have been in a drawer, and a framed photograph of Adrian at a conference seven years earlier, shaking hands with someone the detective would not recognize but who was, in fact, the person Adrian tried hardest not to think about.
204: 
205: Kai Nakamura. Twenty-nine years old in that photograph. Already a billionaire. Not yet a stranger.
206: 
207: Slowly, Morrow extracted a notepad from his coat chest pocket, clicked a pen, and without raising his eyes, uttered his first words. "You've seen the news, I assume."
208: 
209: "My mother called me," Adrian said. "She treats CNN as a religious obligation. She wanted to know if I was going to be arrested."
210: 
211: "Are you?"
212: 
213: "I told her no. She seemed unconvinced. She's been unconvinced about most of my life choices since I left pre-med."
214: 
215: A small smile from Morrow. Good. Adrian used humor the way other people used locked doors — to control the pace of an interaction and keep visitors at a comfortable distance.
216: 
217: "Professor Marsh, I'm going to be direct with you, because I think you'd prefer that to the alternative."
218: 
219: "The alternative being?"
220: 
221: "Twenty minutes of small talk about your research before I get to the point. I've read enough about you to know you don't like wasting time."
222: 
223: "You've read about me."
224: 
225: "I've read your papers. Well — my technical people read your papers and then explained them to me in English. You've written compulsively about AI systems like the ones at NovaMind. Behavioral prediction models. The architecture of control — I think that's a phrase you coined?"
226: 
227: "It is," Adrian replied, something tightening in his chest at the pointed reference. His own words, quoted back at him by a detective in a burgundy tie.
228: 
229: "The suspect we arrested yesterday, the one who was all over the news, he had your work. Not just citations. Annotated copies. Marginal notes. He'd been studying your frameworks, working through the weaknesses you described."
230: 
231: Morrow let that settle. Then, quieter: "He hasn't said a word since his arrest. Not to us, not to his public defender, not to anyone. Whatever he was doing inside NovaMind's systems, he'd rather face federal prison than explain it."
232: 
233: The pause that followed was different from the ones before — heavier, more deliberate. The warmth drained from Morrow's voice like water from a basin.
234: 
235: "Did you know Yoon personally, Professor? He seemed to know you very well. You had a real impact on him."
236: 
237: Adrian felt Santos shift against the wall behind him. He did not turn around. His hand found the arm of his chair and he leaned back — a half-inch, maybe less, but enough to feel the distance open between himself and the detective's question. This was the pivot — the moment the conversation stopped being collegial and started being an interrogation. Adrian recognised it because he'd studied the architecture of persuasion systems for twenty years, and this was one: the friendly approach, the shared joke, the sudden blade.
238: 
239: "Daniel Yoon took one of my undergraduate sections," Adrian said carefully. "Two or three years ago. Bright kid. Nothing remarkable — I have three hundred students a year. Sad to see where his mind took him." He let the sentence sit, then added: "Detective, my work is published and publicly available. I'm cited in undergraduate textbooks. If the suspect also had a copy of *Introduction to Machine Learning* by Alpaydin, I trust you're not interrogating the publisher."
240: 
241: "No, sir. But Alpaydin didn't map the specific vulnerabilities in NovaMind's behavioral prediction architecture. You did."
242: 
243: That landed. Adrian kept his face neutral, but Morrow's eyes were sharp — not aggressive, just attentive, watching the board after moving a piece.
244: 
245: "I mapped *theoretical* vulnerabilities," Adrian said carefully. "In a generalized class of systems. My work doesn't mention NovaMind by name."
246: 
247: "It doesn't need to. NovaMind is the market leader. If you describe the vulnerabilities of the tallest building in the city, you don't have to print its address."
248: 
249: Adrian studied the detective. There was something about Morrow, a directness, a lack of pretense, that was disorienting in its simplicity. Most people who entered Adrian's office wanted something and tried to hide it. Morrow wanted something and was putting it on the table like a man dealing cards face-up.
250: 
251: "What do you need, Detective?"
252: 
253: Morrow leaned forward slightly. "The espionage case is more complicated than what's been reported. The systems the suspect accessed — I can't give you details, not yet — but my team doesn't fully understand what they are. We have engineers from NovaMind explaining their own technology to us, and I trust them about as far as I can throw their stock options." He paused, seeming to hear himself. "Look — I'm not saying you're involved. But your help in understanding what's at stake here would really help. I need an independent expert who understands these systems and has no financial connection to the company."
254: 
255: "You need a translator."
256: 
257: "I need someone who can look at what was accessed and tell me what it actually means. Not what NovaMind says it means. What it *actually* means."
258: 
259: Adrian considered this. The responsible thing, the safe thing, was to decline. Refer Morrow to a colleague. There were dozens of qualified people who could consult on AI systems without the complication of having their name already entangled in the case. The smart play was to smile, offer a list of names, and return to the comfortable business of writing papers about what was wrong with the world without ever having to touch it.
260: 
261: "I should tell you," Morrow added, "this is voluntary. You're not a suspect. You're not under investigation. I'm asking for your expertise as a consultant. You can say no, and I'll walk out that door and find someone else."
262: 
263: Adrian looked again at the photograph on his desk, then moved it aside to make room for Morrow's notepad. He did not yet know what he could offer beyond an interpretation of the evidence. But he could offer that.
264: 
265: "I'll need to see the case materials," Adrian said.
266: 
267: Morrow nodded, and something in his posture released — not dramatically, just a small unbinding of tension, a shift that communicated genuine relief rather than performative gratitude. "I'll have them couriered to you. Standard NDA, consultant agreement, the usual. Nothing classified — this is still SFPD jurisdiction. For now."
268: 
269: "'For now' is doing a lot of heavy lifting in that sentence."
270: 
271: "Yes, it is."
272: 
273: Morrow stood. Santos pushed off the wall without a word — the first movement he'd made in twenty minutes that wasn't breathing. Morrow extended his hand. Adrian shook it.
274: 
275: "We'll be in touch, Professor. For now."
