Chapter Forty One.

Morrow.

The building in Vauxhall didn't have a sign. Buildings that don't need signs are, in Morrow's experience, the ones you should worry about most.

Four days after arriving in London, Morrow was still being introduced to people by first name only. Their business cards, when they had them, listed consultancies; their briefing rooms had no windows and too many screens. They gave him exactly the information they wanted him to have, with the practiced warmth of professionals trained to make cooperation feel like friendship.

Morrow was cooperating. He was also keeping notes.

The MI5 liaison, a woman called Sarah who had the polished courtesy of a person who could ruin your career between sips of tea, had given him a thick folder of financial records connecting Adrian Marsh to the movement's operational funding. Wire transfers. Shell companies. Account numbers in jurisdictions that cooperated with Western intelligence when it suited them. The paper trail was comprehensive, well-organized, and damning.

It was also wrong.

Not wrong as in fabricated. The numbers checked out. The accounts existed. The transfers had occurred. Morrow had verified enough of them independently to confirm that the basic facts were solid.

Wrong as in too well-organized. Wrong as in formatted.

Morrow had spent two decades looking at financial records. He'd reviewed bank statements, forensic accounting reports, IRS filings, SEC disclosures, and unofficial ledgers that drug traffickers kept in spiral notebooks. Financial records generated by law enforcement workups had a particular look: messy, annotated, layered with analyst notes and cross-references and the visible residue of human effort. They looked like work.

These didn't look like work. They looked like a deliverable.

The formatting was consistent across every document. Same margin widths. Same header conventions. Same method of redacting sensitive information, a specific pattern of black bars that Morrow had never seen in any law enforcement or regulatory document. The dates were presented in a format that wasn't standard for American, British, or European financial institutions: YYYY.MM.DD with periods, not slashes or dashes.

Someone had generated these documents from a single system. Not a law enforcement database. Not a regulatory filing system. Something internal. Something corporate. Standardized output from a private organization's own reporting system.

He sat in his hotel room in Bloomsbury, the folder open on the bed, his notebook beside it. The room was adequate. The minibar was overpriced. The wallpaper had the aggressive inoffensiveness of a chain hotel that knew its guests had more important things to worry about than decor.

Internal document formatting, he wrote. Private. Corporate. Who generates financial intelligence in this format?

He photographed the header conventions and asked Santos to compare them with the public filings of the companies named in the case. A match would not prove who had supplied the dossier. It would give him something to test.

On a fresh page he drew two columns: What the records establish and What the briefing claims. The transfers belonged in the first. The assertion that every recipient was part of a terrorist network belonged in the second. He would verify those links himself.

Morrow capped his pen and went to find dinner. He was done accepting conclusions along with documents.

Tomorrow he had another meeting at the building with no sign. He'd go. He'd listen. He'd take whatever they gave him.

And he'd keep looking at the margins.
