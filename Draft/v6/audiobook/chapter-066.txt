Chapter Sixty Six.

Monday, 12:17 p.m. CET — twenty four hours remaining — London

The safe house was above a chip shop in Brixton, south London, and smelled of vinegar and frying oil. Nadia had arrived seven hours earlier on the reserved flight from Zurich, then a courier's car. She had barely noticed the room before laying out the phones.

The flat belonged to Danny, a Jamaican-British former postal worker who had spent thirty-two years delivering letters for the Royal Mail and had, in that time, developed an encyclopedic knowledge of London's geography, an unshakeable commitment to showing up on time, and an absolute contempt for any institution that failed to do the same. He'd been fired after whistleblowing about postal route changes designed to cut services to low-income neighborhoods. Nadia had covered the story in 2019, when no other journalist would touch it. Danny had never forgotten.

"Anything you need," he'd said when she called. "The flat's yours. I'll be downstairs running the shop."

What Nadia needed was a table, six burner phones, a stack of paper, three pencils, and silence. Danny provided all of these, along with unsolicited portions of fish and chips that appeared at the top of the stairs every four hours with the regularity of a man who had spent three decades making deliveries.

Nadia sat at the table and ran the switchboard.

This was the heart of the operation now. Not a server farm. Not an AI agent. Not an encrypted network humming in an Icelandic data center powered by volcanic energy. Just a woman with phones and a list of numbers and the discipline to make each call count.

Each team member reported through a relay known to Nadia. Most messages passed from a short call to a courier and then a dead drop. Emergency lines allowed direct contact when delay was the greater risk. The system kept complete instructions out of any one intercepted call. It could still lose a courier, a phone, a person. Her job was to notice the missing link before the next task depended on it.

She had nineteen burner phones remaining. At the current rate of consumption, she would run out in approximately twelve hours. She had already dispatched Danny's nephew, a seventeen-year-old named Devon who asked no questions and rode a bicycle with the kind of velocity that suggested a promising career in either cycling or crime, to purchase additional phones from shops across south London.

The status board was on the wall. Paper, pinned to a corkboard Danny had mounted for her. Each team member's codename, status, and last check-in time.

Scaffold (Ines): Active. Berlin. Network online — 3,100 active of 11,000 total; remainder went dark during AI compromise. Ready for data. Last check-in: 2 hours ago.

Locksmith (Andrei): Active. Tallinn. Five exploits verified clean; Barrington package has a conditional route. Drives in courier chain. Last check-in: 2 hours ago.

Courier (Tomás): Active. In transit Lisbon to Austin. Three devices with him; three in secondary courier chains. Estimated arrival: 3 hours. Last check-in: 1 hour ago.

Auditor (Sophie): Active. Geneva. Financial forensics framework 70% complete. Last check-in: 1 hour ago.

Timekeeper (Ólafur): Active. Portable timing system rebuilt in London. Reserved flight to Copenhagen; final simulations will continue there. Last check-in: 90 minutes ago.

Adrian: Active. Amsterdam. Final map corrections in progress. Completion expected by noon. Last check-in: 2 hours ago.

Seven people. European safe houses, one short relocation flight, and one westbound charter. Twenty-four hours remaining. All alive. All working.

Nadia studied the board and felt the weight of what it represented. Every name was a life she was responsible for. Every check-in was a confirmation that someone she'd spoken to was still breathing. The gaps between check-ins were holes in the world, minutes and hours during which anything could happen, and she would not know until the next call came.

Or didn't come.

She'd learned this feeling during the Snowden story. She'd been a junior reporter then, young, idealistic, convinced that the truth was a force that protected the people who told it. She'd learned otherwise. The truth was a weapon, and weapons didn't care about the people who wielded them. Sources got burned. Journalists got fired. People who did the right thing got punished for it while the people they exposed hired better lawyers and carried on.

But they carried on because no one could deliver the truth at scale. A single story could be suppressed. A single leak could be contained. A single whistleblower could be discredited, dismissed, destroyed.

Three thousand, one hundred surviving distribution nodes were harder to silence. She needed to get the evidence to them.

Nadia picked up a burner phone. Time for the next round of check-ins.

She started with Ólafur. The boy was in a flat in Shoreditch, a converted warehouse space that Nadia had arranged through a contact in the London tech scene who owed her a favor for not publishing a story about his company's data practices that would have ended his career.

Ólafur answered on the second ring. His voice was tight with concentration.

"Progress?"

"The timing infrastructure is coming together. I kept the original logic. The London machines aren't the Iceland servers, though. I'm measuring each route again and recalibrating the arrival offsets."

"How imperfect?"

"I need every activation inside the same four-hundred-millisecond spread. A late command could leave one target untouched while another is already sending a warning. The fastest warning takes five seconds, but I'm not using that as permission to be sloppy."

"Can you fix it?"

A pause. "I think so. I'm writing a compensation algorithm. It pre-calculates the expected network latency to each deployment point and adjusts the trigger timing accordingly. It's like... if you're conducting an orchestra and the violinist is sitting farther from you than the cellist, you give the violinist's cue slightly earlier so the sound arrives at the audience at the same time."

"Twelve hours for the calibration. Then we hold it steady until the signal."

"I know." Another pause. "Nadia? How is everyone?"

"Alive. Working. Thinking of you."

"Tell them I said hi."

Nadia ended the call and logged the result. Twelve hours was enough only if the portable setup stayed intact and he rechecked the routes after each relocation.

Next: Adrian. The relay to Amsterdam was a courier chain. Nadia called a number in Rotterdam, left a coded message, and within thirty minutes a man on a bicycle delivered a handwritten note to the houseboat on the Prinsengracht. The response came back the same way. Slow. Analog. The phone leg could still be intercepted. The paper handoff kept the complete exchange out of any single digital archive.

Adrian's response arrived within the hour: Maps finalized. All six complete. Preparing deployment packages for courier handoff. Status: operational. Morale: present.

That last word made Nadia smile. Morale: present. Not good. Not high. Just present. The honest answer of a man who was doing the thing anyway.

She checked the other reports. Sophie, still in the vault. Andrei's drives moving toward the integration points. Tomás was aboard the charter to Austin, reading the maintenance packet. The secondary devices were traveling separately.

Ines, the last check-in of the round. The relay to Berlin was a bicycle courier (Berlin, like Amsterdam, was a city where bicycle couriers attracted no attention) who carried messages to and from the Kreuzberg factory.

Ines's response: Network at full surviving capacity. 3,100 of 11,000 nodes active — AI compromise killed the rest. Enough for global distribution. Running continuous integrity checks. Ready for real data. Suggest you eat something. You sound tired in your messages.

Nadia looked at this response for a long moment. Then she picked up a piece of fish from the plate Danny had delivered an hour ago and ate it standing at the status board.

She wasn't tired. She was terrified. But terror, like morale, was present, and presence was enough.

Less than twenty-four hours. Seven people. One deadline.

Nadia picked up the next burner phone and kept the world turning.
