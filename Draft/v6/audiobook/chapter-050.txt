Chapter Fifty.

By 12:31 p.m. Central European Time on Sunday, every member of the council was on an emergency call. Each empire was burning.

Venn: "My cloud authentication layer has been breached. Twelve hospital systems, two national defense networks, four hundred commercial enterprises on compromised infrastructure."

Strutt: "The surveillance pipeline is reversed. Our own monitoring data is being exfiltrated. It was a trap."

Cole, the sound of something being thrown: "My entire network is down. Who built this thing?"

Barrington: "My internal communications are being exposed." The man who controlled narratives for a living, watching his own narrative collapse.

Laine: "If Venn's cloud fails, the medical infrastructure my foundation supports..." He couldn't finish.

At 6:31 that morning in Washington, Polk opened the emergency video session from his D.C. residence. Screens showed damage assessments. Phones rang constantly. Suspicion crystallized quickly: someone with insider knowledge of all six companies had designed these exploits.

Eyes moved across the grid of faces. The calculus of suspicion that powerful people perform when the enemy might be among them.

"We need to focus on containment," Kai said, and his voice was louder than any of them had heard it. Not shouting. Not close. But the volume was up, measurably, and his jaw was set in a way that drew Polk's attention. A muscle worked in his cheek. "Each of us needs our best people on damage control. Patching the exploits, stopping the exfiltration. That's the immediate priority. Not the movement. Triage."

Cole erupted. "These people just nuked our infrastructure and you want to give them a time out?"

"I want us to stop bleeding before we go after the people who cut us."

"The boy's right," Venn said.

Polk watched Kai as the call fractured into separate emergencies. Something was different on this call. The controlled neutrality that Kai wore to every meeting had slipped, just barely, revealing an edge beneath. It could be stress. Everyone was stressed. But Kai's stress looked less like fear and more like something under pressure that was threatening to surface.

"Tuesday noon, Central European Time," Polk said. "That is the recovery deadline. Contain the damage. Then the movement."

"If there's still a council by then," Cole said, before his feed cut out.

Kai sat back. His jaw unclenched. His hands, which had been pressed flat against the table, relaxed. The edge retreated behind the mask, so smoothly that Polk almost doubted he'd seen it.

Almost.
