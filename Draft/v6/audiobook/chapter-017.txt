Chapter Seventeen.

The decision arrived not as an epiphany but as an exhaustion — the point at which every alternative had been examined, stress-tested, and found insufficient. Adrian had spent five days since the Hunters Point meeting in a state of cognitive vertigo, cycling between the analytical frameworks of his professional life and the raw, ungovernable reality of what he'd seen.

He'd considered the options with the methodical thoroughness of a man trained to think in ethical systems.

Walk away: return to the comfortable architecture of academic life, write a paper about what he'd learned, and let the movement succeed or fail without him. This option had the advantage of personal safety and the disadvantage of moral cowardice — a trade-off he'd been making, in smaller ways, for twenty years.

Go to the FBI: report everything — the anonymous tips, the Hunters Point cell, the structural overview of Project Lumen — and let federal law enforcement handle it. This option had the advantage of institutional legitimacy and the disadvantage that the institutions in question were, according to every piece of evidence he'd seen, compromised by the very forces the movement was targeting. Harrison Polk's lobbying network didn't stop at Congress. It reached into the Department of Justice, the intelligence community, the regulatory agencies. Reporting to the FBI was, potentially, reporting to the enemy.

Continue alone: keep following the breadcrumbs, keep investigating, keep building the picture. This option had the advantage of independence and the disadvantage that Adrian Marsh was a professor, not an intelligence operative, and the people on both sides of this conflict had demonstrated a willingness to kill.

Or: the option that had been assembling itself in the back of his mind since the warehouse, taking shape with the quiet inevitability of a logical conclusion whose premises had been established one by one over the preceding weeks.

Go to Morrow. Tell him enough to stay connected to the official investigation. And then go deeper into the movement — not as an outsider following anonymous tips, but as a participant. An informant who could feed Morrow intelligence on the network while simultaneously learning enough about it to understand whether it was what it claimed to be, or something else entirely.

It was the worst option. It was duplicitous. It required lying to two sets of people simultaneously — the movement about his connection to law enforcement, and law enforcement about the depth of his involvement. It violated every principle of honesty and transparency he'd built his professional reputation on.

It was also the only option that gave him access to both sides. And access — the ability to see the whole picture, to evaluate both the threat and the response — was the only thing that could answer the question at the center of everything: was the movement justified?

He called Morrow.

"Detective, we need to talk. In person. Today."

"We have a meeting this morning, Professor. I was beginning to wonder whether you'd show." Morrow's voice carried a weight that Adrian couldn't parse — somewhere between professional caution and personal concern. "If you need more time, make it two o'clock. My office."

Adrian arrived at the Hall of Justice at 1:55 p.m., which gave him five minutes in the lobby to contemplate the building's grim architecture and the fact that a man had been murdered in this building a little over two weeks ago while in the custody of the police department he was about to deceive.

Morrow's office was on the fourth floor, a glass-walled space that communicated the detective's position in the department hierarchy: senior enough for an office, not senior enough for a corner one. Through the glass, Adrian could see him at his desk, studying something. Photographs, it looked like. Morrow looked up as Adrian knocked, gathered the photographs quickly — not quite hiding them but not leaving them visible — and waved him in.

"Sit down, Professor."

Adrian sat. The chair was uncomfortable — designed for utility, not for putting people at ease.

"I'm going to be direct with you," Morrow said. "I've received information suggesting that your involvement in this case has moved beyond consulting."

Adrian held the detective's gaze. This was the moment. The pivot point. What he said in the next sixty seconds would determine the trajectory of everything that followed.

"You're right," he said. "It has."

Morrow blinked. Whatever he'd expected — denial, evasion, outrage — straightforward confirmation was apparently not on the list.

"I was at a community forum in the Mission District two Thursdays ago," Adrian continued. "I spoke with someone, a young activist, who hinted at organized resistance. Not protest. Something structural. After the forum, I followed leads — anonymous tips I've been receiving — to a meeting in Hunters Point with what appears to be a cell of the underground network connected to Daniel Yoon."

Morrow's jaw tightened. His eyes narrowed. His hand, resting on the desk, closed into a loose fist and then opened again.

"You attended a meeting of a suspected criminal network. Without informing law enforcement. While consulting on an active investigation."

Adrian adjusted his glasses — pushed them up the bridge of his nose with one finger, a gesture he'd performed ten thousand times but that now felt like the only thing his hands knew how to do. "Yes."

"That's — Professor, that's obstruction at minimum. Depending on what was discussed, it could be conspiracy."

"I know what it could be. I'm telling you anyway."

"Why?"

"Because I can get deeper."

The words hung in the air between them. Morrow studied Adrian with the intensity of a man evaluating whether the person in front of him was an asset or a liability.

"Explain," Morrow said.

"The network is real. It's larger, more organized, and more ambitious than anything in your case file. They're not hacktivists. They're not protesters. They're building something — a coordinated operation targeting multiple major technology companies simultaneously. And they're being hunted. Someone killed Daniel Yoon to protect this operation's secrecy. The same people will kill others if they're found."

"And you want to — what? Join them?"

"I want to understand them. From the inside. The anonymous tips I've been receiving are leading me deeper into the network. The cell I met in Hunters Point is a low-level node. There's a hierarchy above them — leadership, specialist teams, a plan they call Project Lumen. I can follow the trail. I can learn the scope of the operation, identify key members, map the organizational structure."

"You're proposing to become an informant."

"I'm proposing to do what I've been doing — investigating — but with your knowledge and with the commitment to share everything I learn with you."

Morrow leaned back in his chair. The leather creaked. Through the glass walls of his office, the department floor was visible — detectives at their desks, the ambient machinery of law enforcement, the institutional normalcy of people doing difficult work within defined boundaries.

"The information I received," Morrow said carefully, "came from someone who wanted me to see you as a threat. Surveillance photographs. Communication records. Someone is tracking your movements, Professor, and they wanted me to arrest you."

"Who?"

"I don't know. Anonymous caller, courier delivery. But the quality of the surveillance — this isn't a street-level operation. Whoever's watching you has serious resources."

"The oligarchs." The word came out before Adrian could moderate it. "The companies being targeted. They know about the movement. They're looking for it. And they're using you — using law enforcement — to find it."

Morrow's expression didn't change, but something shifted behind his eyes. The moment when a piece of information aligns with something a person already suspected but hadn't yet articulated.

"That's a significant accusation."

"It's a logical inference. Someone with the resources to conduct surveillance on a university professor and deliver evidence to a police detective is not a random concerned citizen. The technology companies being targeted by this network have the capability, the motive, and the infrastructure to conduct exactly this kind of operation."

"You're saying they're feeding me information to steer my investigation."

"I'm saying the evidence that landed on your desk didn't come from nowhere. And I'm asking you to consider who benefits from you looking at me instead of looking at them."

The silence that followed was the longest of their acquaintance. Morrow was thinking — Adrian could see it in the stillness of the detective's face, the unfocused gaze of a man running calculations behind his eyes. James Morrow was a good detective. Good detectives didn't just follow evidence — they questioned where the evidence came from.

"If I agree to this," Morrow said slowly, "you report to me. Everything. Every meeting, every contact, every piece of information you receive. You don't freelance. You don't go rogue. You don't decide on your own what's worth sharing and what isn't."

"Agreed."

"And you understand that if you cross a line — if you participate in criminal activity, if you aid this network's operations — I will arrest you. Regardless of whatever larger picture you think you're seeing."

"Understood."

"And Professor — you should know that what you're proposing is dangerous. Not in the abstract, theoretical way you're used to. Dangerous in the way that gets people like Daniel Yoon found dead in a cell."

"I know."

Morrow looked at him for a long moment. Then he opened his desk drawer, removed a card, and slid it across the desk. It was a standard SFPD business card with a handwritten phone number on the back.

"That's my personal cell. Not the department line. If you need to reach me outside normal channels, use that. If you're in danger, call 911 first and me second."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I'm letting you walk into something that might get you killed because I think you're right that something bigger is happening here, and because every other avenue I've pursued has led me to evidence that arrives too neatly and answers too conveniently. But if this goes wrong—"

"It'll be on me."

"It'll be on both of us."

Adrian took the card. He stood. Morrow stood. They shook hands — not the perfunctory grip of their first meeting but something more deliberate, the handshake of two men entering into an arrangement they both knew was imperfect and possibly the only option that made sense in a situation where the normal options had been exhausted.

Adrian left the Hall of Justice. The San Francisco afternoon was clear and sharp, light that made the city's geometry look precise — the grid of streets, the vertical thrust of the skyline, the hard line of the horizon where the Bay met the sky. Everything had edges. Everything was defined.

He was an informant now. A man with two allegiances and two audiences, feeding information in both directions, standing in the exact space between the law and the lawless where the only certainty was that both sides would eventually demand he choose.

The phone in his pocket chimed.

A new message from the anonymous source: Good. Now the real work begins.

Adrian read it, pocketed the phone, and walked toward his car. Behind him, through the windows of the fourth floor, Detective James Morrow watched him cross the street, and wondered — not for the first time, but with a new and unfamiliar intensity — whether the man he'd just made a deal with was the solution to the case or part of the problem.

The answer, which neither of them knew yet, was both.
