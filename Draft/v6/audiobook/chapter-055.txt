Part Four. The Fall.

Chapter Fifty Five.

At 12:17 a.m. on Monday, Adrian Marsh was still staring at the phone. Seb had missed the 11:55 p.m. check-in. That was what Adrian would remember later: the way the worst news arrived with a silence where Seb's voice should have been. The burner sat on the scarred wooden table of a rented flat in Zurich. Nadia had reached the city ahead of him; he had come through Dusseldorf on a flight booked under a reserve identity.

Three minutes late could mean traffic. Five minutes could mean caution. Ten minutes meant Seb was running.

Twenty minutes meant Seb was dead.

Adrian stared at the phone as if will alone could make it vibrate. Around him, the safehouse smelled of stale coffee and old wallpaper paste. A crack ran through the ceiling plaster like a river delta, like one of Priya's network maps, he thought, and then hated himself for thinking it.

At twenty-two minutes, Nadia's burner buzzed instead. She answered, listened, and the color drained from her face like water from a cracked vessel.

"They got him," she said. "Seb's gone."

The words landed in the room like a grenade with the pin already pulled. Adrian felt his chest compress, felt the air leave the space as if someone had opened an airlock. He gripped the edge of the table with both hands.

"How?"

"The farmhouse. A courier found him in the upstairs room. Gunshot."

The farmhouse. The place they had cleared for the last meeting. He saw the coffee pot, the oil lamp, the muddy road beyond the doorway. Seb had stayed behind to finish the contingency instructions while the others moved toward Zurich.

Adrian closed his eyes. Four hours earlier he had been sitting across from Seb at that table. The older man had looked tired. Not the performative exhaustion of the overworked activist but the bone-deep fatigue of someone who knew what the next decision might cost.

"If something happens to me," Seb had said, "the operation continues."

Adrian had tried to stop him. Seb had repeated the instruction until he listened.

Now Seb was gone too.

Priya. Then the cell members exposed by the AI hack. Now the man who had started it all.

The movement wasn't just wounded. It was hemorrhaging.

Adrian opened his eyes. Nadia was watching him with that journalist's gaze, cataloging his reaction, filing it, already composing the narrative. Not out of coldness. Out of survival instinct. She processed the world by turning it into story, the same way he processed it through ethical frameworks. Right now both methods felt spectacularly useless.

Grief was — no. Not now. He could feel it pulling at him, the undertow of it, wanting him to sit with it, to think it through the way he thought everything through. But Seb wouldn't have sat. Seb would have been making calls already. Seb would have been three steps ahead, assigning roles, checking timelines, turning loss into momentum.

So that's what Adrian would do. All of it. Everything Seb would have done, because Seb couldn't do it anymore, and because the only framework that mattered now was the one that kept the people in this room alive and moving.

The grief could wait only if he kept moving. He opened his notebook to the instructions Seb had dictated.

"We have to tell the others," Adrian said.

"I know."

"And we have to keep moving. The window —"

"I know, Adrian."

The 48-hour window. The zero-day attack the AI had fired as its dying act, a parting gift from their murdered digital guardian, forcing every oligarch company into crisis mode. Engineers at six of the most powerful corporations in the world were currently scrambling to patch exploits they didn't yet understand, their attention turned inward, their surveillance apparatus running at reduced capacity.

It was the opening Seb had been waiting for.

And Seb would never walk through it.

Adrian released the table. His knuckles were white. He flexed his fingers and felt the blood return in tingling waves. An AI ethics professor. That's what he was. A man who wrote papers about hypothetical dilemmas in hypothetical worlds, who debated trolley problems in temperature-controlled lecture halls, who had built an entire career on the luxury of thinking carefully before acting.

That man was gone now. Or rather, that man was still here, but the world around him had changed so completely that his careful thinking would have to happen at a dead sprint.

"Call Ines," he said. "Use the Kreuzberg relay, the number Seb gave us for emergencies. Then get word to Andrei through the Tallinn dead drop. I'll reach Tomás and Sophie through Nadia's network."

Nadia raised an eyebrow. "You're giving orders now?"

"Somebody has to."

It wasn't bravery. It wasn't leadership. It was the simple, terrible mathematics of being the last person standing in a room that kept emptying. Seb was dead. Priya was dead. The AI was compromised. Every digital tool they'd relied upon was gone or turned against them. What remained was a scattered team of brilliant, frightened specialists, a journalist with an analog network built from decades of being dismissed by the mainstream, and an ethics professor who had spent the past months learning that the distance between theory and practice was measured in human lives.

Adrian picked up his burner phone. His hands were steady, which surprised him.

"Thirty-six hours," he said. "Seb started this. We finish it. We owe him that."

"We owe them all that," Nadia said quietly. "Priya too."

Yes. Priya too. The network mapper who had spent years as a double agent inside one of the oligarch companies, smiling at colleagues she was cataloging for exposure, her conscience eating through her like acid through copper. Adrian had inherited her work, her maps of internal architectures, her knowledge of where the data lived and how it flowed. He had taken it and run with it, using his years as a tech investor to fill the gaps her death had left.

He had never met her. He knew the intent look in her photograph and the tiny annotations in her diagrams, the routes she had checked, the gaps she had marked honestly. Her work was the closest he would ever come to hearing her explain it.

Finish the maps, he told himself. Plant the devices. Pull the trigger. Watch the data flood the world like water through a broken dam.

And then what? Democracy restored? The oligarchs dethroned? A better world, risen from the ashes of the old one?

Maybe this was how it always went. Build the towers. Watch them become prisons. Tear them down. The species lurching between control and resistance, always. The only question that mattered — the one Adrian couldn't answer yet — was whether anything better would rise in their place.

That was a question for after. Right now seven people had to finish the operation before Tuesday at 12:17 p.m., when the oligarchs expected to recover their surveillance. Thirty-six hours. Less if the engineers were ahead of schedule.

Adrian looked at Nadia.

"Let's go to work," he said.

He didn't say what they were both thinking: that the last three people who had led this operation were dead, and he had just volunteered to be the fourth.
