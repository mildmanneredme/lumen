Chapter Twenty Nine.

Tallinn in November was a city of contrasts so aggressive they felt like an argument.

The Old Town rose from the flatlands of the Estonian coast like a medieval fever dream: limestone walls, red-tiled rooftops, Gothic spires piercing a sky the color of wet concrete. Cobblestone streets wound between buildings that had been standing since the Hanseatic League traded furs and salt through this port six hundred years ago. Fat-bellied towers with names like Kiek in de Kok, "Peek into the Kitchen," a tower so tall that medieval guards could supposedly see into the homes below, anchored the corners of a defensive wall that still encircled the old city like the grip of a stone fist.

But step outside the wall, cross any of the streets that traced the old moat, and you entered one of the most digitally advanced societies on Earth. Estonia had rebuilt itself after the Soviet collapse as a nation that ran on code. Digital identity cards for every citizen. Online voting. E-residency for anyone in the world. A government whose cabinet meetings were paperless, whose tax system was automated, whose entire public infrastructure lived on a blockchain-based platform called X-Road that connected every government database in the country through encrypted, auditable links.

It was, Adrian reflected as he walked through the Viru Gate, a pair of round towers that served as the Old Town's eastern entrance, the perfect city for a man who made his living finding flaws in digital systems. Where better to hide a locksmith than in a country made entirely of locks?

There was something instructive in the juxtaposition, he thought, something that spoke to the fundamental tension of the age. Our species had always built fortresses, from the limestone walls of Tallinn to the encryption protocols of X-Road, and we had always produced the people who could breach them, and the cycle of building and breaching had accelerated with each generation until the walls were invisible and the keys were mathematical and the stakes were not a medieval city but the information architecture of civilization itself, and yet we persisted in the ancient belief that the next wall would be the one that held, that the next lock would be the one that couldn't be picked, as though the entire history of human ingenuity hadn't been a sustained demonstration of the opposite.

His Canadian passport, in the name of David Chen, had sailed through border control at Lennart Meri Airport without a second glance. Estonia's e-gates used facial recognition, a fact that would have troubled Adrian deeply in any other context but that currently served him well, since the face the system scanned matched the biometric data embedded in the passport chip, and the biometric data matched a carefully constructed identity that would withstand anything short of a deep intelligence background check.

He didn't want to think too hard about where the movement had obtained the capability to produce documents this convincing. That ethical question could wait in line behind the several dozen already queued up.

The address Seb had given him was in the Old Town, on a narrow street called Rataskaevu, Wheel Well Street, named for a medieval well whose waters were rumored to be poisoned. Adrian found this charming in a city that also had a street called Pikk Jalg (Long Leg) and another called Luhike Jalg (Short Leg). Estonian toponymy, it seemed, favored the literal.

The building was a four-story medieval structure with walls three feet thick and windows too narrow for a modern human to squeeze through, originally designed to prevent exactly that. The ground floor was a bookshop specializing in rare maps and nautical charts. The upper floors, according to the intercom directory, housed a translation service, a graphic design studio, and a company called Baltic Digital Consulting that Adrian suspected did not actually consult on anything Baltic or digital.

He pressed the buzzer for the fourth floor. Nothing happened. He pressed it again. Still nothing. He was about to press it a third time when a voice crackled through the speaker, not from the intercom, but from what sounded like a separate system entirely.

"Step two meters to your left."

Adrian stepped. He was now standing directly beneath an overhang, in a shadow cast by the building's medieval gutter system.

"Look up."

He looked up. A camera the size of a fingernail, almost invisible against the dark stone, stared back at him.

"You're David Chen. Canadian passport. Arrived Lennart Meri at 14:37 local time. Took the number four tram to Viru, walked from there. You stopped once at a coffee shop on Muurivahe and ordered a double espresso, which tells me you're tired. You're carrying a shoulder bag, left side, weight distribution suggests a laptop and printed documents. No phone. Smart. No watch. Smarter."

The voice was male, accented, and carried the flat affect of someone reading a diagnostic report rather than conducting a conversation.

"Come up. Fourth floor. The door will be unlocked. When you enter, stand still until I tell you to move. There are measures in the hallway."

"Measures?"

"Stand still and you won't find out what kind."

The door buzzed open. Adrian climbed four flights of stairs that creaked in a way that suggested the building's wooden bones were older than most nations. On the fourth floor, a single door stood ajar. He pushed it open and entered a short hallway lit by a single bare bulb.

He stood still.

Thirty seconds passed. Then: "Clear. Come forward."

Adrian walked through the hallway and into what had once been a medieval merchant's living quarters and was now the operational center of one of the most skilled exploit researchers in the world. He stood in the doorway with his hands at his sides, unsure where to put them, unsure where to stand, aware that every surface in this room belonged to someone else's purpose.

The room was large, low-ceilinged, with exposed timber beams dark with age. Three of the four walls were covered in acoustic foam. At a seam by the door, Adrian could see metal shielding beneath it. The fourth wall was dominated by a window that overlooked the rooftops of the Old Town, church spires backlit by the perpetual twilight of a Baltic November afternoon. Six monitors arranged in two rows of three occupied a desk that ran the full length of the room. Three keyboards. Two mice. A soldering station. And, incongruously, a small espresso machine that appeared to be worth more than everything else in the room combined.

Andrei Lepp sat in a high-backed chair at the center of the desk array, watching Adrian with the careful stillness of a man who had trained himself to register everything and reveal nothing.

He was forty-one but looked older, aged not by years but by the sustained expenditure of psychological resources. Thin, angular, with sharp features that might have been handsome in better light and more generous circumstances. His hair was dark, cut short, and his eyes were a pale blue that in certain light appeared almost gray. He wore a black thermal shirt, black jeans, and the expression of someone who had been expecting a package and was reserving judgment on whether the contents matched the description.

"You're the professor," Andrei said. It wasn't a question.

"And you're the Locksmith."

"I don't use that name. Codenames are an operational convenience, not an identity. My name is Andrei. Use it."

"Andrei. Thank you for agreeing to meet."

"I didn't agree. I was told. There's a difference." He gestured to a chair, the only other chair in the room, positioned precisely two meters from the desk. "Sit. Don't touch anything. The machines are running processes that will abort if they detect unauthorized interaction."

Adrian sat. He noticed that the chair was bolted to the floor.

"Your machines detect unauthorized interaction from a chair?"

"The chair has a pressure sensor that registers your approximate weight. If someone substantially lighter or heavier than the person I'm expecting sits in it, the system triggers a wipe protocol." A pause. "Also, the floor beneath the chair is rigged with a very sensitive vibration detector. If you shift your weight in a pattern consistent with reaching for a weapon, the system responds."

"What does 'responds' mean in this context?"

"It means I have twelve seconds to evaluate the situation before the room's Faraday shielding engages and every device in here begins a cascade destruction sequence. After which I leave through a door you haven't noticed yet, and you sit in a very dark, very electromagnetically dead room until the police arrive."

Adrian was very still. "You're serious."

"I spent a decade finding vulnerabilities in the most secure systems on the planet. Then I discovered that the same exploits I'd developed for NATO were being sold to the companies we were supposed to be defending against. Trust is not a word I use casually." Andrei's pale eyes locked onto Adrian's. "Seb says you're trustworthy. Seb's judgment has been wrong before. I'll make my own assessment."

"Fair enough."

"Tell me what you know about zero-day vulnerabilities."

Adrian organized his thoughts. This was a test, and he suspected the scoring was pass/fail.

"A zero-day is a software vulnerability that's unknown to the vendor, meaning they've had zero days to patch it. In the exploit market, zero-days are the most valuable commodities. A high-quality zero-day for a major operating system can sell for over a million dollars to government buyers. The market is split between white-hat researchers who disclose vulnerabilities to vendors for bounties and gray- and black-hat operators who sell them to intelligence agencies, private military contractors, or criminal organizations."

Andrei's expression didn't change, but something in his posture shifted, a fractional relaxation, like a safe door settling into its frame.

"Continue."

"You developed exploit chains, not individual vulnerabilities but sequences of exploits that, chained together, bypass multiple security layers. Each target company has different infrastructure, so each chain is bespoke. You're finding and weaponizing flaws in firewalls, intrusion detection systems, and encryption-at-rest protocols. The value of your work degrades over time because the targets are constantly patching and updating, which means every day between now and trigger day increases the risk that a critical exploit gets discovered and fixed."

"Good." Andrei reached for the espresso machine and began making two shots. "You understand the theory. Now let me show you the practice."

He pulled up a screen on the leftmost monitor. Lines of code scrolled in a terminal window, code Adrian couldn't read fluently, but he recognized the structure. Assembly language. Low-level, close to the hardware, talking directly to processors and memory addresses.

"This is one exploit in a chain of seven for the Machine's cloud infrastructure," Andrei said. "It targets a buffer overflow in their load balancer's TLS handshake, a flaw in how the system negotiates encrypted connections. When triggered, it allows code execution inside the load-balancing service. From there, the rest of the chain can escalate privileges, move laterally through the network, and access any data store the load balancer routes to."

"And the Cartographer's maps tell you where to go once you're in."

"Exactly. My exploits open the doors. Priya's maps tell me which rooms to enter. Without the maps, I'm breaking into a building the size of a small city with no directory and no floor plan." He handed Adrian an espresso. It was, Adrian had to admit, exceptional. "Which is why you're here. I need to know: can you finish what she started?"

"I can complete the structural mapping. The high-level architecture: where data stores live, how they're interconnected, what protocols govern the traffic between them. Priya was working at a deeper level than I can match. She had insider knowledge of specific database schemas, API endpoints, internal service meshes. But I know how these companies are built from the outside, and I can infer a great deal from public documentation, job postings, conference presentations, and the architectural decisions that companies at this scale are forced to make."

Andrei studied him. "Job postings?"

"Companies hiring for specific technical roles reveal their internal architecture. If a company posts a job for a 'Senior Engineer, Kafka Pipeline Infrastructure,' I now know they're using Apache Kafka for their event streaming layer. If they post for 'Security Engineer, Zero Trust Network,' I know their internal network architecture is moving toward zero-trust principles. You'd be amazed how much classified infrastructure leaks through HR departments."

For the first time, something that might have been amusement crossed Andrei's face. "You're not what I expected."

"What did you expect?"

"An academic who would lecture me about ethics while I tried to work."

"I reserve the lectures for after you've shown me the exploits. Consider it professional courtesy."

Andrei almost smiled. It was a near thing, the muscles around his mouth twitching in a direction that suggested the machinery existed but was rarely used.

"Priya's files," he said. "Do you have them?"

"I have them. The checksums were verified before I left. I'll show you her working files."

"Then we have forty-eight hours of my time. We'll start with her assumptions. I'll walk you through every exploit chain, and you'll tell me what map data I need to make them work."

He turned to the monitors. Six windows opened simultaneously, each displaying a different company's exploit chain in various stages of completion. The code was dense, elegant in places, brutal in others, the work of a mind that understood systems at the deepest possible level and had spent years learning exactly where they broke.

Adrian leaned forward, carefully, mindful of the pressure sensor, and began to work.

Outside, the first snow of November began to fall on the cobblestones of Rataskaevu Street, dusting the medieval rooftops in white and turning the Old Town into something from a fairy tale.

Inside, two men who had never met before were building the skeleton key that would open every locked door in the digital world.

Neither of them noticed the snow.
