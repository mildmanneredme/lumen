Chapter Fifty Six.

Raven was in a bathtub in an Arnhem hotel when the men came to kill her.

She had checked in forty minutes earlier under a Canadian passport that identified her as a marine biologist named Helen Park. The farmhouse was twelve kilometers away. Its isolation had simplified the kill; the hotel would let her wash, change, and leave with the morning traffic. That was the plan.

She had drawn the bath at 12:14 a.m. on Monday, after sweeping the room for surveillance devices, checking the window sightlines, and placing her suppressed Glock 19 in a waterproof bag between the tub and the wall. Paranoia was a professional qualification.

The water was 38.5 degrees Celsius. She had checked it with the small thermometer in her kit. Every variable controlled, every contingency mapped. That discipline had carried her through dozens of contracts across eleven countries. It had carried her into the farmhouse less than an hour ago. A single shot, no witnesses, the letter left where Hale wanted it.

Clean, she thought, sliding deeper into the water. Professional. Another solved equation.

But the Hale job had felt different. Rushed. The council had accelerated the timeline after the zero-day attack hit their systems, and Raven didn't like acceleration. Acceleration meant pressure, and pressure introduced variables, and variables were the enemy of craft.

She had received the Hale contract six hours before the kill. She'd used the interval to study the farmhouse, test the approach, and arrange an exit. Enough to complete the work. Less than she preferred. The speed of the order still bothered her.

She was thinking about this, cataloging the imperfections, filing them for future reference, when she heard the door.

Not the front door. The connecting door to the adjacent room, which the receptionist had said was unoccupied.

The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Raven was out of the tub before the door opened. No conscious decision. No ethical calculation. Fifteen years of training collapsing into muscle memory. Her hand found the Glock in its waterproof bag. Her feet found the bath mat. Water sheeted off her body onto the marble floor as she pressed herself against the wall beside the bathroom door, naked and dripping and holding a nine-millimeter pistol at eye level.

Two men. She heard them before she saw them: tactical boots on carpet, the careful tread of professionals trying to be silent and almost succeeding. Almost was an important word in Raven's industry. Almost was the distance between a closed casket and an open one.

She processed the situation in the time it took the first man to cross the suite's living area.

Two operators. Suppressed weapons; she could see the extended barrel profile through the crack in the bathroom door. Tactical formation. Professional but not elite. Private military, probably. Hired fast.

Who sent them? The answer arrived instantly, cold and crystalline.

The council.

Harrison Polk, or whoever was handling logistics now that the oligarchs' world was on fire. She was a loose end. She had killed the leader of the movement currently dismantling their digital empires, and the men who had hired her had decided that the most efficient way to ensure silence was to ensure she stopped breathing.

Something moved inside Raven's chest, something she hadn't felt in years. Not fear. She knew fear; it was a manageable chemical reaction. This was different. This was the feeling of a contract being broken. Of specifications being violated. Of the orderly universe she had spent her adult life constructing cracking like ice on a spring lake.

They were supposed to pay the second half, she thought, and the banality of the grievance almost made her laugh.

The first man reached the bathroom door. She could see his shadow on the marble tiles, the elongated barrel of his suppressed weapon leading his body like a divining rod.

Raven shot him through the door. He fell against it, forcing it inward. Her left hand was on the frame. Two fingers caught between wood and jamb before she could pull them clear.

The second man fired through the bathroom wall. Raven dropped, striking the left side of her chest against the hard edge of the tub. Pain burst along her ribs. She kicked the door away from her trapped hand, crouched low, and put two rounds into his center mass. He hit the carpet.

The suppressed shots had been quiet enough to delay recognition, but someone in the next room had struck the wall. She had seconds, not a safe interval, before a call reached reception.

Clothes. Passport. Cash. Go bag. Her left hand was swelling. She bound the damaged fingers against a short strip of stiff plastic from her kit, then checked the first man's pockets. His phone was locked with a fingerprint. His thumb opened it. She photographed the most recent messages and left the device on his chest.

Forty-five seconds. She checked the connecting room. Empty. Whoever had arranged the attack had put the men beside her. The master keycard on the carpet made the route plain.

Professionals, she thought. But not my caliber.

She left through the fire stairs. Seventh floor to ground in under a minute, each landing checked with the Glock leading, each corner taken wide. The stairwell smelled of concrete and industrial cleaning solvent. Her wet hair dripped onto the back of her shirt.

She reached the Arnhem street. The night was cold. Raven walked away from the hotel at a steady pace, searching for the nearest turn that would take her out of its sightlines.

Her left side burned. She looked down.

A fragment from the bathroom wall had cut her left flank. Blood spread beneath her shirt. The ribs above it hurt with each breath; her splinted fingers were beginning to throb. She could move. That was the only assessment she had time to make.

Specifications violated, she thought again, and this time the dark humor didn't surface.

She needed stitches. She needed antibiotics. She needed a clean room and twelve hours without pursuit.

She had none of these things.

What she had was a list of names, the council members who had hired her and then sent men to kill her, and a feeling she hadn't experienced since childhood. Something older than anger, older than betrayal. Something that lived in the brainstem, beneath the prefrontal cortex where reason and calculation and all of Raven's carefully maintained control systems operated.

The wolf doesn't negotiate.

Raven walked through Arnhem with blood soaking through her shirt. For fifteen years she had been a precision instrument: aimed, fired, cleaned, stored. Now the hand that held her had tried to throw her away.

Something primal had woken up. Something that didn't work in specifications and tolerances. Something that hunted.

She had a camera full of text messages from the dead man's phone. She had the names of every council member. She had skills that those council members had been perfectly happy to deploy when they pointed in someone else's direction.

Now they pointed at the people who had tried to kill her.

She reached the rental car by a different entrance to the garage and drove toward Germany. The first service station with a locked bathroom would have to do for treatment.

Behind her, someone in the hotel corridor began knocking on the door.
