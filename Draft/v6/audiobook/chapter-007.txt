Chapter Seven.

The call came at 6:47 a.m., early enough to be alarming and late enough that Adrian was already awake, standing at his kitchen counter with a French press and the nagging sense that the world had shifted overnight in a direction he couldn't yet identify.

"He's dead." Morrow's voice carried none of its usual measured control. It was flat. Compressed. "Yoon. Dead in his cell this morning. Cardiac arrest, they're saying."

Adrian set the French press down with more care than necessary, as though the steadiness of his hands could compensate for the unsteadiness of everything else. "He was twenty-six years old."

"Yeah."

"Twenty-six-year-old men don't have cardiac arrests."

"Sometimes they do. Undiagnosed conditions. That's the working explanation. The autopsy hasn't happened yet."

"You don't sound like you think it's consistent."

A long exhale. Morrow was somewhere with traffic noise — his car, probably, on the way to the scene or coming back from it. "I've been a detective for fifteen years, Professor. I've seen people die in custody. Drug interactions, suicide, medical emergencies — it happens. It's terrible and it happens. But I've never seen someone die of cardiac arrest the night after his initial hearing in a case involving one of the largest tech companies in the world, at the exact moment when the entire intelligence community is trying to figure out who else was involved."

"That's a lot of coincidence."

"That's what I'm saying."

Adrian sat down at his kitchen table, still covered with the case materials from Wednesday's review — the network diagrams, the access logs, the documents describing ORACLE and SHEPHERD and COMPASS. Systems designed to predict and manipulate human behavior at scale. Systems that someone had sent a twenty-six-year-old software engineer to map, and that someone else had apparently decided were worth killing to protect.

"Detective, who had access to him?"

"That's the thing. In isolation? Guards, the facility nurse, his public defender, his lawyer. The hearing was yesterday — went fine, nothing unusual. Lawyer gave him an earpiece afterward, said it was for secure legal communication. Standard practice, his team says. Yoon went back to his cell. By morning he was dead."

"And the medical examiner?"

"Not there yet. The nurse found no obvious injury. The guards logged no incident. We're waiting on the examination and toxicology. Right now, all I can tell you is that a twenty-six-year-old man just — stopped."

The chill arrived in full. Not the kind produced by temperature but by understanding. The kind that settled in his bones and along his spine. The distance between a theoretical threat and a real one had collapsed overnight from the width of an academic paper to the width of a holding cell wall.

"This wasn't an accident," Adrian said.

"No."

"And it wasn't a lone actor. Someone with resources, intelligence, and access to professional — Detective, this is a professional assassination. In a county jail. Under federal jurisdiction."

"I know what it is."

"Then you know what it means. Daniel Yoon wasn't just a spy. He was a witness. He knew something — about the network he was part of, about who sent him, about what they're planning — and someone with the power to reach into a federal holding facility and kill him decided that whatever he knew couldn't be allowed to come out."

Morrow was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had regained some of its composure, but beneath it Adrian could hear a man recalibrating his understanding of the size of the thing he'd stepped into.

"I need you to come in today. To my office. Bring the materials."

"Why?"

"Because when I brought you in on Tuesday I thought I was working a corporate espionage case. Now I'm working a homicide inside my own jurisdiction's jail. And the only person I trust to tell me what the hell is actually going on is a professor who specializes in the systems that someone is apparently willing to kill for."

Adrian looked at the documents on his table. At his name, listed as a citation in the seized materials of a dead man. At the phrase intervention architecture and the clinical language of control.

He thought about Daniel Yoon. Twenty-six. A software engineer. Someone who had been recruited by a network sophisticated enough to penetrate one of the most secure technology companies in the world. Someone who had sat in a cell, silent, refusing to speak to anyone, more afraid of what was behind him than what was in front of him.

He'd been right to be afraid.

"I'll be there by ten," Adrian said.

He hung up and sat in his kitchen as the morning light crept across the table, illuminating the documents one by one. The case had changed. It had been abstract before — intellectually fascinating, professionally relevant, a puzzle with the academic distance that allowed him to examine it without being inside it. Now a man was dead. A young man, barely older than Adrian's graduate students, killed in a cell he couldn't leave by a method designed to be invisible.

Adrian opened the university directory and searched Yoon's name. An old student page remained, the photograph smaller than a postage stamp. No smile. Just a young man standing against a wall, trying to look serious for a camera.

He closed it before he could begin searching for a family. He had no right to call them, nothing to offer except questions they were already being asked by people with badges. He put the case materials in order, then took them out of order because the first arrangement hid the routes between the systems.

The work steadied him. The silence on Morrow's call did not.

If the people behind him could reach into a federal jail and kill him, what could they do to someone who wasn't behind bars?

What could they do to someone sitting at a kitchen table in Berkeley, reading documents he was never supposed to see?

Adrian gathered the materials, put them in his briefcase, and locked the front door behind him as he left. The Berkeley morning was bright and mild, the Bay Area light making everything look like a postcard — the hillside, the campus in the distance, the jogger passing on the sidewalk. A normal Saturday. A beautiful day.

He got in his car and drove toward a police station in San Francisco, carrying the annotated blueprints of a dead man's reconnaissance mission, and the growing certainty that he was no longer on the sidelines of anything.
