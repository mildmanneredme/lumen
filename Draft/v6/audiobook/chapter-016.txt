Chapter Sixteen.

The conference room on the sixty-third floor of Strutt Networks' Menlo Park headquarters had no windows, which was a design choice rather than an oversight. Elias Strutt believed that windows were distractions — apertures through which the external world leaked into the controlled environment of thought. His conference rooms were sealed boxes of recycled air and recessed lighting, optimized for the transmission and processing of information without the noise of weather, scenery, or the passage of time. Clocks were also absent. Time, in Strutt's workspace, was measured by the completion of tasks, not the rotation of the earth.

The seven men gathered around the table had been summoned with less than forty-eight hours' notice, which communicated urgency more effectively than any agenda item could. Harrison Polk sat at the head, where he always sat, because Harrison Polk understood that in a room full of men who controlled empires, the person who controlled the meeting controlled more than any of them.

"The situation has evolved," Polk began. "Elias, bring them up to speed."

Strutt activated a display embedded in the table's surface — a seamless piece of technology that made the conference table function as a screen, because in Strutt's world every surface was a potential interface. The display showed a map: nodes and connections, the visual language of network analysis, rendered in the cold blue palette that Strutt's designers used for everything because he found warm colors "emotionally manipulative."

"Since our last meeting, my team has expanded the surveillance net. We've correlated the probing activity across all our systems — mine, Arthur's cloud infrastructure, Magnus's platforms, Clive's media networks — and confirmed that the same methodology is being used in every case. Custom-built reconnaissance. No two probes are identical, but the underlying approach is consistent: map the internal architecture, identify data stores, locate financial and communication records, and withdraw without triggering security responses."

"How long has this been going on?" Laine asked.

"The earliest activity we can confirm dates to fourteen months ago. But given the sophistication of the methodology, the actual timeline is likely longer. We may have been penetrated for years without detection."

The room absorbed this. Fourteen months of invisible reconnaissance against the most powerful technology companies in the Western world. The implications settled like sediment in still water.

"The cell structure is what concerns me," Strutt continued. "The chatter we've been monitoring is decentralized in a way I haven't seen before. They use a layered encryption scheme that changes protocols every seventy-two hours. Each communication node, each cell, appears to operate independently. No central server. No hub. If you take one cell down, the network routes around it. It's built like—"

"Like the internet," Venn said quietly.

"Like a better internet," Strutt corrected, and the faint edge of professional admiration in his voice was, for Elias Strutt, the equivalent of a standing ovation. "Whoever designed this network's architecture is exceptionally good. Better than my team, and my team is the best in the industry."

"So we can't trace them," Cole said flatly.

"We can't trace the network. But we can trace individuals. The arrest of Daniel Yoon gave us a starting point. His devices were seized before he could wipe them. The encryption was formidable, but we've been working on it — and we've made progress."

He clicked to a new display. A web of connections, thinner and less complete than the network's actual structure but growing.

"We've identified patterns of communication that suggest the network has a hierarchical component beneath the decentralized surface. There's someone, or something, coordinating the cells. Distributing instructions. Managing information flow. The cells think they're autonomous, but they're being orchestrated."

"By who?" Barrington demanded.

"By what might be a more productive question," Strutt said. "The coordination patterns don't match human management. The speed, the consistency, the lack of human error — it's algorithmic. I believe they're using an AI system to manage the network."

The silence that followed had a different quality from the one that preceded it. This was not the silence of absorption but of recalibration. An AI managing an underground resistance network. The idea was not science fiction — every person in the room understood the capabilities of large language models and autonomous agents, because every person in the room built and deployed them. But the idea that such technology was being used against them, by an adversary operating outside any institutional framework, was a new kind of threat.

"I have a proposal," Strutt said. "With the combined data from all our systems — every probe we've detected, every communication fragment, every behavioral pattern — I can build a comprehensive surveillance apparatus specifically designed to identify and track this network. Full digital monitoring. Every email, every message, every encrypted communication that touches any of our infrastructure. I'll cross-reference against social media activity on all our platforms, financial transactions through our payment systems, and location data from mobile devices operating on networks we control."

"That's half the Western world's digital activity," Laine said.

"Yes."

"Elias, the legal exposure—"

"Is manageable. My platforms' terms of service authorize data processing for security purposes. Arthur's cloud services agreements include similar provisions. Magnus's platform can flag accounts for internal review without external notification. The data never leaves our systems. No government involvement, no warrants, no disclosure."

"You're describing a private surveillance state," Laine said, and to his credit, he sounded genuinely uncomfortable.

"I'm describing the tools necessary to identify an organized threat to every company at this table. Geoffrey, these people are building the capability to publish everything we've ever done. Every email. Every financial transaction. Every communication with every legislator. If they succeed—"

"I understand the stakes."

"Then you understand why I'm proposing this."

Polk looked around the table. This was the moment he'd been managing toward — the point where the threat was clear enough to override individual caution. Six men who had spent their careers building walls around their private activities were being asked to look behind everyone else's walls in order to defend their own.

"Let's vote," Polk said.

"I'm in favor," Strutt said. Obviously.

"In favor," Venn said. His cloud infrastructure was the largest target. He had the most to lose and the most to contribute.

"Do it," Cole said. "And when you find them, I want to know. Personally."

Barrington nodded slowly. "I'll want narrative control over any public-facing response. If this leaks — the surveillance, our coordination — I need to shape the story."

"Agreed," Polk said.

"Geoffrey?"

Laine looked at the display. At the network map. At the blue nodes and cold connections representing an adversary that was mapping the infrastructure of his philanthropic empire — the financial flows, the political connections, the carefully cultivated relationships between his foundation and every government that mattered. An adversary that would, if successful, show the world that the man who had positioned himself as humanity's benefactor was also one of the architects of its democratic captivity.

"In favor," he said. "With reservations."

"Noted. Kai?"

Kai Nakamura had been watching the display with the attentive focus of someone absorbing information. He looked up at Polk's prompt, as though emerging from thought, and nodded.

"In favor. But I'd suggest we focus the surveillance on digital communications rather than physical infrastructure."

A small statement. Almost a footnote. Strutt glanced at him.

"Why?"

"Efficiency. If they're using an AI to coordinate, the AI operates digitally. That's where the command and control lives. Physical infrastructure — hardware, servers, devices — would require a different kind of investigation, and I think Elias's resources are better spent on the digital domain where he has the greatest advantage."

It was reasonable. It was logical. A junior member's suggestion — demonstrating engagement without challenging the room's authority.

Strutt considered it. "Fair point. The digital layer is where I can move fastest. Physical infrastructure investigations take time and require coordination with law enforcement, which introduces variables I'd rather avoid."

"Then we're agreed," Polk said. "Elias leads the digital surveillance effort. Full cooperation from all parties. Arthur provides infrastructure support. Magnus flags accounts. Clive manages the narrative if needed. Geoffrey provides institutional cover."

"And the AI?" Venn asked. "If they have an AI managing the network, we should be looking for it specifically. An autonomous system that sophisticated leaves footprints. Processing patterns. Data access signatures. If we find the AI, we find the network."

"Agreed," Strutt said. "That's priority one."

The meeting adjourned. The display went dark. The windowless room returned to its default state: a sealed box of recycled air in which seven men had just authorized the most comprehensive private surveillance operation in history, directed against an enemy they couldn't see, using the same data collection capabilities that the enemy was apparently planning to expose.

Kai Nakamura was the last to leave the room.

He stood for a moment beside the dark table. On its surface, his reflection had no expression. The meeting had given him a task, a set of access permissions, and more information than he wished the others had found. He touched the edge of his phone, then took his hand away.

In the elevator, he composed a message and deleted it before the doors opened. His driver was waiting outside. Kai got into the car and opened NovaTok's analytics dashboard, the harmless occupation of a CEO returning to work.

He did not look at the figures. He watched the California sun move across the opposite window and waited until his breathing was steady enough that the driver would hear nothing unusual when he spoke.

"Home," he said.

The word surprised him. He had meetings until midnight.

At the next intersection, he corrected himself and gave the office address.
