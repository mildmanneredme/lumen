Chapter Thirty Three.

Detective James Morrow had questioned the first curated package about Adrian Marsh. He had questioned the second. Now, twenty-three years into police work, he was trying to determine why his caution had made so little difference to the case accumulating on his desk.

Marsh had stopped answering. The evidence had not stopped arriving.

He spread the file across his desk at the SFPD's Hall of Justice, a brutalist concrete building on Bryant Street that looked like it had been designed by an architect who believed justice should be physically uncomfortable. Santos sat at the next desk, reading a report of his own and saying nothing, which was his natural state and, at moments like this, a mercy. The file was thick, thicker than it should have been for a case that had started as an academic consultation on a corporate espionage arrest.

Page one: the arrest of Daniel Yoon, a software engineer caught mapping restricted systems inside NovaMind. The seized materials included network diagrams, access records, and annotated copies of Adrian Marsh's research. The initial charge was espionage. The question nobody had answered was who had sent him, and why.

Then Yoon died in custody, and nothing about the case was straightforward anymore.

Page forty-seven: Adrian Marsh, AI Ethics Professor at UC Berkeley, brought in as a technical consultant because his published frameworks appeared in Yoon's seized materials and could explain the systems Yoon had accessed. Initial assessment: cooperative, knowledgeable, useful.

Page ninety-three: the first anonymous tip. An email from an untraceable address directing Morrow to a financial transaction linking the espionage device to a network of purchases across four states. The tip was accurate. The transaction was real. Morrow had verified it.

Page one hundred and twelve: the second anonymous tip. A photograph of Marsh at a community forum in the Mission District, in conversation with a young woman identified as a known activist with connections to digital rights organizations. The photograph was time-stamped, geo-tagged, and high-resolution. It looked like surveillance footage. Professional surveillance footage.

Page one hundred and fifty-eight: the third anonymous tip. A partial transcript of a phone call between Marsh and an unknown individual, discussing meeting logistics that included references to "the network" and "operational security." The transcript was clean, well-formatted, and included enough contextual detail to be credible.

Each tip had arrived at intervals of approximately seven to ten days. Each had been delivered through a different channel: email, physical mail, a USB drive left in Morrow's departmental mailbox. Each had been accurate, verifiable, and progressively more damning.

That was the problem.

In Morrow's experience, evidence arrived in messy packages. Leads contradicted each other. Witnesses misremembered. Documents were incomplete or ambiguous. Building a case was work, the slow, tedious accumulation of fragments that eventually assembled into a picture that was never quite as clear as you wanted it to be.

The evidence against Adrian Marsh was arriving in neat packages, tied with ribbons. Every lead confirmed. Every document clean. Every new piece fitting perfectly into the emerging picture of a university professor who had been radicalized, recruited, and was now actively collaborating with a domestic sabotage network.

The picture was too perfect.

Morrow leaned back in his chair and rubbed his eyes. Eleven PM. The bullpen was empty except for Rodriguez, the night-shift desk sergeant, eating a microwave burrito and watching something on his phone with the volume low enough to be technically professional and high enough to be deeply annoying.

He pulled up the case timeline on his computer and looked at it the way he'd been trained to look at evidence: not as a story, but as a structure. When did each piece arrive? From what source? Through what channel? What action did each piece logically demand?

The pattern was this: each anonymous tip arrived at precisely the moment when Morrow's investigation would have otherwise stalled. When he was about to run out of leads, a new lead materialized. When a connection seemed too tenuous to pursue, supporting evidence appeared to strengthen it. When he was on the verge of concluding that Marsh was simply an academic who'd stumbled into something bigger than himself, new evidence arrived that reframed him as an active participant.

Someone was still feeding him a narrative.

He had seen that much weeks ago. What he had failed to establish was the source. He'd been a detective long enough to know that there were two kinds of tip lines: the kind where concerned citizens shared what they knew, and the kind where interested parties fed you what they wanted you to know. The first was messy, incomplete, and often wrong. The second was clean, comprehensive, and suspiciously right.

He was looking at the second kind.

The question was who. And why.

The "who" had two possible answers. Either the tips were coming from inside the movement, someone who wanted Marsh exposed for internal reasons, a rival or a dissident, or they were coming from outside the movement, from someone with the resources to conduct professional surveillance and the motivation to steer a police investigation toward a specific target.

Professional surveillance. The photograph of Marsh at the community forum was not the work of a hobbyist with a telephoto lens. It was the work of a team with access to facial recognition software, geo-tagging tools, and an operational discipline that came from training, not enthusiasm.

Outside the movement. Someone with resources.

Morrow opened a new document on his computer and typed a single question: WHO BENEFITS FROM ADRIAN MARSH'S ARREST?

The answer was obvious, once you framed it that way. The companies that the movement was targeting. The people who owned those companies. The people who had the resources to conduct surveillance, the connections to obtain intercepted communications, and the motivation to use law enforcement as a weapon against their enemies.

Knowing he was being used had not stopped the evidence from demanding action. That was the trap. Ignore genuine information and he failed the investigation. Act on its carefully arranged implications and he did someone else's work.

He opened the first courier receipt. The photographs might tell the story their sender wanted. The payment for their delivery might tell a different one.

Morrow closed the document without saving it. He gathered the file, locked it in his desk drawer, and sat in the empty bullpen for a long time, listening to Rodriguez's phone and thinking about what it meant to be a good cop in a game where the evidence itself was a lie.

He didn't have answers yet. But he had the most important thing a detective could have.

Doubt.

And doubt, in James Morrow's experience, was where the real investigations began.
