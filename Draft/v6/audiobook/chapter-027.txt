Chapter Twenty Seven.

On Friday night, two days after the news of Priya's death reached the inner circle, Nadia Osei sat beside the wall her editors had called the work of a conspiracy theorist.

The wall behind her desk, the one visitors saw first when they walked through the door, the one that had caused her landlord to suggest, delicately, that she might want to "consider the aesthetic implications," was covered floor to ceiling in paper. Photographs, printouts, sticky notes, newspaper clippings, and hand-drawn diagrams connected by colored string in a web so dense it looked like the nervous system of a very anxious creature.

Red string for money flows. Blue for corporate connections. Green for political relationships. Yellow for media ownership. And a single strand of black thread that connected a cluster of nodes in the upper left corner labeled, in Nadia's precise capitals, THE COUNCIL.

Four years to build this wall. Four years of Freedom of Information requests that came back heavily redacted. Four years of source cultivation, the patient, tedious work of building relationships with people inside the institutions she was investigating, earning their trust one coffee meeting at a time, never pushing too hard, never asking for anything they weren't ready to give. Four years of cross-referencing public records, SEC filings, FEC disclosures, corporate board appointments, and the revolving door between lobbying firms and regulatory agencies.

Four years during which her editors had progressively distanced themselves from her work, her colleagues had stopped returning her calls, and the label "conspiracy theorist" had attached itself to her name with the permanence of a brand.

The label didn't bother Nadia. What bothered her was that the label worked. The moment someone was designated a conspiracy theorist, everything they said, no matter how well-sourced, no matter how meticulously documented, was pre-discredited. It was the most elegant suppression mechanism she'd encountered: you didn't need to disprove the evidence. You just needed to discredit the person presenting it.

But tonight, sitting cross-legged on her floor surrounded by printouts and a half-eaten bowl of jollof rice, Nadia Osei was not thinking about labels. She was thinking about a pattern she'd found that morning in the FEC filings for the last three election cycles, and the pattern was making her skin crawl.

The numbers were these: seven PACs, all established within six months of each other, all funded through five oh one, c four organizations that were themselves funded through a cascade of shell companies registered in Delaware, Wyoming, and the Cayman Islands. Standard dark money architecture: opaque by design, legal by the thinnest of margins, and deliberately structured to make tracing the source of funds a Sisyphean exercise.

Except Nadia was very good at Sisyphean exercises.

She'd traced the shells backward through layers of corporate filings until she hit what she privately called the Convergence Point, the layer where the separate streams merged into a single source. In this case, the source was a consulting firm in Arlington, Virginia, called Meridian Policy Group. Meridian had no public website, no social media presence, and no employees listed in any searchable database. But it had one very interesting detail: a suite of offices in the same building as Harrison Polk's lobbying firm.

Not adjacent to Polk's firm. In the same building. On the same floor. Suite 1404 and Suite 1408, separated by a shared conference room that appeared on the building's floor plan but not on either company's public-facing materials.

Nadia had photographed the floor plan from a building management filing during a public records request for fire safety inspections. The building manager had been confused by her interest. "Nobody ever asks about floor plans," he'd said. "You'd be amazed what's in floor plans," she'd replied.

The connection between Meridian and Polk's firm didn't prove anything by itself. But when she combined it with the PAC funding patterns, the corporate board overlaps, and the legislative outcomes that the PAC money was designed to influence, the picture was damning. Seven PACs had collectively spent four hundred and twelve million dollars across three election cycles, targeting specific congressional races in states with pending legislation on data privacy, antitrust enforcement, and algorithmic transparency. Every race the PACs invested in had been won by the candidate who opposed regulation. Every single one.

And the money originated from companies controlled by people she'd identified on her wall, the cluster she'd labeled THE COUNCIL.

Nadia picked up her phone. She'd been debating this call for three weeks, ever since she'd watched Adrian Marsh walk out of a community center in the Mission District looking like a man who'd just been handed a live grenade. She'd been at the forum too, not because she cared about the panel discussion, but because her sources told her the movement had people there. She'd gone to observe. What she'd observed was a tenured professor having his worldview rearranged in real time by a twenty-three-year-old activist who probably didn't know she was being used as bait.

Since then, Nadia had watched Adrian from a careful distance. She'd seen him meet with Morrow. She'd seen him visit locations that her own investigation had flagged as potential movement safe houses. She'd tracked the trajectory, an academic being drawn into something much larger than he understood, and recognized it because she'd been on a parallel trajectory for years.

The difference was that Nadia had been laughed out of the room. Adrian was being invited in.

She dialed the number she'd gotten from a mutual contact at Berkeley, a professor in the computer science department who owed Nadia a favor for not publishing a story about his department's contract with a surveillance technology company.

Adrian picked up on the fourth ring.

"Professor Marsh?"

"Who is this?" His voice was tight. Guarded. The voice of a man who'd recently learned to distrust phone calls.

"Nadia Osei. We met at the cafe, a few weeks ago. You were uncomfortable with my reputation and impressed by my evidence."

A pause. "I remember."

"I need to meet with you. In person. Not a cafe this time, somewhere private. I have information you need, and I believe you have information I need."

"Ms. Osei—"

"Nadia."

"Nadia. I'm not in a position to—"

"You're not in a position to ignore this either. I know about Priya Chandrasekaran."

The silence on the line was absolute.

"I know she's dead," Nadia continued. "I know it wasn't a heart attack. And I know that the same people who killed her are connected to a funding network I've been mapping for four years, a network that leads directly to a group of individuals coordinating to suppress any challenge to their control over democratic processes."

"How do you know about Priya?"

"Because I've been doing this work while you were writing papers about it. I have sources inside the coroner's office, inside the Palo Alto Police Department, and inside two of the companies your friends are targeting. I don't have the full picture. Nobody does. But I have pieces you don't have. And based on what I've been observing, you have pieces I don't have."

Another pause. Longer this time. She could hear him thinking.

"Where?" he said.

"Dolores Park. South end, near the J-Church stop. Tomorrow, two PM. Come alone. And Adrian?"

"Yes?"

"Bring your questions. I have a wall full of answers that nobody's been willing to listen to for four years. I'm very ready to be listened to."

She hung up and looked at her wall. The web of connections stared back: red, blue, green, yellow, and a single black thread leading to a cluster of names that most people would call paranoid fantasy.

Nadia picked up a sticky note and a marker. She wrote ADRIAN MARSH and stuck it to the wall, in the narrow space between the movement's network and the council's.

He was in the gap. The space between the two sides.

The question was which way he'd fall.

At two the following afternoon, Adrian found her at the south end of Dolores Park. He looked tired. For once he didn't attempt a joke before sitting beside her.

She gave him a paper folder. The PAC records, the shell companies, the shared offices in Arlington. "Polk's money routes," she said. "Read them beside whatever you've been shown. If the stories disagree, I want to know."

"I can do that."

"And Priya?"

He looked out across the park. "I was meant to work with her."

Nadia waited. He didn't say more. His hands had tightened around the folder.

"I'm leaving the country," he said. "Tomorrow. I can't give you names or locations."

"I didn't ask for them. I need to know whether there's going to be evidence behind the promises."

"That's what I'm going to find out."

She took a card from her pocket. A postal address in Oakland, care of someone whose name meant nothing to him. "Send word there if you can't call. If the evidence comes, I'll need enough notice to have people ready to read it."

"You'd publish it?"

"I'd verify it. Then I'd publish what it proves. That's the arrangement."

Adrian slipped the card inside the folder. They sat for another minute, watching a child chase a ball down the slope. When he stood, Nadia let him go without another question.
