Chapter Eleven.

The phone was not his phone.

This was the first thing Adrian registered upon waking at 3:12 a.m. to a sound he didn't recognize — a chime, soft but persistent, repeating at ten-second intervals with an algorithmic regularity that marked it as something designed rather than accidental. His own phone sat on the nightstand where he always left it, dark and silent. The sound was coming from his jacket.

He got out of bed with the caution of a man who had recently learned that people in his orbit were being killed, crossed the bedroom, and retrieved his jacket from the back of the desk chair where he'd draped it after returning from the coffee shop. The inner pocket, the one he never used because it was slightly too narrow for his phone and slightly too deep for his wallet, contained a device he had never seen before.

It was a phone. Or rather, it was a phone-shaped object: a modified handset, older model, no branding, with a screen that displayed a single notification.

You're looking at the wrong building.

Adrian stared at the screen. The text was white on black, no sender information, no app icon, no interface elements that would identify the messaging platform. Just the words, stark and unadorned, and below them a link.

He set the device on his desk and stepped back. The existence of this phone in his jacket meant that someone had placed it there. Someone who had been close enough to touch him. Someone who knew which jacket he wore, which pocket he didn't use, and where he had been today.

The coffee shop. Artis. The meeting with Nadia.

His mind ran the timeline. He'd hung his jacket on the back of his chair. He'd been focused on Nadia's laptop, on the diagram, on the conversation. The cafe had been busy — the normal Berkeley crowd of students, freelancers, and the occasional elderly regular reading a physical newspaper with the defiant dignity of someone who refused to surrender to the digital age. Any of them could have passed his table. Any of them could have slipped a device into an unattended jacket with the dexterity of a pickpocket running in reverse.

He picked up the phone again. You're looking at the wrong building.

The link below the text was a dot onion address — a Tor hidden service URL, one that existed only on the encrypted dark web and could not be accessed through a normal browser. Adrian knew what Tor was. He'd lectured about it. He'd written about the tension between privacy infrastructure and criminal exploitation. He had never, in his personal life, used it.

He considered his options. The responsible thing was to call Morrow. Turn over the device. Let the police forensics team trace its origins, analyze the message, follow the link in a controlled environment. This was, by any reasonable assessment, the correct course of action.

If you call Morrow now, you lose the source before you know what it has sent. He could feel the argument assembling itself, his own voice making the case against his own judgment. Look at the evidence first. Then decide.

That's not the same thing and you know it.

Do I? Do I really?

Adrian went to his study. He had a laptop there that he used for personal projects, not connected to the university network, not synced to any cloud service, the closest thing he owned to a clean machine. He booted it up, installed a Tor browser from its official source — the process took seven minutes, during which he made coffee, because he was a man who coped with existential uncertainty through caffeine — and entered the dot onion address.

The page that loaded was minimal. No graphics, no branding, no formatting beyond plain text. It contained a single document: a set of network access logs from NovaMind Technologies, timestamped over a period of three months, showing authenticated access to the ORACLE, SHEPHERD, and COMPASS systems from IP addresses that resolved to a building in Washington, D.C.

Not NovaMind's offices. Not a government agency.

A lobbying firm. Specifically, the lobbying firm of Harrison Polk.

Adrian sat back. The coffee was too hot to drink but he drank it anyway, barely registering the burn.

NovaMind's most sensitive AI systems — the behavioral prediction engine, the influence manipulation platform, the political modeling tool — were being accessed remotely by a lobbying firm. Not a client relationship in any standard sense. The access logs showed direct connections to the systems' administrative interfaces, not the output dashboards that a paying customer would see, but the configuration layer. The layer where you decided what the system predicted and who it influenced.

Harrison Polk's firm wasn't using NovaMind's tools. They were steering them.

This was the thing Adrian had theorized about for years. The convergence point. The place where technology companies' capabilities intersected with political operatives' intentions to create something that neither could achieve alone: the capacity to model, predict, and manipulate political outcomes at scale, directed not by algorithms or engineers but by the people who understood which outcomes to target. The bridge between Silicon Valley's power and Washington's purpose.

And it was operating through NovaMind's internal systems, invisible to regulators, invisible to the public, invisible to anyone who didn't have access to the specific logs that had just been delivered to his desk by a phone that appeared in his jacket like a message from the underworld.

You're looking at the wrong building.

He'd been focused on NovaMind. On the company. On the technology. On Daniel Yoon's reconnaissance and what it revealed about the systems themselves. But the anonymous tip was redirecting his attention. The building didn't matter. The company didn't matter. What mattered was who was using the company — and for what.

Adrian looked at the phone. A device he hadn't purchased, running software he hadn't installed, delivering information that shouldn't exist, from a source he couldn't identify. Every alarm in his rational mind was sounding. This was manipulation. This was someone with an agenda, guiding him toward conclusions they wanted him to reach. The very definition of the information asymmetry he'd spent his career warning about.

But the data was real. The access logs were genuine — he could verify the format, the timestamps, the technical signatures against the case materials Morrow had shared. And they showed something that the police didn't know, that NovaMind would never voluntarily disclose, and that no one in any position of authority was looking for.

The question wasn't whether to trust the source. The question was whether to follow the evidence.

Adrian Marsh had spent twenty years in a profession built on the principle that evidence leads to truth. He had also spent enough time in the real world to know that evidence could be manufactured, curated, and deployed to lead you exactly where someone else wanted you to go.

He saved the document to an encrypted drive, closed the Tor browser, and shut down the laptop. Then he sat in his study in the dark, holding a phone that didn't belong to him, and thought about who in the world had the capability to place a device in his jacket, access classified network logs from a major technology company, and deliver them through an encrypted channel that he, an AI ethics professor, could follow.

The list of entities with that capability was short. Intelligence agencies. Major technology companies. Organized criminal networks.

Or something else entirely. Something that didn't fit any of the existing categories because it hadn't existed long enough to earn one.

The phone chimed again. A new message.

The building is a symptom. The network is the disease. Keep looking.

Then the screen went dark, and the phone powered itself off, and Adrian Marsh sat in the silence of his Berkeley home at three in the morning, holding a dead device and a growing certainty that he was being guided, carefully, deliberately, and with precision, toward something that someone very much wanted him to find.

The question he couldn't answer was whether they wanted him to find it because it was true, or because finding it served their purpose.

He suspected, with the uncomfortable clarity that comes in the small hours, that the answer might be both.
