Chapter Seventy Nine.

four hours remaining

The relay phone rang at 8:17 a.m. Tomás's voice, filtered through two intermediaries, was clipped and precise: "All seven confirmed. Network measurements transmitted."

Adrian wrote down the Seattle latency numbers on the margin of Ólafur's timing diagram. One-point-seven milliseconds to the administrative subnet. Three-point-two to the external gateway. He fed them into the integration framework and watched the validation model check the real-world data against Ólafur's stage timeouts.

For a long, horrible moment, the screen was blank.

Then: green. The Seattle device integrated. The timing window held. The Heartbeat Protocol accepted the seventh and final node.

Seven green lights on a dead man's laptop. Seven devices, planted in seven of the most secure facilities in the western world, all counting down to the same heartbeat, all synchronized within a four-hundred-millisecond window that a twenty-two-year-old from Reykjavik had spent four months engineering.

He read the remaining Seattle values into the relay, one at a time. The operator at Frankfurt applied them to the staged template and read the completed manifest back. Adrian checked its fingerprint against his own file. They matched. The station held the final bundle; all that remained was authorization.

The trigger was armed.

Adrian sat back in his chair and felt the weight of it settle onto him, not metaphorical weight but physical, as though gravity had increased in the room. His shoulders ached. His eyes burned. His hands, steady through six hours over the code, were now beginning to tremble with the exhaustion that adrenaline had been holding at bay.

He checked the status board, a matrix he'd built on a separate laptop, tracking every component of Project Lumen.

The seven implants were planted and answering their brief diagnostic checks. Andrei's exploits were loaded. Sophie's parsers were ready to sort the incoming records, and the Scaffold had enough surviving nodes to carry them. Ólafur's timing layer held.

Adrian's laptop had no network connection. Once he confirmed the command, it would read an authorization code aloud to the relay operator on his phone. That operator would carry it to the Frankfurt broadcast station, where the code would be checked against the fingerprint of the staged bundle. The encrypted transmission would carry the device-specific settings and the authorization together. The implants would store them and activate at the common epoch. Three seconds past twelve-seventeen. They could not be made to start early from this room.

Everything depended on one keystroke.

Adrian looked at the keyboard. At his hands. At the cursor blinking on the command line, waiting.

Four hours remained. Adrian could authorize the release now, but until the scheduled heartbeat he could still discover a fault that required an abort. He would keep checking. The final command would wait until he had used every minute available to verify what he was about to release.

He stood up. Walked to the window. Pulled back the curtain an inch and looked out at Brussels waking up. Cars on the street below. A bakery opening. A man walking a dog. The ordinary machinery of a city beginning its day, oblivious to the fact that a rumpled academic in a rented apartment was about to try to change everything.

Morrow's folder sat on the desk beside the laptops. Adrian had read it twice. The detective's work was thorough, meticulous, damning. Financial connections between Polk's lobbying network and seventeen sitting senators. Communication records showing Venn's company providing surveillance data to political campaigns. Evidence that Cole's infrastructure had been configured to throttle and deprioritize traffic to independent news outlets during three election cycles. Documentation of Barrington's media empire coordinating narrative campaigns with the council's political strategy.

And a timeline of the assassin's contracting, dates, intermediaries, payment chains, that connected every one of Raven's kills back to the council's authorization.

Morrow's evidence would not open the breaches. In four hours, Project Lumen would release far more than one detective had compiled through this investigation. But his documents would offer something different: a record gathered outside the movement, with sources that investigators could question and chains of payment they could verify.

A good cop's honest work, confirming what a radical act of transparency would reveal.

Adrian let the curtain fall. Returned to the desk.

He picked up the relay phone one more time. Dialed the chain.

"Status check," he said. "All stations."

The responses came back over the next twenty minutes, filtered through Nadia's network of intermediaries:

Ines, from Berlin: "Scaffold is hot. Thirty-one hundred nodes confirmed active — the survivors of the original eleven thousand. The rest went dark when the AI was compromised. But thirty-one hundred is enough. Replication protocols verified. Say the word."

Andrei, from a location he wouldn't disclose: "Exploits are loaded and armed. All seven custom keys in the locks. Waiting for the turn."

Sophie, from Geneva: "Financial parsers online. Money trail visualization tools ready. The moment the data flows, the receipts go public."

Nadia, from London: "Media contacts primed. Twelve journalists across six countries ready to break the story simultaneously. Independent verification teams standing by."

Tomás, from a flight heading east: "Hardware is planted and active. All seven devices responding to diagnostic pings. The house is wired."

Five voices. Five confirmations. Five people who had given up their safety, their anonymity, their normal lives to reach this moment.

And three who weren't here. Priya, who had mapped the targets from the inside and paid with her life. Seb, who had started the movement and been cut down before its purpose was fulfilled. Ólafur, who had built the heartbeat and never heard it play.

Adrian looked at the blinking cursor. Four hours.

He reached for the Icelandic licorice Ólafur had packed alongside his laptops. Opened the bag. Ate one piece. It was salty and sweet and profoundly weird, entirely on-brand for a twenty-two-year-old Icelandic prodigy, and Adrian almost smiled.

Almost.

"Four hours," he said to no one. To Priya. To Seb. To Ólafur. To the empty room and the blinking cursor and the seven green lights on the status board.

"Four hours, and then we find out if the world is ready for the truth."

The cursor blinked. The heartbeat counted. And Adrian Marsh, AI ethics professor, former angel investor, reluctant revolutionary, sat alone with the most consequential decision of the twenty-first century and waited for the clock to run down.

Raven — three hours remaining.

Amsterdam. The wrong city.

Raven stood in a doorway on Reguliersdwarsstraat, watching a hawala broker's office across the canal, and knew she was close. Close enough that the architecture of the network was becoming visible, the shape of the thing emerging from the noise the way a face emerges from static if you stare long enough.

She had caught an early flight from Hamburg after the overnight train, then taken a car from Schiphol into Amsterdam. For nine hours, since Copenhagen, she had been tracing the chain ahead of her. The Nokia phone led to a courier. The courier led to a second dead drop, this one a locker at Amsterdam Centraal. The locker contained a handwritten note — no digital trace, no metadata, nothing her tools could parse — with a six-digit number that corresponded to a hawala transfer.

Hawala. Informal value transfer. Money moved through trust networks, not banks. No wire records. No SWIFT codes. No digital footprint. Raven had encountered hawala networks in her previous work, but always as peripheral channels, side payments to local assets. This was the first time she had seen one used as primary financial infrastructure for an operation of this scale.

The six-digit number led her to the broker's office. She had been watching it since her arrival. Three people had entered. Two had left. The transactions were small, deliberate, structured to avoid the reporting thresholds that would flag them in any monitoring system.

But one transaction had touched a monitored account. A single transfer, routed through a Somali corridor broker in Rotterdam, had briefly intersected with a European financial intelligence database that Raven still had access to through credentials she had not yet been locked out of. The intersection lasted eleven seconds — long enough for the monitoring system to log the transaction amount, the destination corridor, and a partial routing code.

The routing code pointed to Brussels. Or possibly Antwerp. The corridor was Belgian, the amount consistent with operational funding, and the timing aligned with the movement's forty-eight-hour window.

Brussels. The trigger man was in Brussels.

Raven left the doorway and walked along the canal, moving with the careful gait of a woman whose body was a catalog of healing injuries. Her phone — not a burner; her own phone, the last piece of her old infrastructure — showed a map of Belgian rail connections from Amsterdam. Two hours and fourteen minutes to Brussels Midi.

But Brussels was a city of a million people. Knowing the city was not enough. She needed an address. She needed the next link in the chain, and the chain was analog, built from handwritten notes and trusted couriers and hawala transfers that left no digital wake.

Her tools were failing. The CCTV scraping, the cell tower triangulation, the financial database queries — all of them were designed for a world that ran on digital infrastructure. The journalist's network ran on trust. On human memory. On the deliberate, infuriating absence of anything Raven could trace from a laptop.

She was the most dangerous digital hunter in the western hemisphere, and she was being defeated by handwritten notes and Nokia phones.

Three hours until the window closed. She boarded the next train to Brussels.
