Chapter Eight.

Nadia Osei had been right about everything for six years, and it had cost her almost all of her once seemingly normal life.

She sat in her apartment in Oakland — a one-bedroom above a Vietnamese bakery on International Boulevard, chosen for its rent and its proximity to the BART station — rereading the San Francisco Chronicle's coverage of Daniel Yoon's death three days after it happened with the focused intensity of a hunter who has spotted a track the rest of the party missed. Her apartment smelled of pho from the kitchen below and coffee from the mug she'd refilled four times since waking at five. The walls were covered in what she privately called her cartography and what her former editor at Reuters had called, in the termination meeting that ended her career in mainstream journalism, "evidence of an unhealthy fixation."

The cartography was a web of printed articles, photographs, financial documents, corporate filings, and connecting threads — actual threads, red cotton from a spool she'd bought at a craft store in a moment of self-aware absurdity — that mapped the relationships between seven of the most powerful people in the Western world. It had taken her four years to build. It covered three walls. It was, by any reasonable measure, the most comprehensive visual representation of oligarchic power concentration in modern democracy that any journalist had ever assembled.

It had also gotten her fired, blacklisted, and labeled a conspiracy theorist by the very industry that was supposed to hold power accountable. Reuters. Then the Guardian. Then WIRED. Then Politico. Each outlet had been interested in her reporting right up to the moment it implicated someone whose advertising budget would be considered "material" relative to the publication's annual revenue. The pattern was always the same: initial enthusiasm, cautious legal review, increasingly nervous meetings with editors, and finally a conversation in which someone used the phrase "editorial standards" to mean "we can't afford to publish this."

She'd landed at The Parallel — an independent investigative outlet with a modest but devoted readership, fully funded by subscriber support and meagre institutional grants that valued truth over traffic. It was honest work on a budget that barely supported the next story. Enough to keep Nadia reporting, but it reached approximately one-fiftieth of the audience she'd had at Reuters. Most of the people who needed the information never saw it.

The Daniel Yoon story had caught her attention the moment it broke. Corporate espionage at NovaMind — that was a story she could have written in her sleep. Tech companies spied on each other constantly, professionally, and with plausible deniability. But the details didn't fit. The suspect wasn't a corporate spy. His background, NovaTok, pointed to a different origin. And the systems he'd accessed, based on what the Chronicle's sources were describing, were not proprietary technology a competitor would target. No one needed to steal NovaMind's public-facing technology. Their commercial applications were open source and well-documented — the company had made a point of it, publishing white papers and releasing code libraries that anyone could build on. If you wanted to build a competing product, you'd start with what they'd already given away and iterate.

What you would steal from NovaMind was operational intelligence. How the systems were deployed. Who was using them. What they were being used for.

Nadia had spent two days working her sources — former colleagues in tech journalism, a contact at the DA's office, a congressional staffer who owed her a favor from a story she'd killed at his request three years ago. The picture that emerged was consistent and alarming: the NovaMind breach targeted systems connected to government and defense applications. Not the commercial side. The shadow side.

Then Yoon died. Cardiac arrest in his cell, the night after his hearing. And Nadia Osei, who had spent six years being told she was paranoid, felt the familiar click of a pattern completing itself.

She knew that click. It was the sound of being right, and it was the loneliest sound in the world.

She pulled up the Chronicle's updated coverage. The story was already fading — a prisoner's death in custody was not, in the calculus of news editors, a compelling follow-up to a corporate espionage arrest that had itself been displaced by louder stories. The media's attention span was shorter than the public's.

But buried in the Chronicle's coverage, four paragraphs from the end of an article most readers would never reach, was a detail that made Nadia set down her coffee:

SFPD Detective James Morrow, lead investigator on the NovaMind case, was seen last Tuesday afternoon entering the UC Berkeley campus offices of Dr. Adrian Marsh, a professor of AI Ethics whose academic work was cited in materials seized from the suspect.

Adrian Marsh.

Nadia knew the name. She'd read his work — not because it was relevant to her investigation, but because it was among the few pieces of academic writing about AI and power that demonstrated actual understanding rather than performative concern. His paper on behavioral prediction architectures had been cited in her own reporting, back when mainstream outlets still published her reporting. He was smart, serious, and had the credibility that came from being a former insider who'd walked away — a man who'd made money in tech and then devoted his career to explaining why the industry that made him rich was destroying democracy.

She also knew that Morrow visiting Marsh's office meant something. Morrow was not a man who made social calls. She'd profiled him briefly while reporting on the SFPD's cybercrime division two years ago — a sharp, dogged investigator with a clear rate that was the envy of his department and a reputation for not wasting time. If he was visiting an AI ethics professor, it was because the case had moved beyond what his team could interpret alone.

Nadia opened a new document and began typing, the way she always did when the pieces were moving faster than her ability to publish them — a private chronology, timestamped, connecting the dots she could see and marking the spaces where dots should be but weren't.

NovaMind breach targets government/defense AI systems.

Suspect: Daniel Yoon, 26, former NovaTok employee.

Arrested with annotated academic work on AI vulnerabilities (Marsh).

Dies in custody. "Cardiac arrest." Night after hearing. No visible cause.

Lead detective consults Marsh days before Yoon's death.

Timing: Marsh consulted → investigation deepens → Yoon silenced. Someone knew.

She stared at the last line. It implied something she couldn't prove yet: that whoever killed Yoon had done so in response to a development in the investigation. Morrow bringing in an outside expert, someone who could actually interpret the technical evidence, had been the trigger. The people behind the curtain didn't mind a police investigation they could manage. What they couldn't allow was someone who understood the technology looking at what the technology actually did.

Nadia closed the document. Her source database remained on an air-gapped laptop in a safe deposit box at a credit union in Emeryville, backed up in two separate locations. She didn't need to expose those contacts to find a professor's public schedule.

She opened the UC Berkeley Computer Science department's website. Professor Adrian Marsh held office hours on Tuesdays and Thursdays, 3:00 to 5:00 p.m., in 773 Soda Hall.

But she wasn't going to Soda Hall. Office visits were formal, documented, and easily monitored. She needed something that looked like coincidence.

She checked Marsh's public calendar — academics put everything online — and found what she needed. Tomorrow, Wednesday, he had a two-hour gap between his morning seminar and a faculty meeting. On his personal website, a blog post from three months ago mentioned his regular coffee spot: a place called Artis on Shattuck Avenue, where he went "to grade papers and pretend I'm not procrastinating."

Academics. They told you everything about themselves if you just read what they wrote.

Nadia pulled up the location on her phone. Artis Coffee. Shattuck Avenue. A twelve-minute walk from Soda Hall. A place where a chance encounter would look natural — two people interested in the same news story, happening to sit at adjacent tables, falling into conversation the way people do in Berkeley coffee shops where every third person has an opinion about AI governance.

She closed her laptop and looked at the wall. Three walls of thread and paper and years of being told she was seeing things that weren't there. Three walls of connections that every editor she'd worked for had dismissed as pattern-matching gone feral.

Except now someone was dead. A twenty-six-year-old man had died in a jail cell because he'd touched the edge of something these powerful people wanted kept hidden. And the only detective investigating it had gone to the one academic who might understand what the technology actually revealed.

Nadia Osei was going to accidentally bump into Professor Adrian Marsh.

She was going to show him what she knew.

And she was going to find out what he knew, because in six years of chasing this story, she had never once had a source with his combination of technical expertise, institutional credibility, and — if the look on Morrow's face from the university's security camera footage was any indication — growing terror.

Terror was useful. Terror meant someone had seen the same thing she'd seen.

The only question was whether he'd seen enough to act on it, or just enough to be afraid.
