Chapter Forty.

The private dining room in Venn Tower in Seattle had been designed to project control. Italian marble floors. A table of polished walnut that seated twelve but tonight held seven. Floor-to-ceiling windows overlooking Puget Sound, where container ships moved like slow thoughts across dark water. The lighting was calibrated: warm enough to suggest intimacy, cool enough to remind everyone that this was business.

Tonight, the room projected something closer to a dentist's waiting room. Tension. Impatience. The discomfort of powerful people accustomed to controlling outcomes, discovering that this outcome was not cooperating.

Harrison Polk stood at the head of the table, less a position of authority than a position of having arrived first and not wanting to sit down. He'd been on the phone for the past four hours with Senate staffers, lobbyists, and a retired FBI deputy director who owed him a significant favor. The calls had not gone well. The retired deputy director had been the worst: "Harry, I can pull strings, but I can't pull strings that don't exist. These people aren't in any database I can access."

The movement, it turned out, was infuriatingly competent at not existing.

Elias Strutt was next to arrive, punctual as always. He sat at the far end of the table, illuminated by the glow of a tablet that appeared to contain the sum total of human digital interaction. His eyes moved across data with the metronomic precision of a machine reading its own output. He had not spoken since sitting down. Strutt communicated primarily through silence and the occasional data point delivered with the emotional warmth of a spreadsheet.

Arthur Venn sat to Polk's right, he was the host after all, with the patient stillness of a man who had long ago learned that the most powerful position in any room was the one that required the least movement. He'd built the infrastructure the modern world ran on. He could afford to wait.

Geoffrey Laine arrived next, looking like he'd just come from a UN reception, which he probably had. His suit was immaculate. His philanthropist's smile was firmly in place. But his eyes were doing that thing they did when Laine was worried: darting to exits, calculating distances, assessing the room as though measuring the distance to every lifeboat on a listing ship.

Clive Barrington swept in with the theatrical energy of a man who had spent fifty years commanding newsrooms and boardrooms and occasionally, it was rumored, small European governments. He was seventy-three, silver-haired, and moved with the aggressive confidence of someone who believed that age was something that happened to other people.

"Phase Two is live," Barrington announced, dropping into his chair. "Seventeen outlets, four countries, seventy-two hours. By Friday, these people won't be rebels. They'll be terrorists."

"And yet," Polk said, "they continue to operate."

"Operating and winning are different things, Harrison. I control what three hundred million people believe. These underground rats can scurry all they like. The public is mine."

"The public was always yours. The underground is the problem."

Magnus Cole arrived late, either a power move or a genuine inability to care about other people's schedules. Polk had never been able to tell the difference. Cole dropped into his chair with the energy of a man who found the entire proceeding beneath him but was willing to be entertained.

"Are we losing?" Cole asked, grinning. "Because I was told we don't lose."

"We're not losing," Polk said.

"We're not winning," Strutt said, without looking up from his tablet. It was the first thing he'd said in twenty minutes, and it landed like a brick on glass.

The room shifted. Venn's stillness became something harder. Laine's eyes stopped darting. Barrington's theatrical confidence flickered.

Cole laughed. "Beautiful. The Architect speaks. What's the damage, Elias?"

Strutt set his tablet down and looked at the room with the flat affect of a man who experienced other people's emotions as an abstraction he'd read about but never fully grasped. "I've had sixty-two analysts working full-time on digital surveillance for the past three weeks. We've intercepted over four hundred thousand communications flagged as potentially connected to the movement. Of those, ninety-seven percent are noise: unrelated chatter, false positives, deliberate misdirection. Of the remaining three percent, approximately half are communications we can decrypt. Of those, none have yielded actionable intelligence about the movement's leadership, structure, or operational plans."

Silence.

"It's the AI," Strutt continued. "Some form of adaptive counter-surveillance system operating at a level I've not encountered. It generates false signals, redirects our monitoring to innocent targets, and encrypts legitimate communications using keys that rotate on a pattern I cannot predict. My team has been chasing ghosts engineered to look exactly like the real thing."

"So crack the AI," Cole said, as though suggesting someone open a window.

"I intend to. But it will take time and resources beyond what I've currently allocated."

"Then allocate more."

Strutt's expression didn't change. "I wasn't asking permission."

Cole smirked and leaned back, arms folded. The two men's mutual contempt was one of the room's reliable constants, like gravity or Polk's exhaustion.

Kai Nakamura was the last to arrive. He slipped in quietly, nodded to the room, and took a seat in the middle of the table, neither at the head nor the foot. He pulled out his phone, glanced at it, and set it face-down. If the others noticed his arrival, they gave minimal indication.

This was Kai's position on the council, and it had been since the beginning: present, participating, unremarkable. The youngest by a considerable margin, the owner of NovaTok, a platform the older members still privately regarded as a fad, tolerated for the reach of his algorithm and the demographic data his company collected. He voted when votes were called. He offered opinions when opinions were solicited. He never drove the agenda.

Polk cleared his throat. "The situation is this. Barrington's media campaign is working on the public. Our polling data shows a twenty-three-point swing in public perception of the movement over the past ten days. But the movement itself is unaffected. They're not operating in the public sphere. They're underground, and our ability to reach them underground is —"

"Shit," Cole offered.

"— limited," Polk finished.

"So the public hates them, but we can't find them," Cole summarized. "That's like winning an argument with someone who's already left the room."

"An apt metaphor," Venn said, speaking for the first time. His voice was calm, measured, the voice of a man who processed the world as a supply chain and was currently identifying a bottleneck. "We've been approaching this as a public relations problem because that's where our strengths lie. But this isn't a public relations problem. It's an operational problem. We need to find them, identify them, and neutralize them. Media campaigns are theater. Useful theater, but theater."

Barrington's face tightened. "My theater, as you call it, is the reason the public isn't rallying behind these people."

"And if the public doesn't know who these people are, what exactly are they not rallying behind?" Venn's tone was mild. His point was not.

"Gentlemen," Polk said, with the weariness of a man who had been managing egos since before several of the people at this table had been born. "The media campaign continues. But Arthur is right: we need an operational solution. Elias, you said you can crack their AI. How long?"

"Weeks. Possibly less if I reassign teams from other projects."

"Do it."

"I said I wasn't asking permission."

"And I said do it. For the record." Polk turned to the room. "In the meantime, our contractor continues to work the list. Several confirmed kills since the initial custody action. But the targets are getting harder. The movement is adapting."

"They should be," Strutt said. "They have good operational security. Which is another way of saying their AI is better than my team."

This admission, from Strutt, was the equivalent of a normal person screaming into a pillow. The room absorbed it with varying degrees of alarm.

Cole leaned forward, and the performance dropped away. The grin was gone. What replaced it was something Polk had rarely seen from the Showman: genuine strategic intelligence, unadorned.

"I can handle this myself," Cole said. His voice was flat, calm, entirely serious. "I have private security operations across fourteen countries. I have surveillance contractors who don't need warrants. I have the infrastructure to find these people and end this without a committee meeting every time someone needs to make a decision." He looked around the table. "I'm telling you this as a courtesy. Not asking permission."

The room went still. This was not bluster. This was Cole threatening to break from the council, to operate independently, and the implicit message was clear: the alliance was optional.

"That would be extraordinarily unwise," Venn said.

"Unwise is sitting in this room while people who can barely afford a server farm are outmaneuvering six of the wealthiest men on the planet."

"Magnus." Polk's voice carried steel. "We stay coordinated, or we give them exactly what they want: six separate, uncoordinated responses they can pick apart one at a time."

Cole held Polk's gaze for three seconds, then leaned back. The threat remained in the air like smoke.

Into the silence, Laine spoke. "What if we're approaching this backwards?"

Every head turned. Laine's contributions were usually moral hand-wringing followed by acquiescence. This was different. His voice had the measured tone of a man who had been thinking while everyone else was talking.

"The movement has public sympathy because it claims to stand for transparency and accountability. Those are popular ideas. You can't kill popular ideas with media campaigns. You co-opt them." He paused, letting the room catch up. "My foundation funds a new transparency initiative. Real funding. Real staff. A friendly alternative that advocates for reform through institutional channels. We give the public somewhere constructive to direct their energy. The underground movement becomes redundant. The radical version withers because the moderate version exists."

Barrington stared. Venn's eyebrows rose a fraction of a centimeter, which for Venn was the equivalent of a standing ovation. Even Cole looked momentarily caught off guard.

"That's..." Polk considered. "Actually worth discussing."

"It's a long game," Strutt said. "We don't have time for long games."

"We run it in parallel," Laine said. "Short-term: Elias cracks their AI. Long-term: we drain their support base."

"The question," Kai said, and the room turned to him with the mild surprise of people who had forgotten he was there, "is how we find their physical infrastructure in the meantime. We know they have hardware somewhere. Server locations. Staging areas. The AI protects their communications, but the physical assets have to exist in the real world." He pulled up something on his phone. "I've been thinking about a passive surveillance approach. Instead of hunting their digital traffic, we monitor the infrastructure they depend on: geothermal data centers, academic computing clusters, the physical locations where distributed processing networks need to be housed. Flag anomalous power consumption patterns. Cross-reference with facility access logs."

It sounded reasonable. Thorough. Exactly what a technically literate council member would naturally offer.

Polk glanced at Strutt, who was already taking notes. Power consumption and access logs. A second line of inquiry, independent of the traffic their analysts had spent weeks failing to decipher. Whether it would find a hidden operational wing or merely produce another mountain of irrelevant data remained to be seen.

"Do it," Polk said. "Coordinate with Elias."

Kai nodded and returned to his phone.

They discussed logistics. Funding. The contractor's next targets. Barrington's Phase Two timeline. Venn's offer to restrict cloud service access for flagged accounts.

It was, Polk reflected, the machinery of power doing what the machinery of power did: grinding. Slowly, heavily, with considerable friction and noise. But grinding.

The problem was that the thing they were grinding against was not cooperating.

The meeting ended near midnight. Cars were called. Coats were retrieved. Laine was the first out, followed by Barrington, then Strutt, then Cole (who paused at the door to clap Venn on the shoulder and say something that made Venn's expression curdle).

Kai lingered. He stood at the window, looking out at Puget Sound. The container ships were still moving, slow, steady, indifferent to the machinations of the men who believed they controlled the world.

Polk walked over. "You made a good point. About decentralized systems."

"It was an obvious point."

"Sometimes obvious points need saying."

Kai said nothing. His reflection in the glass was a silhouette, dark against the distant lights of the sound.

"You seem distracted tonight," Polk said.

"I'm always distracted. Short-form content has ruined my attention span." Kai smiled, a thin, professional smile that Polk had seen a thousand times at a thousand networking events. "Goodnight, Harrison."

He walked out. Polk watched him go, then turned back to the window.

The container ships moved through the dark water. The machinery ground on.

And somewhere, in the spaces between powerful men's certainties, something they could not see continued to grow.
