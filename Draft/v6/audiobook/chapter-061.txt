Chapter Sixty One.

Monday, 10:17 a.m. CET — twenty six hours remaining

The houseboat on the Prinsengracht was exactly where Nadia said it would be, moored between a flower shop and a cheese museum, because Amsterdam — cheese museum next to canal, tulip market next to red-light district — was a city that expected everyone to act like this was normal.

Adrian found the ceramic frog on the deck. It was a remarkably ugly frog, hand-painted in shades of green that did not exist in nature, and the key was underneath it, exactly as promised. He let himself in, locked the door behind him, and moved through the narrow cabin without pausing — checking the portholes, the exits, the sight lines, his body cataloging the space with a speed that would have surprised him three months ago. No hesitation. This was his territory now.

The houseboat smelled of old wood, linseed oil, and pipe tobacco. The retired Dutch intelligence officer had left the place tidy but lived-in: a bookshelf of spy novels in four languages, a chess set mid-game on a small table, and a note on the kitchen counter that read, in neat English: Coffee in the tin. Don't use the microwave, it trips the fuse. Good luck with whatever you're doing. Burn this note.

He burned the note over the gas stove, then made coffee. It was 10:17 a.m. on Monday. Twenty-six hours until the Tuesday deadline.

He spread Priya's maps across the galley table.

The nausea came immediately. Not seasickness — the houseboat's motion was gentle. This was the sick, physical wrongness of opening a dead woman's work and sitting in her place. Priya's fingerprints were on the paper. A coffee ring in one corner, her coffee, from a morning she'd had and he hadn't witnessed. Adrian pressed the back of his hand against his mouth, breathed through it, and kept going.

The network diagrams were drawn on translucent paper in Priya's distinctive hand, a blend of engineering precision and artistic sensitivity that made the diagrams look like architectural blueprints crossed with abstract art. Each target company's internal infrastructure was rendered in extraordinary detail: server clusters, data warehouses, communication platforms, financial systems, access control layers. Every node labeled. Every connection mapped. Every vulnerability annotated with Priya's small, angular handwriting.

Four companies were complete: Strutt's surveillance infrastructure, Venn's cloud services, Cole's corporate network, and Barrington's media conglomerate.

Two were incomplete. Geoffrey Laine's foundation systems. And Kai Nakamura's short-form content platform, NovaTok.

Adrian picked up Laine's diagram first. Priya had roughed out the outer architecture, the public-facing systems, the standard administrative infrastructure, but the inner layers were sketched rather than detailed. The foundation had consolidated its grant-management systems, and the revised architecture hadn't been fully mapped before Priya was killed.

But Adrian understood the foundation's systems. He had reviewed similar platforms while evaluating technology companies funded through philanthropic grants. He knew how their public claims of openness could coexist with private administrative records, and how easily the latter disappeared behind layers of contractors. Priya's files gave him the current structure. His experience helped him read it.

Laine's foundation organized its systems around separate programs, each with its own contractors and reporting requirements. Public grant information occupied one layer; internal decisions and financial records occupied another. The trick was knowing where those layers met. That was where the foundation's account of itself could be checked against what it had actually done.

Adrian began filling in the gaps. Priya's diagrams and Cardinal's recent updates lay beside his pencil. His memories helped him interpret the design choices; the current records supplied the addresses and routes his memory could not.

For an hour he concentrated on the annotations. An obsolete route here. A relocated data store there. He cross-checked every change against the latest dated update before extending a line.

Priya would have been faster. Priya had been inside these systems, walking the digital hallways every day, seeing the architecture from within. Adrian was working from the outside, reconstructing from memory and inference what she had known from experience.

But he was good at this. Better than he'd expected. The years of evaluating tech companies had trained him to read infrastructure — the visible structures implying the hidden ones, the architecture revealing its purpose through its form.

By 11:17 a.m., Laine's map was complete. He turned to Kai Nakamura's company.

And here he paused.

Kai's company was the one he knew best. He had been there at the beginning, when it was just an idea, a prototype, two engineers in a rented office in San Jose. He had put in money and mentorship when no one else would. He had watched the architecture grow from a single server to a global content platform that shaped the information diet of a billion people.

He knew where the bodies were buried. Metaphorically speaking, though given the past three months, perhaps not entirely metaphorically.

Adrian put pencil to paper. His hand stopped.

The primary data cluster — the one in the diagram's upper left — he knew this one. He'd been in the room when Kai sketched it on a napkin in a San Jose diner, 2014, rain on the windows, both of them too wired to sleep. That cluster had held the first million user profiles. Kai had named the servers after Studio Ghibli characters.

His fingers tightened around the pencil. He became aware that he wasn't breathing. He set it down, forced air in, and pressed both palms flat on the galley table. The houseboat rocked. Through the porthole, someone laughed on a passing boat.

He picked the pencil back up. Drew the next node.

The Series B expansion. Kai calling him at midnight, ecstatic. Adrian marked the old cluster, checked the migration note in Priya's files, and drew the new connection. The short-form pivot he had argued against was there too. Memories told him what the choices had meant. The dated plans told him what remained.

This was the hardest thing he'd done. Not technically. Technically it was straightforward. But his hands kept stopping, and each time they stopped he had to choose, again, to keep going.

"I didn't walk away from what we believed. I walked into it."

Kai's words at Davos. Adrian had taken them as arrogance, the self-justification of a man who had traded ideals for empire. Now, alone on a houseboat in Amsterdam, pencil in hand, mapping the internal architecture of his former friend's company for the purpose of exposing its every secret to the world, the words returned with a different resonance.

Walked into what?

Adrian shook his head. Kai sat on the council. Seb had described him agreeing to "aggressive measures" in earlier meetings. He had no evidence that Kai had opposed the killings. That was the fact Adrian could use; the rest was guesswork.

Whatever we were to each other, that's over.

Adrian put the pencil to paper and began mapping the infrastructure of the company his former best friend had built.

By 11:55 a.m., the final corrections were on the page. Four sheets of translucent paper, taped together, carrying Priya's confirmed routes and Adrian's additions. He checked the update dates once more.

He sat back and looked at it. The coffee was cold. The houseboat rocked gently on the canal. Through the porthole, he could see a tourist boat passing, passengers taking photos of the canal houses, oblivious to the fact that twelve feet away, a man was diagramming the infrastructure for the largest coordinated data breach in human history.

Six maps. Six companies — and Polk's lobbying network threaded through all of them, a seventh target folded into the six, because Polk's financial architecture didn't live in one place. It lived in the spaces between the others, in the PAC structures and shell companies that connected the oligarchs to the legislators they'd purchased. Sophie would trace those threads once the data flowed.

Seven targets total. Six keys to six kingdoms, and one skeleton key to the corridor connecting them all.

Polk's archive needed its own endpoint. Sophie had identified a server in his Washington office that used Venn's managed network software. Andrei could adapt the same entry chain. A local associate already held one of Tomás's modular spare devices; Nadia would send the new route through her courier chain and have the plant confirmed before the final checks. Six original devices, one additional node in the heartbeat.

Adrian gathered the diagrams, photographed them with the burner phone's camera, no network connection, images stored locally, and placed the originals in a waterproof bag.

Then he picked up a burner phone, dialed the relay number Nadia had given him, and left a message:

"Maps complete. Six tech companies and Polk's network — seven targets ready."

He hung up, snapped the SIM card, and dropped it in his coffee cup.

Outside, Amsterdam carried on being Amsterdam: bicycles, canals, the gentle absurdity of a city built on wooden stilts in a swamp. Adrian leaned against the galley counter and closed his eyes. He thought of Priya, who had started this work. He thought of Seb, who had started the movement that needed this work. He thought of Kai, whose company he had just mapped for destruction.

And then he thought of the clock.

Twenty-four hours and twenty-two minutes. The courier was due shortly after noon. The packages still had to be written.

He opened his eyes, picked up the waterproof bag, and began preparing for the next phase.
