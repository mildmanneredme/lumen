Chapter Thirty One.

The oak-paneled conference room on the forty-first floor of Strutt's San Jose offices had been swept for listening devices three times in the past hour. The first sweep was conducted by Strutt's corporate security team, former NSA operatives who used military-grade spectrum analyzers to detect any electromagnetic emission that didn't belong. The second sweep was conducted by an independent contractor hired by Polk, because trusting any single oligarch's security team to sweep a room used by all of them was a vulnerability Polk was unwilling to accept. The third sweep was conducted by a device Kai Nakamura placed on the table when he arrived, a small black disc, about the size of a hockey puck, that emitted a low hum and which he described, when asked, as "a personal precaution."

None of the other council members objected. They'd all developed personal precautions.

"The good news," Elias Strutt said, standing at the head of the table with the posture of a man who had long ago replaced social comfort with informational superiority, "is that the surveillance program is working."

He tapped his tablet. The room's display wall, a forty-foot seamless LED panel that could render satellite imagery at a resolution sufficient to read a car's license plate, came alive with a network diagram. Nodes, connections, clusters. Each node was labeled with a code name, and many were now highlighted in red, green, or gray.

"Red nodes are confirmed movement members, identified through digital communications intercepts, metadata analysis, and behavioral pattern recognition. Green nodes are under active surveillance. Gray nodes are eliminated threats."

Six gray nodes on the wall. Six people, reduced to gray circles on a display in a climate-controlled conference room.

"Our specialist has been effective," Polk said. He didn't name Raven. He never named her. "The six eliminated targets include the San Francisco cell member, the insider we discussed at our last meeting, and four additional operatives identified through Elias's surveillance."

"The movement has responded by going dark," Strutt continued. "After the insider's death, their communication patterns changed dramatically. Encrypted traffic on known channels dropped by ninety percent. They've either shifted to channels we haven't identified yet or they've gone analog. In-person meetings, physical dead drops, courier-based communication."

"Analog?" Cole said with visible contempt. "What is this, the Cold War?"

"It's effective," Strutt said. "Our advantage is digital. If they abandon digital communication, our surveillance becomes significantly less useful. We can still track physical movements through facial recognition and location data from cellular networks, but that's orders of magnitude more resource-intensive than intercepting their encrypted traffic."

"So they're adapting," Venn said from his customary position at the far end of the table. "What's the assessment of their capability? Can they actually execute whatever they're planning?"

Strutt adjusted his glasses. "Our analysis suggests they're building toward a coordinated action against our companies' digital infrastructure. The insider, the one our specialist eliminated, was a senior engineer at my company. She'd been mapping our internal network architecture. The question is: was she working alone, or is she one of several?"

"Several," Barrington said. "Obviously."

"If she's one of several, then they have people inside multiple companies conducting the same kind of reconnaissance. The threat isn't theoretical. They're building operational capability."

The room absorbed this. The air conditioning hummed. The display wall pulsed with its network of colored nodes.

Kai Nakamura studied the diagram from his seat in the middle of the table. His face revealed nothing, the same engaged-but-unremarkable expression he wore at every council meeting. The expression of a junior member absorbing information, following the discussion, contributing when relevant.

He lingered over the gray nodes. Daniel Yoon's name had been public for weeks. Priya Chandrasekaran's death had reached the industry in a short notice from her employer. The other names were on the target sheets Strutt had circulated before the meeting. Kai read each one twice.

Polk watched him. "Something wrong?"

"I want the source and confidence level attached to every identification," Kai said. "A gray circle isn't an audit trail."

"You have the packets."

"Then put the references on the diagram."

Strutt tapped the tablet. Case numbers appeared beneath the nodes. Kai nodded once. The request had sounded like caution about liability. That was an acceptable thing for him to express in this room.

"What about leadership?" Venn asked. "Have we identified anyone at the top?"

"Not yet," Strutt said. "The organization is well-compartmentalized. The cell members we've identified don't know who runs the operation. They communicate through intermediaries and encrypted channels. We've identified some mid-level coordinators, but the leadership structure remains opaque."

"That's unacceptable," Barrington said. "We're spending resources identifying foot soldiers when the generals are the real threat. Cut the head off the snake."

Kai spoke. "Elias, what about the AI system you mentioned at the last meeting? The one managing their communications?"

Strutt nodded. "We've confirmed the existence of a sophisticated AI agent coordinating the movement's digital operations. It's managing encryption, routing communications, and likely conducting counter-surveillance against our own systems. My team is working on identifying its infrastructure."

"If you can compromise the AI," Kai said, "you compromise the entire network. Every member, every communication, every operational detail, it would all be accessible. That should be the priority."

Several heads nodded around the table. Sound strategic logic: attack the nervous system, not the limbs.

"Agreed," Polk said. "Elias, make the AI your primary target. If we can turn their own system against them, we end this in one stroke."

Strutt's expression suggested that "one stroke" significantly underestimated the technical challenge, but he didn't argue. "I'll allocate additional resources. But I want to manage expectations. If the AI is as sophisticated as I believe it is, compromising it will take time. And whoever built it is not amateur."

"Whoever built it," Cole said, leaning back with the expansive posture of a man who occupied space the way a gas fills a room, "has made a fatal mistake. They built a single point of failure. One system, managing everything. Break that system, and everything it protects becomes visible."

Kai said nothing. His face remained neutral. He lowered one hand to his lap.

Under the table, he pressed his thumbnail into the side of one finger until the pressure steadied him. Cole was waiting for a response. Kai gave him a small nod, no more than the statement required.

"Anything else?" Polk asked.

The room was silent.

"Then we continue as planned. Surveillance on the network. Specialist operations against identified targets. And a priority effort to compromise the AI." Polk looked around the table. "Gentlemen, Kai, we're making progress. The goal is simple: dismantle this organization before it can execute whatever it's building toward. We have the resources, the technology, and the determination. They have ideology and desperation. History tells us how that equation resolves."

The meeting adjourned. The council members stood, exchanged the minimal pleasantries that their mutual distrust allowed, and departed through separate exits.

Kai was the last to leave. He picked up his black disc from the table, slipped it into his jacket pocket, and stood for a moment at the window, looking out at the Silicon Valley sprawl below: the office parks, the Tesla-filled parking lots, the gleaming campuses of companies that employed hundreds of thousands and controlled the information flows of billions.

His own offices stood somewhere beyond the next row of campuses. He could return there, speak about engagement figures, eat whatever his assistant ordered. The day would make room for its ordinary demands.

He took out the black disc. It had finished storing the meeting. Kai checked the seal on its casing, then put it away without opening the file. There were several reasons a man might want a record of what had been said in that room. He had given the others none of them.

At the elevator, his phone rang. He let it ring until the doors closed.
