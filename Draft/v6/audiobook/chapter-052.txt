Chapter Fifty Two.

The second emergency session began five hours after the zero-day attack. Polk and Barrington sat in Polk's dining room; the others joined by video from their crisis management centers. The damage assessments were terrible and getting worse.

The agenda had one item, and Polk delivered it without ceremony.

"Sebastian Hale."

The name landed on the table like a brick.

"Our intelligence from the AI breach, the data we extracted before the zero-day fired, gives us enough to identify the movement's leadership structure. Hale is the head. He's been the driving force behind everything: the recruitment, the philosophy, the operational direction. Every thread leads back to him."

"So arrest him," Barrington said.

"Arresting him takes time. Legal process. International coordination. Even with Laine's government contacts, we're looking at days, possibly weeks, to navigate the jurisdictional complexities. He's a British citizen with residences in three countries and lawyers who make our lawyers nervous."

"Then don't arrest him," Cole said from his screen, his voice carrying the rawness of a man who had been screaming at his engineering team for twelve hours. "We've discussed this before. We have a contractor for exactly this situation."

The room went quiet. Not the productive quiet of people thinking, but the charged quiet of people confronting a line they'd already crossed and were being asked to cross again, further.

"We authorized the contractor for targeted operations against operational threats," Laine said. His voice had the careful precision of a man choosing his words for a future transcript. "Movement members who posed immediate security risks. Hale is a political figure. A public activist. His disappearance, or worse, would generate attention we cannot afford."

"His continued existence is generating damage we cannot afford," Cole shot back. "Every hour he's alive, he's coordinating what's left of the movement. You want to wait for lawyers while he rebuilds?"

"The movement is crippled," Strutt said. "Their AI is destroyed. Their communications are exposed. Their membership is scattered. Hale is leading a broken organization."

"Broken organizations can heal. Dead leaders can't." Cole's bluntness had the effect, as it often did, of saying the thing everyone was thinking and nobody wanted to articulate. "I'm calling the question. Do we authorize the contractor to eliminate Sebastian Hale? Yes or no."

"This isn't a vote on a quarterly earnings report, Magnus," Laine said.

"No, it's more important than a quarterly earnings report. Which is why we should stop pretending we're anything other than what we are and make the decision."

Polk looked around the room. He'd been managing this group for months, their egos, their competing interests, their varying degrees of moral flexibility. He knew each of them well enough to predict their positions before they stated them.

Cole: yes, enthusiastically. The Showman viewed direct action as proof of strength.

Strutt: yes, analytically. The Architect would calculate that removing the leadership node was the most efficient way to collapse the remaining network.

Barrington: yes, with caveats. The Narrator would want to control the story around the death: natural causes, accident, something his media empire could spin.

Venn: yes, reluctantly. The Machine would see it as a necessary optimization of an inefficient process.

Laine: conflicted, but ultimately acquiescent. The Philanthropist would agonize, then defer to the consensus, as he always did. His moral hesitations were real, but they had never, in the history of this council, overridden the collective decision.

Kai: unknown.

Polk turned to the youngest member. "Kai? Thoughts?"

Kai's image occupied a tile near the center of Polk's screen. He had contributed two operational observations, neither staking out a position. Standard behavior. Expected.

He looked up from his phone, the gesture he always made when drawn into a discussion he'd been monitoring peripherally. His expression was neutral. Considered.

"Are we sure this is the right moment?"

The question hung in the air. Polk studied it from multiple angles, as he studied everything.

"Elaborate," Polk said.

"Hale is the visible leader. If we remove him now, during the chaos, while we're still managing our own crises, it looks reactive. Desperate. If the death is ever connected to us, and in this environment we have to assume that's possible, it becomes evidence that we struck at their leader because we were afraid."

"We're not afraid," Cole said. "We're efficient."

"We can be both. I'm asking about timing, not principle." Kai's voice was measured. Reasonable. The voice of a strategist offering a tactical observation. "If we wait until our systems are stabilized, until we're operating from strength rather than crisis —"

"The 48-hour window works both ways," Strutt interrupted. "While our systems are in crisis, so is the movement. They're exposed, scattered, without their AI. If we wait, they regroup. If we act now, we hit them at their lowest point."

Kai nodded slowly, as though absorbing the counterargument with appropriate seriousness. "Fair point. I defer to the consensus."

He picked up his phone. Returned to it. Disengaged.

Polk watched Kai's image on the screen. His question was reasonable. His deference came too quickly. There was tension in his jaw that the camera's poor resolution could not conceal.

"Kai. Your vote?"

Kai looked away from the screen, then back. "Yes."

Polk entered it in the record. Unanimous.

The silence afterward lasted several seconds. Nobody asked what Kai had been looking at. Nobody wanted to prolong a decision they had already made.

"I'll contact the intermediary now," Polk said. "The contractor is already in the region."

The meeting moved on to other business. Damage containment. Communications strategy. The public narrative around the zero-day attack.

Kai participated. He made one comment about platform recovery timelines. He agreed with a proposal from Venn about coordinated system patching. He was present, professional, and entirely forgettable.

When the meeting ended, Polk closed the voting record and opened the next damage assessment. Kai's hesitation stayed with him.

It could mean fear of exposure, fear of a political killing, or merely fear of losing control. Any of those was plausible. He made a note to speak to him alone when the crisis was contained.

Then the phone rang, and another part of their infrastructure failed.
