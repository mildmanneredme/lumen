Part Two. The Descent.

Chapter Nineteen.

The hood smelled like someone else's sweat.

Adrian Marsh sat in the back seat of a vehicle he couldn't identify, breathing through fabric that had been pulled over his head without ceremony or apology, and tried to reconstruct the sequence of events that had brought him here. Twenty minutes ago, or was it forty, he'd been standing on the corner of Valencia and 22nd, waiting for a contact who turned out to be a woman in her sixties wearing a Cal Berkeley windbreaker and the expression of someone who had stopped being impressed by anything decades ago. She'd said one word: "Marsh." Then she'd gestured toward a gray Honda Civic idling at the curb.

He'd gotten in. Because of course he had. Because somewhere between the anonymous encrypted messages and the dead cell member and the look on Detective Morrow's face when the coroner's report came back clean, Adrian had crossed a line he couldn't uncross. He was no longer a professor consulting on a case. He was something else entirely, and the something else was currently wearing a hood in a moving car.

The driver hadn't spoken. Neither had the woman in the passenger seat, a different woman, younger, who'd patted him down with the brisk efficiency of someone who'd done this before. She'd taken his phone, his wallet, his keys, and a ballpoint pen he'd forgotten was in his jacket pocket.

"The pen?" he'd asked.

"You'd be surprised," she'd said, and that was the end of their conversation.

They'd been driving for what Adrian estimated was thirty minutes, though the disorientation of the hood made time elastic. He'd tried to track the turns: left on what felt like a major road, right, another right, a long straight stretch that could have been the 101 or the 280 or, for all he knew, a runway at SFO. The car stopped twice. Doors opened. Voices murmured. Then they were moving again.

At some point, they switched vehicles. He was guided out of the Civic and into something larger, an SUV, judging by the step-up and the leather seats. Different driver. Different air freshener. Same silence.

"Is this really necessary?" Adrian asked the darkness inside the hood.

"Yes," said a male voice he hadn't heard before.

The SUV made a series of turns that felt deliberately random, doubling back, circling, a countersurveillance route designed to defeat anyone following with GPS or line of sight. Countersurveillance. Adrian had read about tradecraft in the context of Cold War history. Living it was considerably less romantic than the textbooks suggested.

When the vehicle finally stopped and the engine cut, someone took his arm and helped him out. Gravel crunched under his shoes. The air was different: cooler, damper, carrying the mineral smell of the Pacific. They were somewhere near the coast. North of the city, he guessed. Maybe Marin. Maybe further.

He was led up three steps, wooden and creaking, through a door, and down a hallway. The floor changed from wood to concrete. A second door opened, heavier, with the solid thunk of reinforced steel.

The hood came off.

Adrian blinked in fluorescent light. He was in a converted industrial space. Concrete walls, exposed ductwork, the utilitarian aesthetic of a place that could have been a startup's office or a survivalist's bunker. A long table dominated the center of the room, surrounded by mismatched chairs. The walls were bare except for a single large whiteboard covered in a web of handwritten notes, connecting lines, and organizational charts rendered in four different colors of dry-erase marker.

Seven people were already in the room. They watched him with expressions ranging from curiosity to open hostility.

A woman in her forties with close-cropped silver hair and the lean build of a distance runner stepped forward. "Professor Marsh. I'm going to be direct with you, because we don't have time for anything else." She had a slight accent, South African, maybe. "You've been brought here because certain people believe you can be useful. Other people in this room believe you're a liability who should be blindfolded, driven to the middle of nowhere, and left there. We're going to determine which assessment is correct."

"I appreciate the candor," Adrian said.

"Don't appreciate it yet. You haven't met everyone."

She turned toward a door at the far end of the room, a door Adrian hadn't noticed, recessed into the concrete wall, heavy enough to belong in a bank vault.

"He's ready," the woman said, and knocked twice.

The door opened from the inside.

Behind it, lit by the warm glow of a desk lamp that seemed to belong to an entirely different century than the fluorescent overheads of the room Adrian stood in, a man sat at a wooden desk covered in papers, three laptops, and what appeared to be a half-eaten sandwich.

The man looked up. His eyes were the color of glacial ice, and they locked onto Adrian's with an intensity that felt almost physical.

"Professor Marsh," the man said. "I've been reading your work for years. I have to say: your paper on algorithmic accountability was brilliant. Your paper on ethical frameworks for autonomous systems was better. And your op-ed last month about the impossibility of reforming tech from within was the most eloquently wrong thing I've read in a decade."

He stood. He was tall, six-two, maybe six-three, with the angular frame of a man who either exercised with discipline or forgot to eat with regularity. His hair was dark, slightly too long, pushed back from a forehead that seemed designed for frowning. He wore a charcoal sweater over a collared shirt, the sleeves pushed up to his elbows, and his hands were stained with ink.

"My name is Sebastian Hale," he said. "And I need you to understand something before we go any further."

He crossed the room in three strides and stood directly in front of Adrian, close enough that Adrian could see the faint scar that ran along his jawline and the dark circles under his eyes.

"Everything you think you know about what's happening," Seb said, "is the surface of the surface of the surface."

He smiled. It was not a reassuring smile.

"Welcome to the deep end, Professor."
