Chapter Forty Four.

Detective James Morrow had been a cop for twenty-three years, and in that time he'd learned to trust two things: his gut and his shoe leather. Technology helped. Databases helped. Forensic analysis helped. But at the end of the day, cases were solved by showing up, asking questions, and paying attention to the things that didn't fit.

Something about the Adrian Marsh case didn't fit.

Earlier that week, before the meetings in Vauxhall, Morrow had sat in the business class cabin of a flight from New York to London. The upgrade came from a "law enforcement travel assistance program" his department liaison had arranged with remarkable efficiency. He had never flown business class in his life. A flight attendant offered him warm nuts in a ceramic bowl, and he accepted with the bewildered gratitude of a man accustomed to airport pretzels.

He was following Adrian Marsh to Europe. Santos was back in San Francisco, holding down the case from the precinct — pulling records, managing the evidence log, doing the quiet, steady work that made the headline work possible. Morrow had tried to explain why he needed to go himself, and Santos had looked at him with that flat, patient expression that meant he already understood and didn't need the explanation.

The evidence trail was compelling. Since Marsh had disappeared from his Berkeley home six weeks ago ("sabbatical," his department chair had said, with the vagueness that suggested he'd been told to say it), a steady stream of intelligence had placed him in Tallinn, Berlin, Geneva, Lisbon, Reykjavik, and most recently Davos. Financial records. Hotel registrations. Airport security footage. Digital breadcrumbs that, when assembled, painted the picture of a man traveling the world in connection with the very movement Morrow was investigating.

The evidence was damning. It was also, if you squinted at it the right way, almost suspiciously convenient.

Morrow had built cases against smart people before. Drug traffickers with MBA-level operational security. White-collar criminals who laundered money through structures so complex they needed flowcharts to explain to juries. Hackers who lived in encrypted worlds and left digital footprints measured in fractions of bytes.

None of them had left trails this clean.

The dossier showed hotel payments in every city Marsh had visited. Credit card transactions under cover names, matched to guest registries accessible to law enforcement with appropriate warrants. Passport scans in three countries had been linked to those identities and then to Marsh, not through any action of Morrow's but through "allied intelligence sharing" from sources his FBI liaison described only as "cooperative partners." The transactions checked out. The identity links were another part of the trail someone had assembled for him.

It was like following a man who was dropping breadcrumbs on purpose. Except Morrow didn't think Marsh was that careless. The man was a professor of AI ethics who had been an angel investor in Silicon Valley. He understood technology. He understood operational security. If he didn't want to be found, he'd be harder to find than this.

So either Marsh was making mistakes, possible but unlikely, or someone else was making sure Morrow could follow.

The question was who. And why.

Morrow pulled out his notebook, actual paper, actual pen, a habit his younger colleagues found quaint and his results proved effective, and wrote two words at the top of a fresh page:

Too easy.

He listed the unsolicited material: an anonymous tip connecting Marsh to movement communications; financial records from a Virginia server; Tallinn airport footage supplied through a liaison whose response had been unusually quick.

Each item had checked out. Together, they raised a question none could answer. Who had assembled the trail, and what had been left out?

London would give him a chance to ask. His FBI liaison had arranged meetings with a British counterterrorism intelligence unit. Morrow would share his findings. He would also request the provenance of theirs.

The flight attendant returned. "Another glass of wine, sir?"

"I'm good. Thanks."

She smiled the smile of someone who dealt with powerful people all day and had learned to treat everyone as though they might be one. Morrow watched her go and wondered, not for the first time, what it was like to live in a world where warm nuts in ceramic bowls were an unremarkable feature of air travel.

He turned back to his notebook. Beneath Too easy, he wrote a second entry:

Who benefits from Marsh being found?

The obvious answer was the companies that had been targeted by the movement. They wanted the espionage network exposed, the perpetrators identified and prosecuted, the threat neutralized. That was straightforward.

But the quality of the intelligence Morrow was receiving went beyond what corporate security teams typically produced. The financial forensics were deep. The surveillance data was comprehensive. The international coordination was seamless. This wasn't a company protecting its assets. This was an operation.

And operations had operators.

Morrow closed his notebook, finished his warm nuts, and looked out the window at the Atlantic, thirty-seven thousand feet below. The ocean was a featureless gray, the same shade as the sky above it, so that the horizon line was invisible and the plane seemed to be floating in an undifferentiated void.

He thought about Adrian Marsh. The man he'd first met in a Berkeley office, surrounded by books and coffee cups and the comfortable clutter of an academic life. The man who'd been surprised and slightly annoyed to have a detective in his office, but who'd looked at the case materials with the genuine curiosity of someone who cared about understanding things.

Morrow had liked him. That was the problem with this job; you sometimes liked the people you were chasing. And liking them didn't change the evidence. The evidence said Marsh was involved with a criminal organization operating across international borders. The evidence said he'd abandoned his position, traveled to six countries, and met with individuals connected to corporate espionage, hacking, and what was increasingly being classified as cyberterrorism.

The evidence said all of this clearly, consistently, and conveniently.

Too easy.

The plane descended through clouds over Heathrow. London materialized below, gray and sprawling and ancient and modern, a city that had been the center of the world's information for centuries and was now, depending on your perspective, either still the center or a very convincing replica.

Morrow collected his bag, cleared customs with his law enforcement credentials, and stepped into the arrivals hall.

A man in a dark suit was holding a sign with his name.

That was new. Morrow had expected to take the Tube.

"Detective Morrow? I'm with the liaison office. Car's outside."

The car was a black sedan with tinted windows. The driver was silent. The route took them not to Scotland Yard, as Morrow had expected, but to a building in Vauxhall that Morrow recognized from news footage as the headquarters of a certain intelligence agency that technically didn't admit to being a certain intelligence agency.

The small doubt in his gut grew a little larger.

He was being steered. By whom and toward what, he didn't yet know.

But twenty-three years had taught him that when the path was this smooth, it had been paved by someone, and the person paving it always wanted you to arrive at a specific destination.

Morrow straightened his tie, stepped out of the sedan, and walked into a building full of people who knew far more than he did.

He intended to change that.
