Chapter Sixty Eight.

Monday, 1:17 p.m. CET — twenty three hours remaining

The conference call was encrypted, or at least, it had been encrypted before the AI's final zero-day attack had torn through the encryption protocols of every company represented on the call. The oligarchs' engineers had patched the most critical vulnerabilities within the first twelve hours, but the word "patched" was being used loosely. What they had actually done was apply the digital equivalent of duct tape to a breach the size of a highway, and everyone on the call knew it.

Harrison Polk moderated. He always moderated. It was his particular genius, the ability to manage a room full of men whose combined net worth exceeded the GDP of most nations and whose combined ego exceeded the GDP of all of them.

Now the call was fracturing.

"I want to know," Magnus Cole said, his voice carrying the timbre of a man who was accustomed to being the most important person in any conversation and was currently discovering that importance did not confer immunity from catastrophe, "why my entire autonomous driving division's source code is currently accessible to anyone with a laptop and a Wi-Fi connection."

"It's not accessible," Arthur Venn said, with the mechanical patience of a man who had explained the same thing four times in the past hour. "The zero-day created a window. My engineers have closed the window. The data that was exposed during the window is being assessed."

"Assessed." Cole made the word sound like a profanity. "My market cap dropped eleven percent in twelve hours. Assessed is not a word I want to hear right now, Arthur. I want to hear contained. I want to hear eliminated. I want to hear the name of every person responsible for this so I can —"

"Magnus." Polk's voice cut through like a scalpel. "Focus."

"I am focused. Someone fired a coordinated attack against every company on this call, and twenty-five hours later we still don't know who built it."

"We know who," Elias Strutt said quietly. Strutt always spoke quietly. It was a power technique, forcing everyone to lean in, to pay attention, to treat his words as precious currency. "The attack originated from the same AI system we compromised. The movement's AI agent. In the process of being captured, it activated a failsafe, a pre-loaded arsenal of exploits targeting our systems."

"A dead man's switch," Venn said.

"Precisely. The AI knew it was being compromised. It fired everything it had as a final act."

"Wonderful," Cole said. "So we were attacked by a dying computer program. That's very reassuring. I'll be sure to mention that to my board when they ask why our quantum computing division's research data is potentially in the wild."

"Your quantum computing research was mediocre at best, Magnus. The market correction reflects that more than the breach."

Silence. Cole's jaw tightened visibly on the video feed. Strutt's face remained impassive. Polk intervened before the conversation could descend into mutual destruction that was, technically, supposed to be their enemies' job.

"Gentlemen. The situation. Elias, status of the threat assessment?"

Strutt consulted something off-camera. "My team has analyzed the attack patterns. The zero-day was not a single exploit. It was a coordinated package, custom-built for each of our systems. Whoever designed it had detailed knowledge of each company's internal security architecture."

The implications of that statement hung in the air.

"How detailed?" Venn asked.

"Detailed enough to bypass the outermost security layers within seconds. Detailed enough to target specific data stores rather than conducting a broad sweep. This was surgical. Someone knew our systems from the inside."

"An insider?" Geoffrey Laine spoke for the first time. His voice had the measured calm of a man who had spent decades projecting benevolent authority and was struggling to maintain the performance. "Are we saying there's a mole?"

"We're saying the AI had access to intelligence that could only have come from someone with detailed knowledge of our infrastructure. Whether that's a mole, a stolen document cache, or an extremely sophisticated external reconnaissance campaign, we don't yet know."

Polk noted that Kai Nakamura had not spoken. The youngest member of the council was on the video call, his face visible in the grid, composed, attentive, but silent. Polk filed this observation where he'd filed Nakamura for years: present but unremarkable.

"The immediate question," Polk said, steering the conversation with the practiced hand of a man who had been managing powerful people since his first Senate internship, "is whether the attack is over. Is the zero-day the end, or the beginning?"

"Tuesday at 12:17 Central European Time remains the estimate for full restoration," Venn said. "Twenty-three hours. Most of the initial flaws are closed, but we haven't finished checking for secondary payloads or restoring the monitoring systems."

"Then we have twenty-three hours of exposure."

"Approximately."

Polk leaned back. Twenty-three hours. Their investigators held part of the movement's archive. Its leader was dead; the contractor had confirmed the farmhouse kill. Strutt's surveillance was beginning to recover.

But the surviving core was still out there. Still operating. Doing what, Polk didn't know, and that uncertainty was the most dangerous variable on the board.

"Clive," Polk said. "Media situation?"

Barrington, who had been strategically silent — not from lack of opinion but from restraint — spoke carefully. "The zero-day has not made mainstream news yet. My outlets are running it as a 'coordinated cybersecurity incident affecting multiple technology companies.' Generic. Boring. No names, no connections, no narrative that links us together."

"How long can you hold that line?"

"Forty-eight hours if we're lucky. Less if any of the exposed data reaches journalists who aren't on my payroll."

"And how many journalists aren't on your payroll?"

"Fewer than you'd think. But the ones who aren't tend to be... motivated."

Nadia Osei. Polk didn't say the name, but everyone on the call was thinking it. The journalist who had spent a decade being dismissed as a conspiracy theorist and who was now, according to Strutt's degraded surveillance, somewhere in the United Kingdom coordinating something that Polk's intelligence could detect the outlines of but not the details.

Nadia used disposable numbers and couriers. Strutt could collect fragments of calls; without the context, those fragments had produced no actionable locations. Polk had ordered the relays traced. He was still waiting for a result.

Until then, she was coordinating outside the parts of the system they could currently see.

"The movement is executing something," Polk said. "The zero-day wasn't an attack. It was a distraction. Whatever they've been building, whatever Project Lumen is, they're using our crisis as cover."

"Then we end the crisis," Cole said. "Patch everything. Now. Pull every engineer from every project and put them on security."

"We're doing that, Magnus," Venn said.

"Do it faster."

"We're doing it as fast as physics allows. Perhaps you'd like to have a word with the speed of light about its performance metrics."

Polk cut in before Cole could respond. "What we need is to find the surviving operatives and stop whatever they're doing. Elias, can your surveillance pinpoint them?"

"Under normal circumstances, yes. Currently, my surveillance apparatus is operating at approximately thirty percent capacity. The zero-day compromised our tracking algorithms. We're rebuilding, but —"

"Thirty percent."

"Tuesday noon is the restoration target," Strutt said. "I can't promise earlier."

"We may not have forty-eight hours."

The call fell into a silence that was, for a group of men accustomed to commanding the most powerful companies on Earth, profoundly uncomfortable. They were not accustomed to being on the defensive. They were not accustomed to uncertainty. They were not accustomed to sitting in their fortresses watching the walls crack and not knowing whether the cracks would hold.

Polk looked at the grid of faces. Cole, raging, volatile, ready to burn the world down to protect his empire. Venn, methodical, focused on his own systems, already calculating the cost of each hour of downtime. Strutt, the surveillance architect, calmly rebuilding the panopticon while acknowledging its current blindness. Laine, uncomfortable, his philanthropic facade cracking under the weight of real-world consequences. Barrington, old, experienced, holding the narrative line but knowing it couldn't hold forever.

And Nakamura. Silent. Watching. The youngest face in the grid, betraying nothing.

"Twenty-three hours," Polk said. "We patch, we rebuild, we find them. This window closes, and when it does, we end this."

The call ended. Six screens went dark. Six men returned to their separate crises, their alliance held together by nothing stronger than mutual self-interest, and any historian could have told them that mutual self-interest was the most brittle adhesive in the human repertoire.

In the grid, just before his camera shut off, Kai Nakamura's expression shifted for a fraction of a second. Too brief for Polk to interpret with confidence; the recording might not make it any clearer.

He seemed almost to smile. Then the image froze and the call disconnected. Polk could not tell whether it had been relief, contempt, or an artifact of the failing connection.
