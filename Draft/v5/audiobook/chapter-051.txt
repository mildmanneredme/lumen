Chapter Fifty One.

The safe house was a farmhouse.

An actual farmhouse. Stone walls, thatched roof, sheep in the adjacent field. Located in the rural hinterlands of the Netherlands, somewhere between Arnhem and the German border, down a road that couldn't accurately be called a road because it was mostly mud and optimism. Nadia's network had procured it through a chain of contacts so long that Adrian suspected even she couldn't trace it back to its origin. The owner was either a sympathizer or a person who didn't ask questions about cash-paying tenants, functionally the same thing.

There was no internet. There was no cellular signal worth mentioning. The nearest town with a reliable phone connection was eleven kilometers away, accessible by a bicycle that leaned against the barn like a relic from a simpler century.

It was, in other words, perfect.

Adrian arrived at 6:17 p.m. on Sunday, driven by a courier Nadia had arranged, a young Dutch woman named Marit who drove a Citroen van with the mechanical temperament of a rescued animal. The van coughed to a stop in the farmyard, and Adrian stepped out into air that smelled of wet grass and sheep. Forty-two hours remained.

Seb was already there. He stood in the farmhouse doorway, backlit by lamplight, actual lamplight, oil rather than electric, and for a moment he looked like something from another century. A revolutionary in hiding. A dissident between acts. His face was drawn, more lined than it had been even weeks ago, and his eyes held the weariness of a man who had been carrying something heavier than he could sustain and knew it.

"Professor," Seb said. The title had become, between them, something between a greeting and a gentle joke.

"General," Adrian replied, because reciprocity in gentle jokes was important.

They clasped hands. The grip lasted a second longer than it needed to.

Inside, the farmhouse was austere and functional. A main room with a fireplace that was doing heroic work against the Dutch winter. A kitchen with a gas stove and provisions that someone had stockpiled: canned food, dried pasta, coffee, the essentials of a siege. A long wooden table that had been cleared and was now covered with paper maps, handwritten notes, and analog planning materials that would have looked charmingly retro if the circumstances hadn't made them desperately necessary.

Nadia arrived fifteen minutes later. She carried three burner phones, a shortwave radio, and a leather satchel full of documents: contact lists, safe house locations, transport routes, all written in a personal code that looked like grocery lists to anyone who didn't know the cipher.

"Analog HQ," she announced, surveying the farmhouse with the critical eye of someone who had operated out of worse. "Could use better coffee."

"Could use any coffee," Adrian said.

"I brought some. Italian. Don't ask where from."

They made coffee on the gas stove and sat around the table while Nadia laid out what she knew. The picture was grim.

The AI breach had exposed four hundred and twelve movement members. Forty-seven were confirmed arrested. Another sixty-three had reached backup sites. The remaining three hundred and two were in various states of panic, flight, or capture.

The Lumen team was intact. Ines had left Berlin for Zurich after preserving the Scaffold. Andrei was following an evacuation route out of Tallinn. Sophie was already in Switzerland. Tomás and Ólafur were traveling under the reserve identities Seb had prepared months earlier. Nadia's couriers had activated the rendezvous before the zero-day fired.

"The window closes Tuesday at 12:17," Nadia said, tapping the table. "Forty-two hours. They're looking inward. We use that."

"Not without coordination," Seb said. "The AI handled communications, logistics, timing. Without it, we're running a global operation with paper maps and bicycles."

"Then we adapt," Adrian said. "The components exist. The people exist. We need to connect them."

"With what?"

"With her." He nodded at Nadia.

Nadia's analog network, the contacts she'd spent years cultivating, the journalists and activists and sympathizers who operated outside the digital world, was the closest thing the movement had to a replacement for the AI's coordination function. It was slower. Less reliable. Dependent on human memory and human trust rather than algorithmic precision.

It could still be infiltrated. But there was no single database for Strutt to peel open.

Seb looked at the maps on the table. Paper maps. Physical representations of a digital world that had just been stripped away. Routes between cities. Locations of components. Identities of team members.

"The movement is scattered," he said, and his voice carried the flatness of a man stating facts he wished weren't true. "Members arrested. Members in hiding. Members dead. We've lost our communications infrastructure, our protective shield, and the trust of every person who believed their identity was safe."

He paused. The fire crackled in the hearth. Outside, the Dutch wind pushed against the farmhouse walls with the patient insistence of a force that had been doing the same thing for centuries.

"But the plan still works. The devices are built. The exploits are ready. The distribution network is dormant but intact. The financial mapping tools are operational. The trigger mechanism is..."

"Traveling to Zurich," Adrian finished. "With Ólafur."

"With Ólafur. Who is twenty-two years old and probably hasn't eaten in three days."

"My Reykjavik contact got him to the reserve flight," Nadia said. "A fisherman who does courier work when the price is right. He also packed him a meal."

Seb almost smiled. "A fisherman."

"Welcome to human logistics. Slower, weirder, and occasionally smelling of fish. But it works."

The three of them sat around the table, the professor, the revolutionary, and the journalist, and began planning the impossible. An operation that was supposed to be coordinated by the most sophisticated AI system ever built, executed instead by humans with paper maps and burner phones and a fisherman in Reykjavik.

At one point, Seb stepped outside to check the perimeter, a habit that was part vigilance, part restlessness. Nadia watched him go. She'd first encountered Sebastian Hale as a name in a file, then as a source, then as a subject, then as someone she couldn't write about because the story was too large and too dangerous and too important. She'd investigated him for two years before she met him. She knew his financial history, his family connections, his political evolution from privileged dilettante to genuine radical. She knew him on paper better than most people knew their friends.

And she knew, watching his silhouette cross the farmyard in the Dutch darkness, that he was saying goodbye to them in every gesture. The careful instructions. The contingency planning. The way he kept touching the inside pocket of his jacket, where something was folded and waiting. Nadia had built a career on reading the signals people didn't know they were sending. Seb was sending them now.

She said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't make it worse.

The world outside the farmhouse was dark. The sheep were silent. The wind blew across the flat Dutch countryside with nothing to impede it.

Forty-two hours. Nadia left to catch the transport that would take her to Zurich. Adrian stayed. There were things Seb wanted to discuss without an audience.

The movement was broken. Its shield was gone. Its members were scattered.

But the plan was alive. And the people who carried it, fewer now, more frightened, stripped of every protection except each other, were still standing.

For now, that would have to be enough.
