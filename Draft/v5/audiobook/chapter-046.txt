Chapter Forty Six.

Elias Strutt had not slept in thirty-one hours, and he was in the best mood anyone on the council had ever seen him in. For Strutt, this manifested as a slight upward tilt at the corners of his mouth and a marginally increased willingness to make eye contact.

"We found it," he said.

The council was assembled in Strutt's own territory this time, a conference room in the basement of his company's Menlo Park campus that, despite being underground and windowless, managed to convey the impression of infinite space through the use of screens, clean lines, and minimalist design that cost a fortune to look like it cost nothing. The room was swept for surveillance devices every four hours. The walls were lined with a Faraday mesh that blocked all wireless signals. Inside this room, the outside world ceased to exist.

Kai Nakamura had been the first to arrive. This was unusual enough that Polk had noted it, filing the observation in the mental dossier he maintained on each council member. Kai was typically last, or close to it, projecting the casual indifference of a man who had more important things to do. Tonight he'd been seated when Polk walked in, phone face-down on the table, hands still, eyes already on the door. Waiting.

"Found what, exactly?" Polk asked, though the answer was evident from Strutt's unprecedented display of near-emotion.

"Their AI." Strutt placed his tablet flat on the table and slid it to the center. A visualization appeared on the wall-mounted display: a web of interconnected nodes, pulsing with data, overlaid on a world map. "It took six weeks and forty-three of my best engineers, but we've identified the architecture. Two hundred and fourteen relay nodes across forty-one countries. The core processing appears to be located in a geothermal facility in Iceland. The system uses rotating encryption and adaptive counter-surveillance. Its trigger network uses a Byzantine fault-tolerance consensus mechanism that I have to admit is..." He paused, and the near-smile flickered. "Impressive."

"Can you break it?" Venn asked.

"I can do better than break it. I can turn it inside out."

The room sharpened. Even Cole, who had been lounging in his chair with the studied boredom of a man who considered most human activities beneath him, leaned forward.

"The system's strength is also its vulnerability," Strutt continued. "It's designed to be a shield, intercepting our surveillance, misdirecting our monitoring, encrypting the movement's communications. But shields are two-way. The same network connections that allow it to protect the movement's data also give us a pathway into the movement's data. If we breach the AI, we don't just disable their protection. We read everything it was protecting."

"Names," Polk said.

"Names. Locations. Communications. Operational plans. Financial records. Everything the AI has been managing on the movement's behalf becomes visible to us the moment we crack the encryption layer."

"How long?" Barrington asked.

Strutt's near-smile solidified into something that, on anyone else's face, would have been called satisfaction. On Strutt's face, it looked like a thermostat reaching its target temperature. "My team has been developing the breach toolset for the past two weeks. We've mapped the encryption rotation pattern. We've identified three vulnerability windows, brief moments when the system cycles its keys and the encryption strength momentarily drops. The next window opens in approximately seventy-two hours."

Silence. The kind that descends on a room when the people in it realize that the thing they've been fighting for is suddenly within reach.

Laine spoke first. "What are the risks?"

"Of the breach? Minimal. The toolset is designed to penetrate the encryption layer without triggering the system's intrusion detection. By the time the AI realizes it's been compromised, we'll already have full read access. After that, it's a matter of extracting the data before anyone on their end can scrub it."

"And what happens to the movement?"

Strutt looked at Laine with the flat incomprehension of a man who had been asked why water was wet. "They lose. Their identities are exposed. Their communications are readable. Their operational plans are visible. Without the AI's protection, they're exactly what they were before they had it: a collection of individuals with names and addresses and vulnerabilities."

Kai had not spoken since the briefing began. His face showed the measured attention of a man following a technical briefing: interested, engaged, unremarkable. His right hand rested flat on the table. His left hand, beneath the table, was gripping his own knee hard enough to leave marks.

"One question," Kai said, and his voice was sharper than usual, precise enough that Polk glanced at him. "When you breach the encryption layer, what happens to the AI itself? Does it shut down? Does it fragment? Or does it have defensive protocols that activate under compromise?"

Strutt considered. "Any well-designed system would have contingency responses. The AI will likely attempt countermeasures: data fragmentation, signal jamming, emergency shutdown of compromised nodes. We've accounted for this."

"You've accounted for defensive countermeasures," Kai said. "I'm asking about offensive ones. What if the breach triggers something we haven't seen? Something pre-positioned?"

It was a pointed question. Too pointed, perhaps, but Kai delivered it with the slightly detached curiosity of a tech CEO who thought in systems architecture. Strutt dismissed it with a fractional shake of his head.

"The AI is a shield, not a weapon. We've seen no evidence of offensive capability."

Kai nodded slowly. He asked no follow-up question. Polk watched him turn his phone face-down again and wondered whether the youngest member was protecting his own systems or simply enjoying the prospect of watching Strutt's confidence tested.

"Seventy-two hours," Polk said, testing the words. "What do we need?"

"Processing power. Venn's cloud infrastructure would be ideal. The breach toolset requires computational resources beyond what my own systems can provide in the window."

All eyes turned to Venn. The Machine considered for precisely two seconds. "Done. I'll allocate a reserved cluster. Untraceable to the primary business."

"Good. And I'll need Cole's platform for the second phase."

Cole raised an eyebrow. "My platform?"

"Once we extract the identities, we need to move fast. Arrests, yes, Polk can coordinate with his government contacts. But we also need to destroy public sympathy. I want the names, the faces, and the evidence of criminal activity flooding every channel simultaneously. Your platform reaches two billion people. Barrington's outlets reach another billion. Between the two of you, we can ensure that by the time the movement realizes they're exposed, the public already sees them as criminals."

"I like it," Cole said, and he meant it. The prospect of spectacle always engaged him more than strategy. "A digital perp walk. Very twenty-first century."

"This is not a game, Magnus," Laine said.

"Everything's a game, Geoffrey. The question is whether you're playing to win or playing to feel good about losing."

"Gentlemen." Polk's voice carried the weight of a man who had been managing the distance between egos and outcomes for decades. "We have seventy-two hours. Let's discuss the operational sequence."

They discussed it. Strutt outlined the technical plan with the precision of a surgeon describing an operation. Venn committed his computational resources. Barrington mapped the media rollout. Cole offered his platform. Laine nodded, his moral hesitations neatly folded and stored somewhere out of sight. Polk coordinated, organized, kept the machine running.

Kai participated. He asked two technical questions, both reasonable, both informed, both designed to clarify operational details. He voted in favor of the plan when Polk called for consensus. He offered to ensure his platform's algorithm would amplify the content Strutt and Barrington prepared.

He did everything that was expected of the youngest, least threatening member of the council.

And beneath the table, his left hand maintained its grip on his knee, because it was the only thing preventing the rest of his body from betraying what his mind was screaming.

Seventy-two hours.

Kai looked at the map again. He asked which nodes would be attacked first, then what safeguards would protect client infrastructure if the AI counterattacked. Both questions were entered into the operational notes. Neither changed the decision.

When the meeting ended, he remained seated until Strutt had taken the tablet away. Polk noticed the red marks on his left hand where he had gripped his knee.

"Something worrying you?"

"The assumption that we know everything it can do," Kai said.

Then he stood, straightened his jacket, and left.

Polk looked at the empty chair. Caution, he decided. Kai had always disliked committing before every variable was known. In seventy-two hours, there would be fewer variables.
