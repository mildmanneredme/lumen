Chapter Eighteen.

The preliminary dossiers arrived in a batch of four, which was unusual. They were requests for assessment, not yet permission to proceed.

Raven operated on a principle of sequential engagement: one problem at a time, each one fully resolved before the next was opened. Parallel work introduced variables. Variables introduced errors. Errors were, in her line of work, non-recoverable.

But the client, the same anonymous intermediary who had commissioned the Yoon job, had transmitted the four dossiers simultaneously with a priority flag that, in the coded vocabulary of their business relationship, meant these are connected and time-sensitive, and the usual protocols are suspended. Raven had received a priority flag exactly once before, three years ago, for a job in Zurich that had required her to work through a deviation that left her unable to sleep properly for six weeks. She did not enjoy priority flags.

She was back in her Bermondsey flat, at her desk, with the tea she reserved for moments requiring sustained cognitive effort: Pu-erh, aged twelve years, brewed at 95 degrees Celsius for exactly four minutes. The flat hummed at its steady 50 hertz. The bookshelves were alphabetized. The tools were locked. Everything was in order, which meant her mind could be in order, which meant she could process what was in front of her.

Four targets. Four movement members, identified by the client's intelligence as part of the network Yoon had belonged to. The dossiers were thinner than Yoon's — these people had been harder to identify, their digital footprints lighter, their operational security superior to the dead man's. But the client's surveillance apparatus had apparently been upgraded since the Yoon job, and the information provided was sufficient: names, photographs, locations, daily routines, known associates.

Raven studied each dossier with the focused attention she brought to any new puzzle. Each target was unique. Different cities. Different security postures. Different levels of vulnerability. This was the part she loved — and the word was not too strong, it was the most accurate descriptor available to her emotional vocabulary — the analytical phase where the constraints of each problem revealed the contours of its solution.

Target One was in Portland, Oregon. A software engineer identified through metadata analysis — not his communications, which were encrypted, but the patterns of his communications: timing, frequency, the digital fingerprint that every device leaves regardless of the encryption protecting its contents. He lived alone, worked from home, and maintained a daily routine that included a 5:45 a.m. run in Forest Park. A jogger on a dark trail in November. The simplest of the four.

Target Two was in Chicago. A woman, mid-thirties, who worked in data analysis for a nonprofit and who, according to the dossier, served as a communication relay for multiple cells in the Midwest. She lived in a shared apartment in Logan Square, which meant witnesses, roommates, the complications of a life lived in proximity to other lives. Harder.

Target Three was in Denver. A former military signals intelligence specialist — a target Raven approached with particular caution, because people trained in electronic surveillance were also trained to detect it. He would be operationally aware. He would notice anomalies. He would, in all probability, be armed.

Target Four was in Austin, Texas. The dossier on this one was the thinnest — a name, a photograph, a general location. The client's intelligence suggested this person was a recent identification, not yet fully profiled. Incomplete information was, from Raven's perspective, the most dangerous variable of all. You could plan around known obstacles. Unknown obstacles killed you.

She arranged the dossiers on her desk in order of difficulty — Portland, Chicago, Austin, Denver. Four constrained problems. She would build each solution separately, then test the schedule against the time and resources they would share.

Portland first. The jogger. She'd been to Portland twice before — once for work, once because she'd read about a bookstore that organized its inventory by emotional resonance rather than genre, which had sounded like either a revelation or an abomination and turned out to be both. Forest Park offered miles of trails and many ways for a runner to disappear from sight.

The dossier was not enough. She needed to walk his route, see the ground, and discover which details the client's surveillance had missed. Until then, any plan would be provisional.

She made no written notes. The route and its unanswered questions settled into the filing system she carried in her head.

Chicago next. The relay in the shared apartment. Harder — the proximity of roommates, the urban density, the need for a method that could function within the constraints of a residential building without alerting the other occupants. She pulled up building codes for Logan Square residential units, researched the target's daily schedule as outlined in the dossier, and began constructing a different approach. Not an outdoor accident. Something domestic. Something that belonged in the life the target already lived.

She worked for three hours. The Pu-erh cooled and was replaced by a second pot. The 50-hertz hum continued its steady hold on the auditory baseline of her consciousness. The four assessments took shape, each distinct, each tailored, each reflecting the specific environment and vulnerability of its subject.

But something was different this time.

Raven registered the difference as a data point, a variation from the established pattern that registered on her internal monitoring system with the same dispassionate clarity as a temperature change or a shift in ambient sound. The difference was not in the work — the work was, as always, precise, creative, and satisfying in its intellectual demands. The difference was in the context.

Four targets. Simultaneously. Connected to the same network. The client's priority flag. The upgraded intelligence. The accelerated timeline.

The client was escalating.

The Yoon job had been a single thread: cut one connection, prevent one disclosure. These four jobs were a campaign. Someone had identified multiple nodes of the network and was moving to eliminate them in parallel — not a surgical intervention but a suppression operation. What intelligence agencies did when they decided an enemy network had become an existential threat.

Raven had learned to keep her clients' motives outside the work. She called this discipline. Her last therapist had called it avoidance, and Raven had not returned for another appointment. The work interested her; that much was true. Whether interest explained the choices she had made was a question she preferred not to test.

But she noticed the escalation. And she noticed something else: the operational tempo the client was demanding left less room for the meticulous preparation that was the foundation of her craft. Four targets in four cities in three weeks. The first job, Portland, was achievable within her standards. Chicago, possibly. Austin, with its incomplete intelligence, was risky. Denver, with a former signals intelligence specialist, was a job that normally took her six to eight weeks of preparation, not the ten days the timeline allowed.

The client was pushing her toward speed. Speed was the enemy of precision. Precision was the only thing that kept her alive and free.

She thought about this. Not emotionally — Raven did not think emotionally about professional decisions — but systematically. She ran the calculations: risk versus reward, timeline versus quality, the probability of clean execution in each city given the constraints. The numbers were acceptable for Portland and Chicago. They were marginal for Austin. They were uncomfortable for Denver.

Uncomfortable was not a word she used lightly.

She sent her response to the intermediary: Assessment accepted. Portland and Chicago feasible on proposed timeline. Austin requires additional intelligence before commitment. Denver requires extended timeline — minimum four additional weeks. Awaiting final authorizations.

The response came within the hour, faster than usual: Austin intelligence in progress. Denver timeline non-negotiable. Final authorizations to follow. The network is adapting. Speed is critical.

Raven read the message twice. The network is adapting. This was the first time the client had provided operational context beyond the immediate target parameters. It meant the targets were becoming harder, not because of anything Raven had done, but because the network itself was changing. Learning. Increasing security. Going dark.

She was being asked to hunt prey that was learning to hide.

This was, she reflected, the first interesting thing about the batch. The Yoon job had been elegant but unchallenging — a static target in a fixed location with known vulnerabilities. These four were different. The network behind them was alive, responsive, evolving in real time. Her targets would be alert. They would be looking over their shoulders. They would be taking precautions, changing routines, communicating less, moving differently.

The puzzle had become dynamic.

Raven finished her tea, closed the dossiers, and began preparing for Portland. She would leave when the client confirmed the target's next movement. She would bring a different set of tools than the Yoon job — lighter, more versatile, suited to outdoor work. She would study Forest Park with the attention she gave to every operational environment: studying the route itself rather than trusting the summary in the dossier.

The movement was going on alert. Its members were becoming harder to reach.

Good.

She preferred the difficult puzzles. The easy ones made her restless, and restlessness, for a mind wired like hers, was the most dangerous state of all.

She packed her bag. She booked a flight. She selected an identity — Claire Whitfield, Canadian, visiting Portland for a sustainable agriculture conference that was actually happening on the dates she'd be there. The identity was three years old, well-established, and had a social media footprint that included, appropriately, an interest in trail running.

Raven smiled her small, private smile — the circuit completing, the connection made — and went to work.

Somewhere in Portland, Oregon, a software engineer who had chosen to resist the most powerful corporations in the world was lacing up his running shoes in the pre-dawn dark, unaware that the trail he ran every morning had just become the most carefully studied three miles of pavement in the Pacific Northwest.

The dossier said he had nearly two weeks before his next trip. Raven was already asking the client to confirm that date.
