Chapter Twenty Eight.

Nadia Osei did not believe in digital security. She believed in people who owed her favors.

Earlier that Saturday, before meeting Marsh in the park, she had sat at her kitchen table with a cup of black coffee, a prepaid phone she'd bought at a bodega in the Tenderloin, and a paper notebook. Not a laptop. Not a tablet. Paper. The notebook had a black cover, college-ruled pages, and no connection to any network on Earth. It was, in Nadia's professional assessment, the most secure communication device money could buy.

She opened it to a page labeled INFRASTRUCTURE and began making calls.

Helena first. Brussels. Helena Voss ran a print shop on Rue du Marche au Charbon that produced, among other things, the finest forged identity documents in Western Europe. She'd been doing it for thirty years, first for refugees fleeing the Balkan wars, then for journalists operating in hostile territory, now for anyone who needed to become someone else for reasons Helena considered morally sufficient. Her standards were exacting. Her prices were fair. Her documents had never been detected.

Nadia dialed the Brussels number from memory. Helena picked up on the fourth ring.

"Print shop."

"Helena. It's Nadia."

A pause. The sound of a cigarette being lit. "I was wondering when you'd call."

"I need packages. Multiple. Clean, EU-standard, biometric-capable. How fast?"

"How many?"

"Start with six. I'll need more later."

"Six takes two weeks if I rush. Three if I do it right."

"Do it right. I'll send specifications through the usual channel."

"The usual channel" was a physical letter, handwritten, mailed to a PO box in Ghent that Helena checked every Tuesday. No email. No messaging apps. No digital trail. Nadia had learned this protocol from Helena herself, seven years ago, when she'd needed emergency documentation for a source fleeing a cartel in Honduras. Helena had delivered in four days. The documents were flawless. The source was alive and living under a new name in Rotterdam.

Nadia made a note in her notebook. Helena — 6 packages, 3 weeks. Specs by Tuesday.

Yannis next. The island of Hydra, a rocky jewel in the Saronic Gulf where no cars were allowed and the only transport was by boat, donkey, or foot. Yannis Katsaros ran a charter fishing operation that also, depending on the season and the client, moved people and cargo along routes the Greek coast guard had never mapped. He'd been doing it since the migrant crisis, when he'd converted his fishing fleet into an informal rescue and transport operation. The crisis had faded from the headlines, but Yannis's routes remained, quiet channels connecting the Greek islands to the Turkish coast, to Crete, to the Italian port towns where certain harbormasters asked no questions.

"Yanni. Nadia."

"Nadia!" His voice was warm, booming, the voice of a man who lived outdoors. "You need a boat?"

"I need options. Safe transport, Mediterranean, for people who can't fly. Multiple routes. How current are your channels?"

"Current as the tide. I ran three last month. Lesbos to Bari. Rhodes to Antalya. Hydra to Brindisi. Clean, every one."

"I'll need confirmed routes with timing windows. And Yanni — this isn't humanitarian. The people I'm moving will have heat on them. Professional heat."

A pause. Longer this time. "How professional?"

"Corporate. Possibly government-adjacent. Surveillance-capable but not military."

"Then we use the night routes. More expensive. No lights, no electronics, GPS dark. I have two captains who can do it. Maybe three."

"Send me the details. Paper. Post office in Piraeus, the one near the ferry terminal."

Yannis — Mediterranean routes, night-capable. Specs via Piraeus PO.

Farhan was more complicated. Farhan Al-Rashid operated a hawala network — the ancient, trust-based money transfer system that predated Western banking by centuries. Money went in at one end, in any currency, and came out at the other end, in any currency, with no wire transfer, no bank record, no digital trace. The system ran on honor, reputation, and a web of personal relationships spanning from Karachi to London to Nairobi to Los Angeles. Farhan's particular network was small, discreet, and had never once been compromised, because Farhan chose his brokers the way a jeweler chooses stones: one at a time, under magnification, with an eye for flaws invisible to anyone less careful.

Nadia couldn't call Farhan directly. She called a restaurant in Dearborn, Michigan, asked for the Tuesday special, and left a callback number. Farhan would return the call from a different phone within four hours. The protocol was elaborate, but Farhan had survived twenty-three years in a business where a single intercepted communication could mean prison or death. His paranoia was operational, and Nadia respected it.

Farhan — hawala network activation. Callback pending.

Farhan returned her call before she finished the coffee. She explained the need without giving names. He confirmed two brokers and asked for the authentication phrase they had agreed years earlier. She supplied it, crossed out pending, and put the phone aside.

The last call was the most delicate. She dialed a landline in Devon, England. It rang six times. A woman answered, sounding breathless.

"Hello?"

"Mrs. Whitfield, it's Nadia Osei. We met at the press freedom dinner in London. 2019. You were wearing a blue dress and your husband told me a story about a dead drop in a Budapest phone booth."

Silence. Then: "I was wearing green. And it was a post office, not a phone booth."

"My apologies. I've been thinking about that story. I could use some advice on... postal matters."

Another silence, longer. Margaret Whitfield, nee Hargreaves, had spent twenty-seven years in the Secret Intelligence Service before retiring to breed Welsh corgis in the Devon countryside. Her husband, Gerald, had done thirty-one years. Between them, they possessed an encyclopedic knowledge of tradecraft, authentication protocols, secure communication methods, and the operational habits of every Western intelligence agency. They also possessed, in Nadia's experience, a deep institutional anger at what those agencies had become and a willingness to share their expertise with journalists they considered serious.

"I think we should have tea," Margaret said. "In person. Not at the house. The cafe in Totnes, the one with the terrible scones. Thursday?"

"Thursday works."

"Bring your questions, dear. And don't write anything down until after we've talked. Gerald has been restless. He'll want to help."

Whitfields — Thursday, Totnes. Authentication protocols, tradecraft consultation.

Nadia closed the notebook and looked at her kitchen wall. Four calls. Four nodes in a network that existed entirely in the space between human beings: in debts owed, trust earned, favors banked over years of work that nobody would ever write about because the whole point was that nobody knew.

Helena for documents. Yannis for transport. Farhan for money. The Whitfields for tradecraft.

It wasn't much. It was also, Nadia knew, more than most intelligence agencies could assemble on short notice, because intelligence agencies operated through bureaucracies and bureaucracies left traces and traces were exactly what the people hunting this movement were following.

Her network left no traces. It ran on paper, phone calls, and the accumulated capital of a decade spent being the journalist who showed up, who protected her sources, who never burned a contact, who paid her debts. That capital was, at this moment, the most valuable asset the movement didn't know it had.

She finished her coffee. It was cold. She didn't care.

There was more to build. Safe houses to identify. Journalists to assess — which ones she trusted, which ones had been compromised, which ones would run the story when the time came. Counter-narrative infrastructure for when the other side started framing the movement as terrorists. She'd seen the Barrington media machine warm up before, and she knew what it could do.

But that was tomorrow's work. Today was about the skeleton: the bones of a network that could keep people alive and moving when the digital world turned hostile.

Nadia washed her coffee cup, dried it, and set it on the counter. Then she picked up the prepaid phone and broke it in half. She'd buy a new one tomorrow. Different bodega. Different number. Same protocol.

The wall behind her desk stared back with its web of red, blue, green, yellow, and black threads. She'd added a new color this morning: white. White for the network she was building. White for the infrastructure that would hold when everything else failed.

It was invisible. It was analog. It was hers.
