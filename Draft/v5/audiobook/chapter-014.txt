Chapter Fourteen.

The call came on Morrow's personal line, which was unusual. The personal line was for his ex-wife, his daughter, and the plumber who was supposed to have fixed the kitchen faucet three weeks ago. It was not for anonymous informants who spoke with the measured diction of people accustomed to being obeyed.

"Detective Morrow. I have information regarding your investigation into the NovaMind breach."

Morrow was at his desk in the Hall of Justice at 850 Bryant Street, the same building where Daniel Yoon had died a little over two weeks ago. That fact made working there feel like conducting an autopsy in the room where the patient was killed. Across the aisle, Santos was doing paperwork — or pretending to, which was his way of being available without being intrusive. Morrow leaned back in his chair and reached for a pen, not because he planned to write anything down, but because holding a pen helped him think. Old habit.

"Who is this?"

"A concerned party. I'm going to share something with you, and then you're going to decide what to do with it. I'm not asking for anything in return, and I won't contact you again through this channel."

Morrow had been a detective long enough to know that unsolicited tips came in two varieties: genuine and planted. The genuine ones were usually messy — incoherent, emotional, motivated by anger or guilt or the impulse that made people pick up the phone at two in the morning because they couldn't live with what they knew. The planted ones were clean. Too clean. Information packaged and delivered with the care of someone who had thought carefully about what a detective would need to see in order to reach a specific conclusion.

This caller was clean.

"Go ahead," Morrow said.

"Your consultant. Professor Adrian Marsh. You brought him in to analyze the NovaMind systems. He's been helpful, I imagine."

"I can't discuss—"

"He attended a public event at a community center in the Mission District eleven days ago. A forum on digital rights. While there, he was observed in conversation with a young woman who is a known associate of an underground activist network operating in the Bay Area. The same network that Daniel Yoon was connected to before his arrest."

Morrow stopped reaching for the pen. He was now fully still, the way he got when a case that had been sitting at the bottom of a lake suddenly broke the surface.

"How do you know this?"

"The how is not your concern. What should concern you is the what. Marsh's involvement with your investigation gave him access to classified technical data about NovaMind's internal systems. He has been meeting with Nadia Osei, a journalist with a documented pattern of publishing allegations against major technology companies. And he is now making contact with individuals connected to the same underground network your suspect was part of."

"You're suggesting he's compromised."

"I'm suggesting he may not be the neutral academic consultant you believe him to be. The evidence will be delivered to your office by courier within the hour. Photographs. Communication records. Location data. I encourage you to draw your own conclusions."

The line went dead.

Morrow sat at his desk for a long moment, staring at the phone. Then he set it down, picked up his pen, and began writing — not notes about the call, but a list of questions. Questions he'd been asking himself since this case started and hadn't been able to answer.

Why was Yoon silent?

Who killed Yoon?

How did the killer access the jail?

Why is the NovaMind CSO so cooperative and so unhelpful at the same time?

Why does Marsh's name keep appearing?

And now, a new question:

Who has the resources to surveil a university professor, document his movements, and deliver evidence to a police detective through an anonymous phone call and a courier package?

The answer to the last question was the one that bothered Morrow most. Because the list of entities capable of that level of surveillance and operational coordination was short, and none of them were entities that typically called San Francisco police detectives with helpful tips.

Government intelligence agencies could do it. But they had their own channels — they'd go through the FBI, not SFPD.

Large technology companies could do it. They had the data, the infrastructure, the security teams.

And that was the thought that stuck. Because if a technology company was feeding him evidence about Adrian Marsh — evidence designed to connect Marsh to the underground network — then the question was not whether the evidence was accurate.

The question was why they wanted him looking at Marsh.

The courier package arrived at 11:47 a.m. A manila envelope, no return address, delivered by a commercial courier service. Inside: eight photographs, high resolution, timestamped. Adrian Marsh entering the community center in the Mission. Marsh in conversation with a young woman. Marsh's car, parked near an industrial park in Hunters Point. Marsh entering a warehouse. Marsh leaving the warehouse forty-three minutes later.

The photographs were surveillance quality — long lens, professional composition, the work of someone who did this for a living. Alongside the photographs: a printed summary of Marsh's recent movements, his meeting with Nadia Osei at Artis Coffee, and a partial transcript of a phone call between Marsh and an unknown party that referenced "the network" and "encrypted channels."

Morrow spread the materials on his desk and studied them with the attention of a man who made his living separating the true from the merely convincing.

The evidence was good. Very good. Good enough to justify bringing Marsh in for formal questioning. Good enough to reclassify him from consultant to person of interest. Good enough, if Morrow wanted to go there, to get a warrant.

It was also too good.

In fifteen years of detective work, Morrow had learned that evidence came in natural packages — messy, incomplete, requiring interpretation. This package was curated. Each piece fit the next with the precision of a jigsaw puzzle assembled by someone who'd seen the picture on the box. No loose threads. No ambiguities. No dead ends. Just a clean, linear narrative: Marsh was connected to the underground network. Marsh was compromised. Marsh was a threat.

Morrow had been a good detective for a long time, and good detectives develop an instinct for the difference between evidence that emerges from investigation and evidence that is delivered on a platter. The first kind you trust. The second kind you question.

He picked up his phone and called Marsh.

"Professor. We need to talk."

"About what?"

"About where you were last Thursday night."

A silence. Not the silence of guilt — he'd heard that thousands of times, and it had a different texture. This was the silence of a man calculating.

"I was in Hunters Point. Before that, the previous Thursday, I was at a public event in the Mission. A digital rights forum."

"Who did you meet there?"

Another silence. Longer.

"I think we should have this conversation in person, Detective."

"I think so too. Tomorrow morning. My office."

Morrow hung up and looked at the photographs spread across his desk. Adrian Marsh, entering a warehouse in Hunters Point. Meeting people connected to an underground network. Following a trail that the detective now suspected was being lit for him by someone who wanted him found.

The question was: who wanted him found, and why?

Morrow picked up the manila envelope and turned it over. No markings. Commercial courier. Untraceable in any practical sense.

He thought about the anonymous caller's voice. The measured diction. The operational precision. The clean delivery. Everything about the call and the package said resources. Power. Capability at a level that dwarfed anything in Morrow's jurisdiction.

Someone very powerful wanted him looking at Adrian Marsh.

And James Morrow, who had built a career on following evidence wherever it led, was beginning to wonder whether the evidence was leading him — or whether it was leading him astray.
