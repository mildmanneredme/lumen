Chapter Three.

"The first generative model that attracted mainstream attention," Adrian said, advancing the slide, "was not, as most people assume, a large language model. It was a generative adversarial network — a GAN — developed for image synthesis in 2014. The fundamental architecture involved two neural networks in competition: one generating images, the other trying to detect which images were fake. Can anyone tell me why this adversarial structure matters?"

Silence. Forty-three undergraduates in Introduction to AI Systems, a Tuesday afternoon section that met in Wheeler Hall's tiered lecture room, and not one of them was thinking about generative adversarial networks. Adrian could see it in the way they sat — leaning forward instead of back, phones in laps instead of bags, the electric, barely contained energy of people who wanted to talk about something other than what they were being taught.

A hand went up. Third row, left side. A sophomore named Priti who usually asked good questions.

"Professor Marsh — is it true the suspect in the NovaMind case was a student here?"

Adrian paused. The name had been released that morning. Daniel Yoon. He searched his memory and found a dim image: a quiet kid in one of his sections two years ago, maybe three. Sat near the back. Submitted his assignments on time. Asked a sharp question once about the ethical implications of recommendation algorithms that Adrian had used in a subsequent lecture without attribution. Bright. Nothing remarkable beyond that.

Adrian set the clicker down on the podium. He could feel the room tilt toward him, forty-three pairs of eyes waiting to see how he'd handle this.

"It's a fair question, Priti," he said. "But this isn't the place for it. If you want to discuss it, come to office hours. Right now we've got GANs to get through. Slide fourteen."

A murmur of disappointment. Another hand.

"But did you know him? Daniel Yoon? They said he had copies of your papers—"

The room went still. Adrian looked at the student who'd asked — a junior he didn't recognise — and then at the podium, where his lecture notes sat in their neat stack.

"I did," he said, quieter now. "He was in one of my sections a couple of years back. Bright kid. Quiet. Asked good questions." He stopped. The image surfaced unbidden — Yoon sitting near the back of a room not unlike this one, hunched over his laptop, always a little apart from the others. What had happened between that student and the one on the news? What turns a sharp, quiet kid into someone who breaks into one of the most powerful companies on the planet?

The silence stretched. Nobody moved.

"Right," Adrian said, blinking himself back. "Let's get back on track. Generative adversarial networks. The discriminator function."

He advanced to slide fifteen. The room settled, reluctantly, into the shape of an actual lecture. Adrian talked about discriminators and generators and the mathematical elegance of adversarial training, and he was good at it — he could feel the room begin to engage despite itself, drawn in by the architecture of his argument as he hit his stride.

Then he saw the two men standing at the glass panel beside the lecture hall door.

One was mid-forties, medium height, dressed in a suit that communicated law enforcement as unmistakably as a white coat. He'd actually chosen his tie that morning. It was burgundy. The other was younger, bigger, standing slightly behind — arms crossed, face blank, the posture of someone whose job was to watch.

Adrian looked at the clock. Twenty minutes left in the session.

"Well," he said, "fair to say we're getting a bit distracted today, so let's wrap it up. I want you to read through the rest of the deck before Thursday. And remember..."

"All the semester's decks are available online," the class droned in unison, already closing laptops and shoving notebooks into bags.

"Doesn't hurt to look ahead every now and again," Adrian said, a flicker of his teacher persona surfacing through whatever else was pulling at his attention.

Adrian gathered his notes, powered down the projector, and walked to the door.

The man in the burgundy tie was waiting in the corridor. Up close, he was well dressed in a way that felt anachronistic — pressed suit, polished shoes, an attention to appearance that most detectives had abandoned a generation ago. The last of the old guard. Not the FBI agents Adrian had met at conferences, who tended toward the aggressively nondescript. This man had standards. Adrian appreciated the effort.

"Professor Marsh? I'm Detective James Morrow, San Francisco Police." He held up a badge wallet. "This is my colleague, Detective Santos." The younger man nodded once, said nothing. "Is there somewhere we could talk?"

Adrian nodded obligingly and gestured to his office down the hall. The institutional reflexes of an academic who had spent twenty years in a system built on the assumption that cooperation was a virtue kicked in before his survival instincts could mount a counterargument.

"So how can I help you gentlemen, interested in taking a course on AI?" he joked, trying to cut through the atmosphere. Both detectives snickered and walked in after Adrian. Santos took a position near the wall, standing. Adrian moved behind his desk and sat — not a conscious decision, but the desk was wide and solid and it put something between him and the room.

Morrow entered the office and performed the subtle environmental scan that was the law enforcement equivalent of a professor surveying a student's bookshelf: the rapid, trained eye cataloging the room for information about its occupant. Adrian watched him note the bookshelf — three shelves of AI and machine learning texts, two shelves of philosophy, one shelf that was entirely Ursula K. Le Guin, which usually prompted questions from visitors but apparently did not register as evidence. Morrow settled into the chair across Adrian's desk. The desk was organized in the manner of a man who believed in organization but had been gradually losing the war against entropy: neat stacks of papers that had begun to lean, a coffee mug holding pens that should have been in a drawer, and a framed photograph of Adrian at a conference seven years earlier, shaking hands with someone the detective would not recognize but who was, in fact, the person Adrian tried hardest not to think about.

Kai Nakamura. Twenty-nine years old in that photograph. Already a billionaire. Not yet a stranger.

Slowly, Morrow extracted a notepad from his coat chest pocket, clicked a pen, and without raising his eyes, uttered his first words. "You've seen the news, I assume."

"My mother called me," Adrian said. "She treats CNN as a religious obligation. She wanted to know if I was going to be arrested."

"Are you?"

"I told her no. She seemed unconvinced. She's been unconvinced about most of my life choices since I left pre-med."

A small smile from Morrow. Good. Adrian used humor the way other people used locked doors — to control the pace of an interaction and keep visitors at a comfortable distance.

"Professor Marsh, I'm going to be direct with you, because I think you'd prefer that to the alternative."

"The alternative being?"

"Twenty minutes of small talk about your research before I get to the point. I've read enough about you to know you don't like wasting time."

"You've read about me."

"I've read your papers. Well — my technical people read your papers and then explained them to me in English. You've written compulsively about AI systems like the ones at NovaMind. Behavioral prediction models. The architecture of control — I think that's a phrase you coined?"

"It is," Adrian replied, something tightening in his chest at the pointed reference. His own words, quoted back at him by a detective in a burgundy tie.

"The suspect we arrested yesterday, the one who was all over the news, he had your work. Not just citations. Annotated copies. Marginal notes. He'd been studying your frameworks, working through the weaknesses you described."

Morrow let that settle. Then, quieter: "He hasn't said a word since his arrest. Not to us, not to his public defender, not to anyone. Whatever he was doing inside NovaMind's systems, he'd rather face federal prison than explain it."

The pause that followed was different from the ones before — heavier, more deliberate. The warmth drained from Morrow's voice like water from a basin.

"Did you know Yoon personally, Professor? He seemed to know you very well. You had a real impact on him."

Adrian felt Santos shift against the wall behind him. He did not turn around. His hand found the arm of his chair and he leaned back — a half-inch, maybe less, but enough to feel the distance open between himself and the detective's question. This was the pivot — the moment the conversation stopped being collegial and started being an interrogation. Adrian recognised it because he'd studied the architecture of persuasion systems for twenty years, and this was one: the friendly approach, the shared joke, the sudden blade.

"Daniel Yoon took one of my undergraduate sections," Adrian said carefully. "Two or three years ago. Bright kid. Nothing remarkable — I have three hundred students a year. Sad to see where his mind took him." He let the sentence sit, then added: "Detective, my work is published and publicly available. I'm cited in undergraduate textbooks. If the suspect also had a copy of Introduction to Machine Learning by Alpaydin, I trust you're not interrogating the publisher."

"No, sir. But Alpaydin didn't map the specific vulnerabilities in NovaMind's behavioral prediction architecture. You did."

That landed. Adrian kept his face neutral, but Morrow's eyes were sharp — not aggressive, just attentive, watching the board after moving a piece.

"I mapped theoretical vulnerabilities," Adrian said carefully. "In a generalized class of systems. My work doesn't mention NovaMind by name."

"It doesn't need to. NovaMind is the market leader. If you describe the vulnerabilities of the tallest building in the city, you don't have to print its address."

Adrian studied the detective. There was something about Morrow, a directness, a lack of pretense, that was disorienting in its simplicity. Most people who entered Adrian's office wanted something and tried to hide it. Morrow wanted something and was putting it on the table like a man dealing cards face-up.

"What do you need, Detective?"

Morrow leaned forward slightly. "The espionage case is more complicated than what's been reported. The systems the suspect accessed — I can't give you details, not yet — but my team doesn't fully understand what they are. We have engineers from NovaMind explaining their own technology to us, and I trust them about as far as I can throw their stock options." He paused, seeming to hear himself. "Look — I'm not saying you're involved. But your help in understanding what's at stake here would really help. I need an independent expert who understands these systems and has no financial connection to the company."

"You need a translator."

"I need someone who can look at what was accessed and tell me what it actually means. Not what NovaMind says it means. What it actually means."

Adrian considered this. The responsible thing, the safe thing, was to decline. Refer Morrow to a colleague. There were dozens of qualified people who could consult on AI systems without the complication of having their name already entangled in the case. The smart play was to smile, offer a list of names, and return to the comfortable business of writing papers about what was wrong with the world without ever having to touch it.

"I should tell you," Morrow added, "this is voluntary. You're not a suspect. You're not under investigation. I'm asking for your expertise as a consultant. You can say no, and I'll walk out that door and find someone else."

Adrian looked again at the photograph on his desk, then moved it aside to make room for Morrow's notepad. He did not yet know what he could offer beyond an interpretation of the evidence. But he could offer that.

"I'll need to see the case materials," Adrian said.

Morrow nodded, and something in his posture released — not dramatically, just a small unbinding of tension, a shift that communicated genuine relief rather than performative gratitude. "I'll have them couriered to you. Standard NDA, consultant agreement, the usual. Nothing classified — this is still SFPD jurisdiction. For now."

"'For now' is doing a lot of heavy lifting in that sentence."

"Yes, it is."

Morrow stood. Santos pushed off the wall without a word — the first movement he'd made in twenty minutes that wasn't breathing. Morrow extended his hand. Adrian shook it.

"We'll be in touch, Professor. For now."

And just like that, he joined his colleague and left. Their footsteps receded down the corridor together — Morrow's measured, Santos's heavier — and then the hallway was quiet, and Adrian was alone with the photograph on his desk and the distinct, unwelcome feeling that for now was doing more work than any two words had a right to.

Adrian sat in his quiet office, the sounds of campus filtering through the window — a distant lawn mower, a burst of undergraduate laughter, the steady ambient hum of a world that didn't know it was running on borrowed time. He looked at the photograph on his desk. Kai Nakamura, forever twenty-nine, forever smiling, frozen in the last moment of a friendship that had ended not with a fight but with a silence that had grown like ice across a lake until one morning you realized you couldn't see the water anymore.

Adrian turned the photograph face down. Then, immediately, turned it back. The late-night arguments about whether technology could be moral. The shared belief, once genuine, that building things could be an act of hope. Kai had kept the company and abandoned the promise. Adrian had kept the photograph. He hated the smallness of that arrangement, and for a moment his hand closed around the frame hard enough to hurt.

Kai had built one of the most powerful behavioral prediction platforms on earth. NovaMind was one of its chief competitors. And someone had just been arrested for trying to map NovaMind's most sensitive systems using Adrian's own analytical frameworks.

He pulled his laptop toward him and opened a browser. The NovaMind story had moved from breaking news to background noise in the relentless churn of the cycle, already being displaced by a senator's scandal and a hurricane forecast. But the arrest record was public — another gift from America's obsessive transparency — and Adrian found the booking information in two clicks.

The suspect's name was Daniel Yoon. Twenty-six years old. No prior record. Listed occupation: software engineer.

And his last known employer, before whatever path had led him to a holding cell in San Francisco County Jail, was a company Adrian recognized immediately.

NovaTok. The short-form content platform.

Kai Nakamura's company.

Adrian closed the laptop. He sat very still in his office as the California sun tracked its slow arc across his bookshelf, illuminating one spine after another — Superintelligence, Weapons of Math Destruction, The Left Hand of Darkness — and he thought about coincidences.

He didn't believe in them.
