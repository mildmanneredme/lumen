Chapter Seventy One.

Monday, 6:17 p.m. CET — eighteen hours remaining

The warning courier arrived at 6:17 p.m., a Moroccan-Dutch woman in a rain jacket. She gave the response phrase through the houseboat door, handed Adrian a folded note, and left before he finished reading. She was a different courier from the one who had collected the deployment papers at noon.

Adrian read it in the galley.

Nadia's handwriting. Compact, urgent, the letters pressed hard enough to emboss the paper.

Raven alive. Council ambush in Arnhem failed. Injured but mobile. Berlin contact confirms inquiries for team locations. No longer under council direction. Motivations unknown.

Update all protocols immediately. Assume she has our trail.

Adrian read the note twice. Then he sat down on the galley bench because his legs had decided, independently of his brain, that standing was no longer a viable activity.

Raven was alive.

The assassin who had killed Priya. Who had killed Seb. Who had murdered movement members with the efficient creativity of an artist who happened to work in the medium of death. The woman the council had deployed as their most precise instrument of violence; she was alive, injured, and hunting.

Adrian had assumed she was dead. They all had. The intelligence from Nadia's network had indicated that the council, having used Raven for the Hale assassination, had moved to eliminate her, standard practice for deniable operations. Sever the chain. Remove the contractor. Ensure the weapon can never testify about who pointed it.

The assumption had been that the council's team would succeed. Raven was good, but she'd been operating under extreme pressure, accelerated timelines, compromised conditions, stress that eroded even the most skilled operator's edge. A professional hit team, sent when she was at her most vulnerable, should have been sufficient.

Should have been.

But Raven was not a "should have been" kind of person.

Adrian's mind raced through the implications. Raven was alive. Raven was tracking them. Raven knew, or was learning, the identities and locations of the surviving team members. And Raven was no longer under anyone's control. The council's leash was severed. She was operating on her own, driven by motivations that Nadia's intelligence couldn't determine.

Revenge? Possibly. The council had tried to kill her, and Raven struck Adrian as someone who took that personally. But revenge against the council wouldn't explain why she was tracking the movement's team. Unless the council was her target and the team was her means of reaching them.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps Raven's motivations were simpler and darker. Perhaps a trained killer with a shattered sense of order simply reverted to the last task in her operational memory: hunting the movement.

Either way, the team was in danger.

Adrian burned Nadia's note over the gas stove, watching the paper curl and blacken, Nadia's handwriting disappearing into ash, and then systematically thought through the vulnerability assessment.

Who was most exposed?

Tomás was in transit, moving between targets in the US, operating under cover identities, with the tactical training to detect and evade pursuit. Low vulnerability. Raven would have to know his route, his aliases, and his timing, and even then Tomás was not a man who was easily cornered.

Ines was in her factory in Berlin, a fixed location, but one she'd fortified with security measures both physical and procedural. Medium vulnerability. If Raven found the factory, Ines would have warning and exit routes.

Sophie was in a vault in Geneva. The vault was designed to be impenetrable. Low vulnerability.

Andrei was in Tallinn, in a safe house chosen specifically for its anonymity. Low vulnerability, as long as the safe house remained uncompromised.

Nadia was in London, operating through a network of cutouts and couriers. Her security was distributed, no single point of failure. Medium vulnerability.

Which left Ólafur.

Ólafur had left London on Nadia's reserved midday flight. He was in Copenhagen now, in a borrowed flat near Christianshavn. A fixed location. No tactical training. No security protocols beyond what Nadia had established. Twenty-two years old. The youngest, the most brilliant, and the most vulnerable member of the team.

If Raven was hunting, Ólafur was the weakest point.

Adrian picked up a burner phone. He needed to warn the team. He needed to update protocols. He needed to get a message to Ólafur specifically, to tell the boy to move locations, to vary his patterns, to be aware that someone very dangerous was looking for him.

But a burner phone call was a single link in a relay chain that took time. Nadia's network was secure but slow. A message to Ólafur would route through at least two intermediaries and take an hour or more to arrive. An hour during which Raven could be closing in.

Move faster, Adrian told himself. Think faster.

He dialed the relay. Left the message, coded, compressed, every word chosen for maximum information in minimum time.

"Priority alert all stations. Asset Raven confirmed active. Hunting our team. Update security protocols. Move locations if possible. Priority protection for Timekeeper. Repeat: priority protection for Timekeeper."

He hung up. Snapped the SIM. Then immediately picked up a second burner, he was running low, only four remaining, and dialed a different relay number. This one was Nadia's emergency line, reserved for situations that couldn't wait for the standard relay chain.

Nadia answered on the first ring. Her voice was controlled, but Adrian could hear the tension beneath it like a wire pulled taut.

"I got the note," he said. "Ólafur."

"I know. I moved him to Copenhagen this afternoon. Our local courier is going to check the flat and stay with him until the handoff. If the address is exposed, there is a second one nearby."

"He needs someone there now if Raven already has his location."

"I'm aware, Adrian. The London rebuild is complete. He carried the portable version with him and has been testing it in Copenhagen. I'm getting someone to his door, but I cannot be in every flat myself."

"Better to interrupt a test than lose Ólafur."

A pause. "Yes."

"How did she survive?"

"She got out of Arnhem. Two men dead. She's been seen buying dressings and tape. Injured, but mobile. Our Berlin contact says she's asking for locations."

"How is she sourcing intelligence without the council's resources?"

"She doesn't need the council's resources. She spent months studying our network as part of her contract. She knows our methods, our communication patterns, the cities we operate in. And she has her own contacts in the private security world, people who trade in locations and identities as readily as Sophie's people trade in money."

Adrian gripped the phone. Evening light lay across the canal. Through the half-drawn curtain he could see bicycles on the bridge and people taking photographs. Somewhere beyond that ordinary view, Raven was closing the distance.

"Can we find her before she finds us?" Adrian asked.

"We're not set up for that. We're set up to hide, not to hunt. Our network is defensive. Hers is offensive. The asymmetry favors her."

"Then we have to be faster. The trigger has to fire before she reaches anyone."

"Eighteen hours," Nadia said.

"Then we have to do it in eighteen hours."

"We were already trying to do it in eighteen hours."

"Then we try harder."

Nadia exhaled. "I'll get Ólafur checked and protected. Everyone gets the warning. No return to a route we think she's seen. Check-ins doubled until we know where she is." She had been running the relays since before midnight. Adrian could hear what the effort was costing her.

"Good."

"Adrian, stay on your assignment. If she's following the latest council intelligence, she may not know you're in Amsterdam yet. We're not treating that as protection. Your driver is on standby."

"The most accessible target is —"

"The person who's alone, in a fixed location, with no tactical training and no security detail."

Ólafur.

"Get someone to him," Adrian said. "Now."

"The courier is moving. Stay on your assignment. The deployment packages need to reach Tomás."

"They went at noon. The relay has the scans and the originals are secured."

"Then focus on that. I'll handle Raven."

The line ended. Adrian removed the SIM and put it with the used cards. Eighteen hours. The first set of access confirmations was still incomplete.

Eighteen hours. And now, in addition to the ticking clock, the patching engineers, the fractured council, the scattered team, and the impossible logistics of an analog operation spanning six countries, now there was a wounded predator in the field, tracking them with the patient, methodical intensity of someone who had nothing left to lose.

Adrian thought of Ólafur. The boy's face in the Zurich basement, red-eyed, trying not to cry, but still, beneath everything, in love with the elegance of his own creation. The timing precision... it's like conducting an orchestra where every musician is on a different continent.

Twenty-two years old. Puffin socks. Spoke about code the way other people spoke about music. And now hunted by an assassin for believing the truth deserved to be heard.

That was the world they'd built. The brightest consumed first. The penalty for idealism: not disillusionment but death. The fight designed, from the beginning, to be unwinnable.

And yet. The fight was only worth fighting because it looked unwinnable. Every generation learned this. The gap between the world as it was and the world as it should be was not a failure. It was a territory. It belonged to anyone stubborn enough to refuse to leave it.

Please be fast enough, Adrian thought. Directed at Nadia. Directed at Devon on his bicycle. Directed at whatever force in the universe might be inclined to protect a twenty-two-year-old prodigy who was too brilliant and too young to understand how much danger he was in.

He turned to those confirmations. Each device needed a valid route, a tested payload, and a place in the synchronized activation. The papers had gone. His work was to catch anything they had missed.

Nothing stopped for fear. That was the lesson the past three months had taught him. You were afraid, and you kept going. You lost people, and you kept going. You learned that a killer was hunting you, and you kept going. Because the alternative was to stop, and stopping meant everything you'd sacrificed, everything Priya had sacrificed, everything Seb had sacrificed, meant nothing.

Adrian picked up his pencil. His movements were faster now, leaner. No wasted motion. He didn't notice the change; Seb would have. Three months ago, this man had paced lecture halls with the ambling gait of someone who had never been in a hurry. Now every gesture was calibrated, every action stripped to its minimum. The professor was still in there somewhere. But the professor had learned to move like someone who knew the clock was real.

The houseboat rocked. The canal shimmered. And somewhere between Amsterdam and London, the distance between the hunter and the hunted was shrinking by the hour.
