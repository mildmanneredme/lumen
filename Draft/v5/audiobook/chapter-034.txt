Chapter Thirty Four.

Sophie Richter poured the wine before she poured the data, and both were precise.

The Chasselas was from Lavaux, lighter than Adrian expected. Sophie's apartment on Rue du Rhone sat above a private bank so discreet it could have been mistaken for an expensive dentist's office. Good furniture, not ostentatious. Real art, not investment art. A wine rack that suggested knowledge. The woman herself was fifty-two, silver-blond, composed, with gray-blue eyes that evaluated Adrian with the calibrated attention of someone who assessed risk for a living.

The laptop on the dining table had its wireless card physically removed. On its screen: a visualization of dark money flows connecting seven individuals to the legislative process of the United States Congress. Three point seven billion dollars, documented with cross-referenced FEC filings, corporate board disclosures, wire transfer records, and email chains showing, among other things, that a senator had read an amendment verbatim from a Word document drafted by the company the legislation was supposed to regulate. Not a comma changed.

Sophie walked him through the architecture without passion or anger. Compliance-officer precision applied to the systematic purchase of a nation's democratic process. Venn's money routed through Cayman trusts. Laine's through his own foundation, even as he publicly advocated for campaign finance reform. Strutt's, Cole's, Barrington's, Polk's. And Kai Nakamura's: seven million dollars, the smallest amount, the thinnest trail. Adrian stared at his former friend's node on the screen and said nothing.

"We are not debating whether the system is corrupt," Sophie said. "I have the receipts. The question is what we do about it."

She had spent twenty years filing internal reports that were buried, approaching regulators who were captured, gathering evidence that no legitimate institution would accept. She had not written a single paper. She had not given a single lecture. She had simply assembled the truth and waited for a delivery mechanism.

"What do you need from me?" Adrian asked.

"The network maps. When the breach fires, my tools need to know where the financial data lives inside each company's infrastructure. Source code and internal communications will generate headlines. The financial records will generate indictments."

"Headlines fade. Indictments don't."

"Precisely." Sophie raised her glass. It wasn't a toast. It was a statement of fact, delivered in crystal.

They worked until two in the morning. Sophie opened a second bottle and produced aged Gruyere. They mapped financial architectures, traced transaction chains, cross-referenced corporate structures. At some point Sophie said, with the driest humor Adrian had encountered on any continent, "If they develop a quantum computer, they'll have bigger problems than my spreadsheets."

<short pause>

Three days later, Lisbon.

Tomás Ferreira opened the door before Adrian knocked. He was forty-five, compact, every gesture calibrated for efficiency, a body built for climbing fences and carrying equipment through tight spaces, not for aesthetics. His apartment in the Alfama was a workshop disguised as a living space: circuit boards, soldering stations, modified networking equipment, and fourteen custom devices arranged on shelves, each disguised as standard corporate IT hardware and each containing a thermite self-destruct charge.

Tomás spoke about his work with focused operational clarity. He did not discuss ideology. He discussed placement angles, timing windows, security patrol patterns. He had spent twelve years designing security systems for three of the six target companies, and now he was using what he'd built to breach it.

Adrian spent two hours walking Tomás through the network maps. Where Andrei had tested him and Ines had challenged him and Sophie had educated him, Tomás simply used him, efficiently, without ego, as one uses any tool that arrives in working condition.

At some point, Tomás made dinner. Rice, grilled fish, a salad dressed with olive oil and lemon. He cooked the way he worked: economically, precisely, without wasted motion.

"Why?" Adrian asked, though by now he knew the answer would be personal.

"My sister. Ana. She was a journalist." The verb tense did the work. "She was investigating a Venn subsidiary's operations in Cambodia. A corporate structure engineered to create distance between the people who profit and the people who suffer. Ana was twenty-nine. She was killed in 2019. The official report said robbery. Three separate sources confirmed it was the subsidiary's private military contractor, tipped off by someone inside the company's legal department."

He looked at his hands, which were resting on the table, very still, and for a moment the operational composure dropped away entirely and what remained was simply a brother who missed his sister, and the missing was not complicated or philosophical, it was just the plain and terrible fact of absence.

"That's why I'm here, Professor. Not because I believe in systems or frameworks or ethical paradigms. Because my sister was killed for doing her job, and the people responsible are still having dinner at restaurants nicer than this one."

<short pause>

On the flight to Reykjavik, Adrian sat with a window full of North Atlantic darkness and felt the cumulative weight of four cities settle into his body.

Andrei's paranoia and precision. Ines's combative brilliance. Sophie's receipts. Tomás's grief. Each one had built a piece of something enormous, in isolation, with the materials of their own damage. Each one had been radicalized not by ideology but by proximity to a truth that no institution would handle. The pattern was the same each time: a person with skills discovered that the systems they served were captured, tried the legitimate channels, found them sealed, and arrived at the same arithmetic Sophie had articulated in her apartment above the bank.

I have exhausted every alternative, and this is what's left.

Ana Ferreira, twenty-nine, killed in Southeast Asia for the crime of journalism. A name he would carry from now on, added to the growing inventory of people who had paid the price for telling the truth.

Priya. Daniel Yoon. Sandra Villanueva. Marcus Webb. Ana.

The list was getting longer. And somewhere out there, the person adding names to it was still working.
