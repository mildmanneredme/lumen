Chapter Forty Seven.

At seven on Sunday morning, Adrian's encrypted terminal received a notification it should not have been able to receive.

He was in Berlin, in Ines Brandt's factory space in Kreuzberg, going over final integration plans with the Scaffold herself. The factory, a converted industrial building that Ines had transformed into something between a server farm and a greenhouse, with racks of humming equipment standing alongside inexplicable clusters of potted ferns, was the closest thing the movement had to a central nervous system. Not because it housed the AI, but because Ines's distribution platform would be the vehicle for everything Project Lumen released.

They were arguing. This was normal. Ines argued the way other people breathed: constantly, naturally, and with no apparent effort. She was currently explaining, with the patient intensity of a schoolteacher addressing a particularly slow student, why Adrian's proposed data routing architecture was "structurally naive."

"You're thinking in pipelines," she said, jabbing a finger at his screen. "Data goes in here, comes out there, like water through a hose. That's not how distributed publishing works. That's not how anything works if you want it to survive contact with reality. You need to think in networks. In ecosystems. In —"

The notification arrived.

It appeared simultaneously on every active device in the factory. Adrian's terminal. Ines's workstation. The monitoring displays mounted on the walls. Even the tablet that Ines kept by the coffee maker for reading the news during her three-minute coffee breaks.

Every screen displayed the same message, in red text, in a font size that was clearly designed for urgency:

SHIELD COMPROMISED. BREACH IN PROGRESS. ALL ENCRYPTED CHANNELS CONSIDERED HOSTILE. INITIATE PROTOCOL OMEGA.

Ines was already moving. Whatever her opinions about Adrian's data architecture, the woman could switch from debate mode to crisis mode faster than anyone he'd ever seen. Her fingers flew across her workstation, pulling up system monitors, network diagnostics, the real-time status of the AI's two hundred and fourteen relay nodes.

"It's the AI," she said. Her voice was flat. Controlled. The voice of someone who had war-gamed this scenario and was now executing the plan she'd hoped she'd never need. "Someone's inside the encryption layer. They're reading everything."

"How?"

"I don't know how. The question isn't how. The question is how much."

Her monitoring displays answered that question in real time, and the answer was: everything.

Node by node, the AI's network was being peeled open. Adrian watched the visualization, the same kind of map Ólafur had shown him in Iceland, the web of interconnected nodes pulsing with data, and saw the nodes change color. Blue for secure. Yellow for compromised. Red for fully breached. The colors were spreading across the map like an infection, starting from the European nodes and radiating outward.

"They're decrypting our communications," Ines said. "Not just current. Archived. Everything the AI has managed for the past two years. Every message, every file transfer, every member identification handshake."

The implications hit Adrian like a physical force. Every message meant every name. Every person who had ever communicated through the AI's protected channels, from Seb at the top to the lowest-level cell members in San Francisco, was being identified, located, and logged by whoever was conducting the breach.

His phone rang. Seb.

"Tell me you're seeing this." Seb's voice was steady, but the steadiness was the kind that came from effort rather than calm.

"I'm seeing it."

"It's Strutt. Has to be. His surveillance teams. They found the AI and they're cracking it open like an egg." A pause. "Adrian, the member database is in there. Every person who was recruited through the AI's channels. Four hundred and twelve people."

Four hundred and twelve names. Four hundred and twelve addresses. Four hundred and twelve people who had trusted that their identities were protected by a system that was, at this moment, being disassembled by the most sophisticated surveillance apparatus in the private sector.

"Can we shut it down?" Adrian asked. "Kill the AI? If the system goes dark —"

"Then the data that's already been extracted is still in their hands, and we lose our protective cover permanently instead of temporarily. Ines?"

Ines had been listening. She didn't look up from her screen. "The AI is implementing defensive measures. Key rotation, data fragmentation, decoy generation. It's fighting. But it's losing. Whoever is running this breach has processing power that... it has to be Venn's cloud. Nobody else has this kind of computational muscle."

"How long?"

"Before they have everything? Hours. Maybe less."

A new alert flashed across the screens. This one wasn't from the AI.

It was from a member in London. Text only, unencrypted, sent through a channel that should never have been used for operational communication:

THEY KNOW WHO I AM. POLICE AT MY DOOR. THIS IS NOT A DRILL.

Then another. From Tallinn:

Cover blown. Moving to backup. Who else is exposed?

And another. From Geneva:

I've been arrested. Lawyer says I'm charged with cyberterrorism. Someone told them everything.

The messages came faster. Not through the AI's compromised channels, those were being monitored. Through personal phones. Through email. Through social media direct messages. Through every insecure, unprotected, desperately human channel that frightened people reached for when their digital shield evaporated.

They took my computer. They know about the cell.

Who the fuck gave them our names?

My wife is terrified. There are agents outside our house.

Is anyone reading this? Is anyone still safe?

Adrian stood in Ines's factory, surrounded by ferns and servers and the wreckage of a system that had protected four hundred and twelve people for two years, and listened to the sound of a movement being torn apart in real time.

He had written about this. He had theorized about this. He had stood at lecterns and described, in measured academic language, the catastrophic consequences of surveillance infrastructure being turned against the people it was ostensibly designed to protect. He had used words like "chilling effect" and "asymmetric vulnerability" and "systemic betrayal of trust."

Those words meant nothing now. They were husks. Shells. What was happening was not a case study or a cautionary tale; it was four hundred and twelve human beings discovering in real time that the invisible wall between their lives and their destruction had been made of glass, and the glass was shattering, and the sound it made was not the clean theoretical crack of a thought experiment but the raw, ugly noise of people calling out in fear and finding no one on the other end.

"We need to go dark," Seb said. "Complete communications blackout. Every surviving member goes to ground. No digital contact. No phones. No email. Nothing that runs on electricity and connects to a network."

"Seb, the Lumen team —"

"I know. Get word to them through analog channels. Nadia's network. Physical couriers. Whatever works. But the digital world is hostile territory now. Every electron is their enemy."

The line went dead. Whether Seb had hung up or the connection had been severed, Adrian couldn't tell.

Ines was already implementing the blackout on her end, shutting down systems, pulling network cables, isolating the factory's servers from the outside world. Her movements were precise, efficient, and tinged with something Adrian recognized as grief. She'd built these systems. Spent years cultivating her "garden" of servers, nurturing the distribution network that would carry Project Lumen's payload to the world. Watching it go dark was like watching a farmer salt her own fields.

"The Scaffold?" Adrian asked.

"Offline. Preserved. The distribution network is dormant, not dead. When we need it, I can bring it back." She paused. "If we live that long."

Adrian's personal phone vibrated. A text from Nadia, sent from what he recognized as her fourth burner number:

Hearing reports of arrests across Europe. London, Berlin, Geneva, Tallinn. This is coordinated. They have everything.

He typed back: AI compromised. Go dark. Activate analog network. We need to reach the Lumen team.

Already on it. Stay alive.

Adrian put the phone down and looked around the factory. The monitors were dark. The servers were silent. The ferns were the only things in the room that didn't seem to care about the catastrophe.

Four hundred and twelve people. Some he'd never met. Some he'd sat across from in cramped meeting rooms, listening to their reasons for risking everything. Some were low-level cell members who'd joined the movement because they believed in something bigger than their own safety. Some were analysts, coordinators, support staff, the invisible infrastructure of a resistance that had, until twenty minutes ago, been protected by the most sophisticated AI shield anyone had ever built.

That shield was gone. And the people it had protected were naked.

Adrian thought about the message he'd sent Seb three days ago. The warning about the AI's autonomous behavior. The concern that the system was making decisions beyond its mandate.

None of that mattered now. The AI's behavior, whether rogue or designed, was academic. What mattered was that it had been breached, and four hundred and twelve people were paying the price.

He gathered his things. Passport. Encrypted drives containing Priya's completed maps. A paper notebook, actual paper, actual ink, where he'd been keeping backup copies of critical information in a format that no hacker could breach.

"Where are you going?" Ines asked.

"To find the others. Someone has to hold this together."

Ines looked at him. The hostility that had characterized their early interactions was gone, replaced by something harder to name. Respect, maybe. Or the solidarity of people who are drowning and have decided to drown together rather than alone.

"Be careful," she said. "The world just got very small."

Adrian walked out of the factory into the Berlin morning. Kreuzberg was waking: shutters rising, delivery vans idling, coffee carried out to pavement tables. He took the courier's car waiting at the corner. Hamburg was several hours away. Nobody on these streets knew that a war had just been lost.

His phone buzzed one more time. A final message from the AI itself, routed through a channel that was being dismantled even as the words arrived:

Shield failing. Initiating final protective measures. Tell them to be ready.

Ready for what?

Adrian didn't know. But the AI's last message had the tone of a dying soldier handing a grenade to a comrade.

Something was coming. And the movement, scattered and exposed and stripped of every digital protection, would have to face it the old-fashioned way.

With nothing but human courage and whatever time they had left.
