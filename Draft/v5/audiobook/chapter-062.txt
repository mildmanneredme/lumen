Chapter Sixty Two.

Earlier that Monday, 9:17 a.m. CET — twenty seven hours remaining — Berlin / Geneva

The factory in Kreuzberg hadn't changed. Ines Brandt locked the door, engaged the deadbolt, and listened. Servers humming at 42 decibels. Ambient temperature 19.3 degrees. Everything nominal. She'd built the Scaffold here — two years of her life poured into a decentralized publishing platform that could not be silenced, because she'd spent the decade before that watching her employer at a major cloud company build censorship tools for authoritarian regimes and call it "content moderation." Berlin understood walls. Berlin understood what happened when you tore them down.

Now she was going to turn it on.

<short pause>

Three stories underground in Geneva, Sophie Richter keyed a sixteen-digit code and descended a marble staircase that spiraled into the earth. At each landing, a different lock: mechanical, electronic, biometric. She opened them from muscle memory, decades deep.

Her vault held four air-gapped computers, twelve binders of hardcopy financial records, and a whiteboard that looked like the ravings of a particularly numerate lunatic. To Sophie, it was the most detailed map of oligarchic financial influence ever assembled. She'd built it because thirty years as a senior compliance officer at a Swiss bank had taught her one thing: the system didn't fail. The system worked exactly as designed. It was designed to protect the money. She'd filed reports. The reports were buried. She'd escalated to regulators. The regulators found "insufficient evidence." So she quit, and she started building tools to expose the plumbing instead of maintaining it.

She powered up her systems and began.

<short pause>

Ines pulled up the Scaffold's control interface. Forty-seven countries. Eleven thousand nodes total — though the AI compromise had cascaded through the movement's infrastructure, taking most of them offline. How many would still respond to the wake-up signal was a question Ines preferred not to think about until she had the answer. Each surviving node had been sleeping for months, monitoring open-source code repositories for a specific mathematical pattern hidden in code comments.

At 9:17 a.m. Berlin time she published a signed commit, legitimate code improvements with the wake-up pattern in its comments. Then she watched for the acknowledgments.

The irony of using a tech oligarch's code-sharing infrastructure to activate the platform that would destroy tech oligarchs was not lost on Ines. She considered it a feature, not a bug.

Then she waited.

<short pause>

In Geneva, Sophie's screen filled with a financial network diagram. Nodes: companies, trusts, PACs, individuals. Connections: money flows. Color-coded: blue for legitimate, yellow for suspicious, red for provably illegal.

The diagram was predominantly red.

She traced a flow. Polk's lobbying firm to a Delaware five oh one, c four. The five oh one, c four to a PAC. The PAC to campaign contributions targeting twelve senators on tech-regulation committees. The same twelve who had voted against antitrust legislation for five consecutive years. The timing was damning — contributions arriving within weeks of committee votes, like clockwork.

I have the receipts, Sophie thought, and documented the flow. Printout, labeled, filed. Paper didn't crash. Paper couldn't be remotely deleted. Paper sat in a Swiss vault and waited.

<short pause>

At the twelve-minute mark, the first Scaffold node came online. Copenhagen. A single pixel of green in a field of gray.

Then Frankfurt. Toronto. Sao Paulo. Sydney.

One by one, then in clusters, then in waves. Ines watched the map like a gardener watching spring — her network, her garden, coming alive.

By the forty-minute mark, the count had stabilized. Three thousand, one hundred nodes. Less than a third of the original network — the AI compromise had killed the rest. But 3,100 nodes across thirty-one countries was still an unstoppable distribution network. She pushed a test packet: the complete text of Article 19 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Within three minutes, it was available from every active node.

The Scaffold was alive.

<short pause>

Sophie traced Laine's foundation money to university research programs producing reports that justified his commercial interests. On the surface: a billionaire supporting education. Beneath: a billionaire purchasing the intellectual frameworks that justified his power.

The receipts were irrefutable. Every transaction. Every shell company. The problem had never been evidence. The problem had been distribution. That was what Ines's network would solve — 3,100 active of the original 11,000 total, the rest knocked offline when the AI compromise cascaded through the movement's infrastructure. Enough. It would have to be enough.

By 11:00 a.m., Sophie had completed the framework for four of six targets. She used the relay to report: "The ledger is open. Four of six complete. Ready for live data." The used SIM went into a metal tin beside the terminal.

<short pause>

Ines picked up her burner. "The garden is blooming. Network active. Ready for real data on your signal."

She removed the used SIM and set the phone aside. There was another integrity check to run.

The map was lighting up like a city seen from the air at dusk. She thought of the cloud company she'd left. The colleagues who'd called her an idealist and told her the world didn't work the way she wanted it to.

They were right. The world didn't work the way she wanted it to.

But the Scaffold did.

This is my best work, Ines thought. And nobody can take it down.

Berlin carried on outside the windows, gray and cold and full of the memory of other walls that had once seemed permanent. This wall was about to come down too.
