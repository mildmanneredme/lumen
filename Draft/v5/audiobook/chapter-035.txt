Chapter Thirty Five.

Nadia saw the Barrington piece at 6:47 AM, sitting on her bathroom floor with her laptop balanced on the toilet lid, which was where she did her best reading because nobody expected to find a journalist there and the tiles were cold enough to keep her alert.

The headline: TECH SABOTEURS LINKED TO FOREIGN INTELLIGENCE NETWORKS. The byline was a staff writer at Barrington's flagship cable news division, a man Nadia had met once at a media ethics conference who had struck her as someone who would write whatever his employer needed written and consider it professionalism. The article cited "senior intelligence officials speaking on condition of anonymity" and "corporate security experts" who described the movement as a "sophisticated domestic terrorism operation with possible state-sponsored backing."

She read it twice. Then she read the three syndicated versions that had already appeared on Barrington's newspaper platforms, each with slightly different framing but identical sourcing. The media machine was running. The narrative was being set.

Nadia closed the laptop and sat on the cold tiles and thought.

The movement was being reframed. Not as activists or whistleblowers or concerned citizens but as terrorists. Foreign-linked. Dangerous. The framing would escalate. She'd seen this playbook before: label, isolate, criminalize, and by the time anyone thought to question the narrative, the public had already sorted the participants into a category that didn't deserve sympathy.

She needed to build counter-narrative capacity. Not by fighting Barrington's media empire head-on — that was asymmetric warfare she'd lose — but by pre-positioning journalists she trusted to receive the real story when the time came. Journalists who would run it. Journalists who couldn't be pressured.

She pulled out her notebook and began a new list.

Trusted. Will run. Can't be bought.

Amara Diallo at the Guardian. Nadia had worked with her on the Cerberus private military story in 2021. Amara had held the piece for three weeks under legal threat from a firm representing Cerberus's parent company, then published anyway, with additional documentation that made the legal threat evaporate. She was solid. She was fearless. She was also, Nadia knew, currently working on a story about algorithmic manipulation that dovetailed neatly with what the movement was about to expose.

Tomasz Wozniak at Politico Europe. Financial regulation beat. He'd broken the story about dark money flows through EU lobbying networks two years ago and had been quietly blacklisted from three industry conferences as a result. He wore the blacklisting like a medal. He would understand Sophie's data instantly.

Jin-Soo Park at the Hankyoreh in Seoul. Technology and surveillance coverage. She'd reported on the Pegasus spyware scandal with a rigor that put most Western outlets to shame. Asian-Pacific distribution would be critical — the story couldn't be framed as a purely Western concern.

Carmen Herrera at Proceso in Mexico City. She'd survived two assassination attempts and still published. If Carmen wouldn't run from narcos, she wouldn't run from tech oligarchs.

Four journalists. Three continents. Each one someone Nadia had worked with, shared sources with, trusted with sensitive information. Each one someone who couldn't be bought, threatened, or pressured into spiking a story.

She'd need more. She'd need broadcasters, not just print. She'd need someone in the documentary space for the long-form version. She'd need legal support — pre-publication review from lawyers who understood national security reporting and wouldn't fold under corporate pressure letters.

But four was a start. Four was a foundation.

Nadia picked up a new prepaid phone and began making calls.

Amara first. She didn't tell her everything. She told her enough: that a major technology story was coming, that the documentation would be comprehensive, that the sourcing would be primary rather than anonymous, and that the subjects of the story had the resources to mount a legal and media campaign to discredit it before publication.

"How major?" Amara asked.

"Paradigm-shifting major. Multiple companies. Financial records, internal communications, source code. Evidence of coordinated political capture."

"When?"

"Weeks, not months. I'm giving you advance notice because when the material drops, the window for responsible publication will be narrow. The companies will move to suppress. Injunctions, DMCA takedowns, national security claims. You'll need your lawyers ready before the material arrives. The claims still need verification and review before publication."

"Send me a backgrounder. Encrypted. I'll start the legal conversation."

Nadia didn't use encrypted email. She wrote a two-page summary in longhand, sealed it in an envelope, and addressed it to Amara's home in Brixton. She'd mail it from a post office in Oakland tomorrow. If the post held to its estimate, Amara would have it the following week.

She made similar calls to Tomasz, Jin-Soo, and Carmen. Each conversation was calibrated to the journalist's specific expertise and geographic reach. Each was conducted on a different prepaid phone. Each phone was broken after use.

The Barrington narrative was running. But narratives could be countered. Not with louder voices — Barrington had those — but with better evidence, pre-positioned in the hands of journalists who would use it.

Nadia was not a true believer. She was not an ideologue. She was a reporter who had spent a decade following the evidence, and the evidence had led her here: to a bathroom floor in Oakland, building a distribution network for a story that would either restore democratic accountability or get everyone involved imprisoned.

She was skeptical about the movement's methods. She was skeptical about Seb's mathematics and his willingness to accept casualties as operational variables. She was skeptical about whether total transparency would produce the democratic correction its architects predicted, or whether it would produce chaos that the oligarchs were better positioned to exploit than anyone else.

But she was not skeptical about the evidence. The evidence was real. Sophie's receipts were real. The dead were real. Priya was real.

And the counter-narrative she was building was not about belief. It was about making sure that when the truth arrived, someone was ready to publish it, and that the publishing would happen simultaneously across enough outlets and jurisdictions that no single injunction, no single legal threat, no single Barrington editorial could contain it.

She washed her coffee cup. She swept the broken phone fragments into a paper bag and dropped them in three different trash cans on her walk to the post office.

The Barrington machine was running. Nadia's machine was smaller, quieter, and analog.

But it was running too.
