Chapter Six.

The trick to extracting someone from federal custody was not getting in. Getting in was a logistics problem, and logistics problems were the easiest kind — they yielded to research, preparation, and the systematic exploitation of the gap between how secure a system was supposed to be and how secure it actually was. Every system had this gap. The more bureaucratic the institution, the wider it yawned.

The trick was making sure no one noticed anything had left.

By Thursday morning, Raven had been in San Francisco for two days, and she had not wasted a single one.

She'd checked into a hotel in the Tenderloin — not because the neighborhood was convenient, but because it was a place where no one remembered faces. The hotel was called The Civic, an aspirational name for a place that bore no resemblance to the word's implications. Her room was on the fourth floor, corner unit, two exits from the stairwell, sightline to the street. She'd set her single bag on the bed — hard-shell, carry-on size, containing precisely what she needed and nothing she didn't — and spent those two days doing what she did best: learning a building's rhythms — its shift changes, its blind spots, its small human failures.

The San Francisco County Jail at 850 Bryant Street was, from a security perspective, a study in contradictions. Its exterior presented the fortress aesthetic beloved by municipal architects: heavy concrete, narrow windows, a lobby that communicated severity through uncomfortable chairs. Its interior was the product of six decades of budget compromises, deferred maintenance, and the institutional entropy that afflicted every government building opened, expanded, retrofitted, condemned, reopened, and partially renovated across multiple political administrations, each with different priorities and none with enough money.

The food was the first key. The jail's meal preparation was managed by a contracted catering service called Allied Institutional, which employed a rotating roster of kitchen workers paid slightly more than minimum wage and subjected to slightly less than adequate supervision. Raven had obtained a temporary shift assignment through a staffing agency that supplied the catering company — one phone call, a Social Security number belonging to a woman in Nevada who had not filed taxes in four years, and a willingness to arrive at 5 a.m. for orientation. The kitchen supervisor barely looked at her badge before pointing her toward the serving line.

The compound she'd selected for Stage One was elegant in its simplicity: a concentrated extract of Staphylococcal enterotoxin B — food poisoning, essentially, but targeted and precise. Enough to produce severe gastric distress within thirty minutes of ingestion. Not enough to kill. Not enough to raise suspicion beyond a bad batch of institutional eggs. She introduced it into the breakfast tray designated for Cell 4-C during the plating process, a movement that took less than two seconds and was invisible to anyone who wasn't specifically watching her hands.

By 6:45 a.m., she was off the premises.

By 7:12, Daniel Yoon was vomiting.

<short pause>

The medical transport protocol at 850 Bryant Street was exactly as the DOJ report had described. When an inmate in pre-trial isolation presented with acute medical symptoms, the procedure was: facility nurse assessment, physician confirmation by phone, transport authorization, and transfer by ambulance to San Francisco General Hospital. Two corrections officers accompanied the patient. One medical orderly. The route followed 7th Street south to Potrero Avenue, left onto 23rd Street, and into the ambulance bay at SFGH's emergency entrance. Eleven minutes, if traffic was kind. Fourteen, at worst.

Raven had driven the route seven times.

The ambulance left the jail at 7:38 a.m. Raven followed in a rented white panel van — unmarked, unremarkable, a vehicle that existed in every urban landscape as furniture — maintaining a two-car distance. At the intersection of 7th and Brannan, a delivery truck — driven by a man she'd hired through three intermediaries and who believed he was participating in a film production's guerrilla shoot — stalled at the green light, creating a brief traffic obstruction. Not a blockade. Just a delay. The ambulance stopped.

The eleven-minute window had become seven.

She pulled the van alongside the ambulance at the Brannan Street loading zone. The two corrections officers in the rear compartment were focused on the patient, who was retching into a medical basin and sweating through his orange jumpsuit. Raven stepped out, opened the van's side door, and approached the ambulance's rear entrance wearing the uniform of an SFGH emergency transport coordinator — badge clipped, radio on hip, clipboard in hand — and the expression of someone whose job was to deal with problems that arrived without warning.

"We've got a receiving conflict at the ER bay," she told the nearest officer through the rear window. "Hazmat situation. They're rerouting all incoming to the overflow entrance on Vermont Street. I need to ride with you and redirect."

The officer — a man in his late forties who had been doing this job long enough to find it tedious and not long enough to find it suspicious — looked at her badge. Looked at her clipboard. Looked at the patient, who was still retching.

"Fine. Hop in."

She climbed into the rear compartment. The orderly barely acknowledged her. The corrections officers were watching Yoon, who was curled on the gurney with the concentrated misery of a man whose body had betrayed him at the worst possible moment.

What happened next took ninety seconds.

Raven produced a medical canister from the supply bag she carried — clearly labeled, hospital-branded, unremarkable — and twisted the valve. The odorless sedative dispersed through the rear cabin. The orderly's head dropped first, then the two officers. Raven held her breath and slipped on a small rebreather she'd palmed from her pocket. She opened the rear doors.

The van was alongside. The man inside — another hired hand, another intermediary, another person who believed he was helping with something far more innocent than reality — helped her transfer Daniel Yoon's limp form to the van. In the van, already lying on a second gurney, was the substitute. Male. Twenty-six. Matching height and build. Close enough in the face for people who weren't looking too hard, and no one in the jail's system would be looking hard at a sick man returned from the hospital.

She transferred the substitute to the ambulance gurney. Orange jumpsuit, already fitted. Wristband, already cloned. The substitute was sedated but conscious — dazed, frightened, paid a sum of money he would never live to spend.

The officers and orderly would wake in three minutes. They would remember nothing useful — the sedative's amnestic properties were its most valuable feature. They would find their patient on the gurney, groggy, still nauseous. They would continue to SFGH, where the emergency department would assess and find nothing requiring admission. They would return the "patient" to 850 Bryant Street.

Raven drove the van south on Vermont Street. The real Daniel Yoon was in the back, unconscious. She had one day before the hearing. The client wanted names, contact points, a route into the network.

Yoon gave them none.

By Friday morning, he was dead. His death appeared in no custody record, no coroner's report, no newspaper. Raven entered it in the private completion report beside a single sentence: No actionable intelligence obtained.

At the hearing, another man would answer to his name.

<short pause>

The hearing went as planned. It always did, when the choreography was precise.

The syndicate lawyer — a man named Alistair Croft, whose firm maintained a spotless reputation and an invisible second client roster — guided the substitute through the initial appearance with practised economy. Brief remarks. Standard plea. Minimal eye contact with the judge. The substitute performed adequately; not well enough to convince anyone who knew Daniel Yoon intimately, but the people in the courtroom didn't know Daniel Yoon. They knew a case file.

Afterward, in the holding area, Croft handed the substitute a small wireless earpiece. "For secure communication with counsel," he explained. "Standard procedure. Keep it in tonight — I may need to reach you regarding the plea arrangement."

The substitute put it in his ear. The casing was warm from Croft's pocket — body temperature, unremarkable. The compound embedded in the silicone tip was stable at room temperature but designed to dissolve on sustained contact with the heat and moisture of the ear canal, releasing a concentrated cardiotoxin through the thin skin and into the bloodstream. Six to eight hours. By morning, his heart would have stopped.

She had synthesized the compound herself, in a rented laboratory space in East London that she used under the name of a postdoctoral researcher at Imperial College. The synthesis had taken three weeks, months before this contract arrived; she kept a small stock for jobs that required it. She had enjoyed it enormously. Chemistry, like her profession, was fundamentally a discipline of precision — the difference between a medicine and a poison was, in most cases, a question of concentration, and the difference between a detectable poison and an undetectable one was a question of molecular architecture. She found both problems deeply satisfying.

The substitute was returned to Cell 4-C that evening. He lay on the cot, the earpiece still in, and fell asleep.

At 5:47 a.m., his heart stopped.

The nurse conducting the morning welfare check found him unresponsive at 6:12. Paramedics arrived at 6:19. He was pronounced dead at 6:23.

Cause of death, pending autopsy: apparent cardiac arrest.

The earpiece was catalogued as personal property — a standard wireless earbud, model consistent with what a young software engineer might own. No one tested its chemical composition. There was no reason to.

<short pause>

Raven was already at SFO when the news broke. She sat at the gate for her London flight — traveling under a Canadian passport in the name of Margaret Chen, a management consultant returning from a client engagement — and watched the breaking news banner scroll across the terminal monitors.

SUSPECT IN NOVAMIND ESPIONAGE CASE DIES IN CUSTODY

She took a sip of her tea. Earl Grey. The one indulgence she permitted herself in airports.

Four stages completed. Two men dead. No public connection between them.

She boarded the flight, settled into her aisle seat — left side, forward cabin — and closed her eyes. The steady ventilation hum was different from her flat in Bermondsey, but regular enough to quiet the noise around her. Her mind, for the first time since Tuesday, was quiet.

Another problem, cleanly solved.
