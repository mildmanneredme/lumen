Chapter Sixty Three.

Earlier that Monday, 9:17 a.m. Tallinn time — twenty eight hours remaining

The safe house in Tallinn's Old Town was a third-floor apartment above a shop that sold handmade candles. The entire building smelled of beeswax and lavender, which, Andrei Lepp reflected, was probably the most pleasant environment in which anyone had ever prepared to commit multiple felonies against the most powerful corporations in human history.

He'd arrived two hours earlier on a flight under a reserve identity. A courier drove him from the airport, then left him to walk the last few streets. He took two extra turns before entering the candle shop. The old route was familiar. That did not make it safe.

Andrei did not use the Wi-Fi. The Wi-Fi was the enemy. All Wi-Fi was the enemy. All digital communication was the enemy. All networks, all connections, all signals traveling through the electromagnetic spectrum were, in Andrei's professional estimation, nothing more than attack surfaces waiting to be exploited.

He knew this because exploiting them was his job.

The encrypted drives were where he'd left them, hidden inside the false bottom of a nineteenth-century writing desk he'd purchased from an antique shop and modified with a concealed compartment accessible only by pressing three specific points simultaneously. The compartment was lined with copper mesh to block electromagnetic signals. The drives themselves were wrapped in Faraday bags. And the desk was positioned against an interior wall, away from windows, because Andrei was aware that certain intelligence agencies possessed the capability to read electromagnetic emissions from electronic devices at ranges of up to three hundred meters, and he had not spent three years building the most sophisticated custom exploit packages in the history of computer security only to have them compromised by a van with an antenna parked on the street below.

Paranoid, people called him. The word had followed him since childhood, a quiet, intense boy in post-Soviet Estonian housing blocks who took apart every electronic device he could find, not to fix them but to understand how they could be broken. His mother had called it a hobby. His teachers had called it antisocial. NATO had called it a career.

Ten years he'd worked for them. Ten years finding vulnerabilities in the systems of adversarial nations, building exploits that could disable communications networks, cripple power grids, and open digital doors that were never meant to be opened. He'd been good at it, so good that his security clearance had eventually been raised to a level where he learned things that made sleep difficult and trust impossible.

Like the fact that the same exploits he built for NATO were being sold, through back-channel agreements he wasn't supposed to know about, to the very tech companies that Project Lumen was now targeting. The oligarchs didn't just control the platforms. They had access to the weapons too.

That was when Andrei had walked away. That was when a paranoid genius with an inconvenient conscience had become a paranoid genius with a cause.

He opened the false bottom of the desk and removed six drives.

Each one was a different color, not for aesthetic reasons but because Andrei's mind worked in systems of categorization, and the color coding ensured he would never, under any circumstances, accidentally load the wrong exploit package onto the wrong deployment device. The consequences of such an error ranged from "the exploit fails silently" to "the exploit triggers a security alert that locks down the target and gives the oligarchs a complete blueprint of the attack methodology."

Red: Elias Strutt's surveillance infrastructure. The Architect's systems.

Blue: Arthur Venn's cloud infrastructure. The Machine's fortress.

Green: Magnus Cole's IP and infrastructure empire. The Showman's domain.

Yellow: Geoffrey Laine's foundation and diplomatic network. The Philanthropist's facade.

White: Clive Barrington's media empire. The Narrator's machine.

Black: Kai Nakamura's content platform. The youngest oligarch's kingdom.

Six drives. Six custom-built skeleton keys. Each one designed to bypass a specific company's security architecture, firewalls, intrusion detection systems, encryption at rest, access control layers, with the surgical precision of a scalpel cutting through exactly the right layers of tissue to reach an organ without damaging the structures around it.

Andrei set the drives in a row on the desk. His hands were shaking. They'd been shaking since Zurich.

He breathed. Counted to seven, a prime number, because prime numbers were calming in a way that composite numbers were not, and Andrei was aware that this preference made him unusual and did not care. He breathed again. The tremor subsided to a manageable vibration.

He connected the first drive, red, Strutt, to an air-gapped laptop. Air-gapped meant no network connection. No Wi-Fi, no Bluetooth, no cellular radio. The laptop's wireless hardware had been physically removed, not disabled in software, which could be re-enabled by malware, but removed with a soldering iron. The empty space where the Wi-Fi card had been was filled with epoxy.

The exploit loaded. Andrei began the verification sequence.

Each exploit was a chain of operations designed to be executed in a specific order against a specific target. Step one: establish a covert tunnel from the physical implant (Tomás's device) to the target's internal network, using a vulnerability in the network management protocol that Andrei had discovered fourteen months ago. Step two: escalate privileges using a flaw in the authentication system that the target's security team had patched in the standard product but not in the customized deployment, a common oversight that Andrei had exploited in nine previous operations during his NATO career. Step three: navigate to the target data stores using Priya's available maps. Adrian's final route corrections would be added before activation. Step four: begin silent exfiltration.

He ran the chain against a simulation built from the latest intelligence. The earlier AI attack had targeted different flaws. Its damage was occupying Strutt's engineers, but Andrei had to assume any one of his own routes could already have changed. That was why he tested each stage instead of trusting the earlier results.

The simulation ran. The exploit chain executed. The covert tunnel established. Privileges escalated. Navigation successful. Exfiltration began.

Andrei exhaled.

One down. Five to go.

He disconnected the red drive, connected the blue, Venn, the Machine, and began again.

The process was meticulous. Each exploit had to be verified, each chain tested, each step confirmed against the simulation. It was work that required absolute concentration, a focus that narrowed the world to a single laptop screen and the mathematical certainty of code executing in sequence.

His hands stopped shaking during the second verification. The work was calming. The work was always calming. The work was the one place where Andrei's mind, restless, anxious, perpetually scanning for threats, found peace. In the exploit chain, there was no ambiguity. No uncertainty. No trust required. Just logic, precision, and the elegant satisfaction of a key fitting a lock.

By the time he had verified four drives, forty-five minutes had passed. The candle shop had opened. Footsteps moved above him while he worked.

He paused before the white drive. Barrington's media empire. This one had been the most difficult to build. Barrington's company was old-school; its digital infrastructure had been bolted onto a legacy system that ran on technology from three different decades. Exploiting modern systems was elegant. Exploiting a hybrid monster built from 1990s databases, 2010s cloud migrations, and 2020s AI integrations was like performing surgery on a patient who had been assembled from spare parts.

Andrei loaded the exploit. Ran the simulation. Watched the chain execute.

Step one: successful. Step two: successful. Step three...

A pause. The simulation hung. The navigation step was looking for a data pathway that didn't exist in the simulated architecture.

Andrei's stomach dropped.

He checked the network maps. Priya's original diagram of Barrington's internal systems showed a direct pathway between the media company's internal communications server and its financial records database. But the pathway was... He looked more closely. The line on Priya's diagram was drawn in pencil, not ink. A tentative connection. An inference, not a confirmed route.

If the pathway didn't exist in the real system, the exploit would fail at step three. The financial records would be unreachable. The breach would produce communications data but not the money trails, and without the money trails, half of Sophie's forensic analysis would be impossible.

Andrei stared at the screen. His hands were shaking again.

He could modify the exploit to attempt an alternative pathway, but the alternative would add time to the execution, perhaps thirty seconds, perhaps a minute. In that minute, the intrusion detection system might catch the anomalous traffic pattern. One alert was all it would take. One alert, and Barrington's security team would lock the system, and the breach would fail, and the evidence connecting the media mogul's money to every politician he'd purchased would remain hidden.

Andrei closed his eyes. Counted to seven. Opened them.

He pulled up the exploit code and began modifying the navigation step. He added a conditional branch: if the primary pathway was blocked, the exploit would attempt a secondary route through the company's backup infrastructure. The backup systems were older, less monitored, and connected to the same databases through a legacy integration that Barrington's IT team had probably forgotten existed.

It was a gamble. The backup pathway might not be there. The legacy integration might have been decommissioned. Andrei was writing code based on assumptions about a system he'd never seen, using intelligence that might be outdated, in a safe house that smelled of beeswax, with hands that wouldn't stop trembling.

He wrote the code. Tested it against the simulation. The conditional branch triggered. The secondary pathway connected. The financial records became accessible.

In the simulation.

In reality, it was a coin flip.

Andrei saved the modified exploit, disconnected the white drive, and connected the black, Nakamura's platform. The last one.

This was the fastest verification. Nakamura's platform was modern, well-architected, and, surprisingly, the least defended of the six targets. Its security was good by industry standards, but it lacked the multiple redundant layers that Strutt and Venn had built. Andrei's exploit chain cut through it cleanly.

He ran the simulation. Every step executed flawlessly.

Too easy, the paranoid part of his brain whispered. Why is the youngest oligarch's security the weakest?

He didn't have an answer. He filed the question away. There were bigger things to worry about.

Six drives. Six exploits. Five verified clean. One modified with a conditional branch that might work and might not.

Andrei placed the drives back in their Faraday bags, the bags back in the copper-lined compartment, the compartment back in the false bottom of the desk. He would transport them to the deployment stage through a courier chain Nadia had arranged, hand to hand, no digital tracking, each courier knowing only their segment of the route.

He picked up the burner phone. Dialed the relay.

"Six keys cut. Five clean, one conditional. Ready for deployment on your signal."

The relay acknowledged. Andrei removed the SIM, put it with the others awaiting destruction, and returned to the drives.

Then he sat in the candlelit gloom of the Tallinn apartment and pressed his shaking hands flat against the desk.

Tomorrow, those exploits would either open six doors that had never been opened before, or they would fail, and the most powerful people in the world would know exactly what had been attempted and exactly who had attempted it.

There were no second chances. No patches. No retries. First attempt or nothing.

Andrei closed his eyes and counted prime numbers until his hands were still.

Two. Three. Five. Seven. Eleven. Thirteen.

Outside, the spires of Tallinn stood against a pale afternoon sky. Andrei checked the courier time once more.
