Chapter Sixty Seven.

Monday, 12:17 p.m. CET — twenty four hours remaining — Amsterdam, in parallel

The houseboat creaked. Outside, tourists crowded the canal bridges and bicycles rattled over the cobbles. Adrian kept the porthole curtain half drawn. Noon had passed. The courier would be here shortly.

Adrian sat at the galley table under a single lamp, surrounded by translucent paper.

He was finishing the deployment packages he had amended alongside the maps that morning, turning the final route corrections into instructions Andrei's exploits could follow. Each target company required a specific sequence: entry point (where Tomás's device connected to the network), navigation path (the route through the internal architecture to the data stores), and extraction protocol (the order in which data was pulled, prioritized by value and vulnerability to detection).

It was intricate work, the kind that required holding the entire architecture of a company's digital infrastructure in his head simultaneously, seeing the whole while attending to the parts. It was like assembling a jigsaw puzzle where the pieces kept shifting and the picture was something you'd never seen before.

Except Adrian had seen these pictures before. That was the strange, uncomfortable truth he kept bumping up against as he worked. He knew these companies. He had spent years evaluating their infrastructure, their culture, their data practices. As an angel investor, he had been trained to see through the surface presentation, the glossy pitch decks and the visionary founder narratives, to the underlying architecture of how a company actually worked.

Where did the data live? How did it flow? Who had access? What were the chokepoints?

These were questions an investor asked to evaluate risk. They were also, it turned out, the exact questions you needed to answer to crack a company open and spill its secrets across the world.

A man who had profited from the architecture of power was now trying to dismantle it. The irony was not lost on him. Neither was the urgency.

He picked up the pencil. The work was in front of him, and the clock was the clock.

Adrian worked on Cole's deployment package first. The Showman's infrastructure was the most segmented, each division operating in its own silo, minimal cross-communication. The approach Adrian designed was a precision strike: Tomás's device would connect in the corporate communications wing (the division housing internal messaging, email servers, and the executive communication platform), and the exploit would navigate directly to the executive data stores while ignoring the surrounding divisions. In and out. Surgical.

He finished Cole's package and moved to Strutt.

Strutt's architecture was different, a vast, interconnected surveillance apparatus where every system talked to every other system. The social media empire had been built on the principle that more data sharing meant better targeting, and the internal infrastructure reflected this philosophy. Everything was connected, which made navigation easier but detection harder. The intrusion detection systems were the most sophisticated of any target, designed by engineers who understood that the same interconnectedness that made the platform powerful also made it vulnerable.

Adrian's approach for Strutt was a slow crawl: the exploit would move through the network in small, randomized hops that mimicked normal internal traffic patterns. Each hop would pause for a random interval, thirty seconds, ninety seconds, four minutes, to avoid triggering the behavioral analysis algorithms that looked for sustained, directional data movement. It would take longer, but it would be invisible.

He hoped.

He picked up Priya's original diagram of Strutt's architecture. Her handwriting was small and precise, every label meticulous, every annotation thoughtful. She'd drawn small arrows in the margins, notes to herself about security cameras, access protocols, the names of the security engineers she'd met during her double life inside the system.

One annotation caught his eye. In the corner of the diagram, in handwriting so small he needed the lamp closer to read it:

Who feeds us this? Too precise. Someone inside.

Priya had noticed. Even before Adrian, even before anyone else in the movement, Priya had sensed that the intelligence fueling Project Lumen was too good to come from outside. Someone was feeding them information from within the oligarch council itself.

She'd written the question. She hadn't lived to find the answer.

Adrian stared at the annotation for a long time. His mind turned to Kai, automatically, irresistibly, the way a compass needle turns north. Who feeds us this? The same question had nagged at Adrian since Andrei had mentioned that the intelligence "was too good." Someone with access to the oligarchs' internal security architectures, their vulnerability assessments, their network topologies. Someone sitting at the table.

But who? The only council member who had shown any sign of hesitation was Laine, the Philanthropist, whose moral qualms about the council's methods surfaced occasionally in the scenes Seb had described. But Laine's hesitations were performative. He always sided with power in the end.

And Kai? Kai was a blank. A follower. The youngest, least experienced member of the council, going along with the consensus, offering nothing of note. Exactly the kind of person you would overlook.

Adrian's pencil hovered over the deployment package for Kai's company. The map he'd drawn of Kai's infrastructure was the most detailed of the six, not because Kai's company was the most complex, but because Adrian knew it the most intimately. He had been there when the first server rack was installed. He had helped design the initial data architecture. He had watched it grow from a startup's improvisation into a global platform's carefully engineered ecosystem.

And Andrei had noted that Kai's company was the least defended of the six targets. The weakest security. The easiest breach.

Why?

The question circled. Adrian pushed it away. There was no time for mysteries. There was only time for work.

At 12:17 p.m., the last package was ready. Priya's instructions supplied most of each route; Adrian had written the final changes beside the dated intelligence. A courier would scan the papers at an isolated terminal and add the instructions to the payloads already staged in the relay. Andrei's verified drives would authorize those payloads locally. Tomás would not need a parcel to cross the Atlantic after him.

The handoffs were slow. A courier could be followed, a note seized. But each handoff carried only a fragment, and none automatically entered an archive the oligarchs could open. Adrian needed that distinction to hold for another day.

He put the papers in a waterproof folder. The courier knocked at 12:30. Adrian checked the response phrase, handed over the originals, and kept a copy.

Trust. The whole operation ran on trust. Every system humanity had ever built was just an attempt to mechanize it — and every crisis was the universe reminding us the substitute had never been real.

He rubbed his eyes, stood, and paced the narrow galley. His body wanted movement. Outside, a sightseeing boat passed close enough for him to hear the guide.

He thought of Priya.

She would have been faster. She would have been more precise. She would have caught things he'd missed, corrected assumptions he'd made, seen vulnerabilities he'd overlooked. But she was dead, and he was here, and the deployment packages were as good as he could make them.

Priya, he thought. I hope this is enough.

He thought of Seb. The man who had started it all, who had looked at the machinery of oligarchic power and decided, with the cold mathematical certainty that was both his greatest strength and his most unsettling quality, that the only response was to dismantle it.

Seb, I hope you were right.

He thought of Kai. The friend who had become a stranger. The idealist who had become an oligarch. The person Adrian had loved like a brother and now mapped for destruction.

Kai, I hope you understand why.

He picked up the burner phone. Dialed the relay. Left the message.

"Deployment packages complete. Courier handoff confirmed. All six targets."

He ended the call, removed the SIM, and put it with the other used cards. He would burn them before leaving.

Less than twenty-four hours.

Adrian sat back down among the copies. The next updates would tell him which of those routes had survived contact with the actual systems.

The houseboat creaked. The canal lapped. And somewhere out there, spread across six countries, six people were working toward the same moment, the moment when the truth would become unstoppable.

If they lived that long.
