Chapter Forty Eight.

Raven was reorganizing her toolkit when her phone began to sing.

Not literally. The phone, a customized device with encryption that would make an intelligence agency weep, emitted a specific sequence of tones that Raven had assigned to priority contract communications. Three ascending notes, like the opening of a music box. She'd chosen the sequence because it was pleasant, and because the contrast between its pleasantness and the content it typically preceded amused her.

She was in a hotel room in Dusseldorf. A good hotel, not extravagant, not shabby, one that attracted business travelers and didn't ask questions about guests who paid in cash and kept unusual hours. The room was clean, well-lit, and organized with the precision that Raven required to function. Her clothes were hung by color and fabric weight. Her toolkit was laid out on the desk in the order she'd use it, an order that changed depending on the assignment.

She was between assignments. The last one had been in Tallinn, a movement member who'd been identified through conventional intelligence work, not the AI breach now making waves that morning. That kill had been workmanlike. Functional. Not her best work, but clean. The target had been cautious but predictable, and predictable people were, in Raven's experience, already dead. They just hadn't been informed yet.

The phone sang. She picked it up.

What she received was not a contract. It was a data package.

Specifically, it was a list. Names. Photographs. Last known locations. Affiliations. Communication histories. Movement roles. Security assessments. Psychological profiles.

Sixty-seven entries.

Raven set the phone down and looked at the ceiling. She did this sometimes when processing large amounts of new information, a reset, like clearing a cache, that allowed her pattern-recognition system to engage without visual clutter.

Sixty-seven targets. The largest assignment she'd ever received by a factor of ten. Her previous record had been a package of seven names, delivered over the course of a month, each one a carefully researched, individually briefed engagement.

This was not that. This was a phone book with crosshairs drawn on every entry.

She picked up the phone again and scrolled through the list more slowly. The entries varied in detail. Some had comprehensive profiles with photographs, addresses, known associates, and daily routines. Others were barely more than a name and a city. The quality of the intelligence was inconsistent, suggesting it had been gathered rapidly from a recently compromised source rather than developed through the patient, methodical process that characterized good contract work.

The AI breach. Someone had cracked the movement's digital shield and was now dumping the contents like a ransacked filing cabinet.

Raven recognized several names. Movement members she'd been tracking, whose operational security had previously made them untouchable. Now she had their home addresses, their children's school schedules, and their preferred coffee shops.

She also recognized the pattern. Flood the zone. Overwhelm the target population with simultaneous pressure from multiple directions. It was a suppression strategy, not about precision but about volume. Kill enough people fast enough and the survivors scatter, demoralize, collapse.

It was, in Raven's professional assessment, inelegant.

She didn't object to killing. Obviously. But she objected to bad killing, the sloppy, industrial kind that treated the craft like manufacturing instead of art. Each of her engagements was a unique solution to a unique problem. The weapon, the approach, the timing, the environment: all custom. All considered. All executed with the attention to detail that the work deserved.

Sixty-seven targets was not a series of unique problems. It was a factory order.

She scrolled back to the top of the list and began, because this is what she did, sorting. Grouping targets by location, by vulnerability, by priority. Even if the order itself was inelegant, the execution didn't have to be.

London: fourteen targets. Berlin: nine. Geneva: four. Tallinn: seven. Scattered across Europe: the remainder. Some she could reach within hours. Others would take days. A few, in locations she'd need to research, might take a week or more.

She opened a new document on her tablet and began building the operational sequence. Routes, timelines, logistics. Hotels, transportation, equipment caches. The familiar architecture of her work, scaled up to an unfamiliar magnitude.

As she worked, a part of her mind, the part that operated in the background, noticing things that the conscious mind was too busy to attend to, flagged something.

The volume was wrong.

Not wrong as in too many. Wrong as in unprecedented. In four years of contract work for the council, she'd received a total of twelve names. Each one vetted, approved, and delivered with the careful deliberation of people who understood that every kill was a risk and every risk required justification.

Sixty-seven names in a single data dump was not careful deliberation. It was panic.

Someone had opened the floodgates, and the water was coming through faster than anyone could direct it.

Raven closed her tablet and walked to the window. Traffic moved along the Rhine below. She had chosen Dusseldorf for its connections: airports, rail lines, roads reaching west into the Netherlands. From here the next contract could take her almost anywhere.

Her phone sang again. A follow-up message from the intermediary who managed her contracts.

Priority targets marked. Compensation adjusted for volume. Timeline: immediate.

Compensation adjusted for volume. As though she were a supplier being offered a bulk discount in reverse. The transactional nature of the communication didn't bother her, all her communications were transactional, but the urgency did. The intermediary, who normally communicated with the measured pace of a Swiss watch, was rushing. The carefully maintained distance between Raven and her employers was compressing.

That meant the employers were scared.

Scared employers were dangerous employers. Not because they became more violent, Raven handled violence, but because they became less rational. Rational employers gave precise orders, maintained clean operational boundaries, and respected the professional relationship. Scared employers made mistakes, changed plans mid-execution, and eventually decided that their contractors knew too much.

Raven had seen it before. Not personally, she was too careful for that, but in the histories of other professionals whose careers had ended badly because their employers' fear outpaced their judgment.

Sixty-seven targets. Immediate timeline. Adjusted compensation.

She noted the shift. Her first contracts had been specific: named individuals who posed defined operational threats. Then the criteria had broadened to include anyone with leadership roles. Then organizers. Then associates. Now sixty-seven names, some of whom were barely more than sympathizers with the wrong email address. The scope had migrated from eliminate threats to eliminate everyone, and Raven recognized the trajectory because it was always the same. When a client's target list expands faster than the justification for it, the client has stopped thinking strategically and started thinking in terms of erasure. And the final item on any erasure campaign was the person who carried it out.

She would take the work. She always took the work; that was what professionals did. But she would take it on her terms, at her pace, with her standards.

And she would, quietly and without informing anyone, begin planning for the possibility that the list would eventually include her own name.

Raven returned to her desk and resumed building the operational sequence. London was the first cluster, fourteen targets, unless the intermediary sent a higher priority. She checked the routes, the equipment caches, the travel time. The list was asking for speed. She would decide what speed was possible.

The music-box tones of her phone had heralded the end of a certain kind of war, the careful, surgical, individual kind. What was coming now was something messier.

Raven didn't do messy. But the world, it seemed, was no longer asking her preference.

She packed her bag, every item in its place, and checked out at 11:47 a.m. Her phone remained on. A revised priority was expected that afternoon.

The Dusseldorf morning was cold and clear. A morning for walking slowly. For noticing things.

Raven walked quickly and noticed everything.

Sixty-seven names. And somewhere in the digital wreckage of a compromised AI, four hundred and twelve people were waking up to discover that the invisible hand that had protected them was gone.

Open season.
