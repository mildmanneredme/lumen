Chapter Sixty Five.

Monday, 4:17 a.m. CET — thirty two hours remaining — Germany

Pain was information.

Raven had understood this since the age of fourteen, when a Krav Maga instructor in Shenzhen had broken her wrist during a training exercise and, instead of crying, she had cataloged the sensation with clinical fascination. Fracture of the distal radius. Immediate sharp onset. Swelling within ninety seconds. Loss of grip strength. Estimated recovery: six weeks with proper immobilization. She had set the wrist herself, using a splint fashioned from a rolled magazine and electrical tape, and returned to training the following week.

The cut along her flank had stopped bleeding. The damage beneath her ribs had not stopped hurting.

At a service station east of Arnhem, she had rinsed the fragment cut, pressed a clean dressing against it, and taped it in place. Her left fingers were splinted together. Every deep breath brought a sharp pain along the ribs that had struck the tub. She suspected two fractures. A clinic could confirm it; a clinic could also record her identity.

For now she had a dressing, tape, and the ability to drive. She checked them in the locked bathroom, changed her bloodied shirt, and went back to the car.

She could not keep this up for two days. She did not intend to.

At 4:17 a.m., Raven sat at a roadside cafe in Germany. The car stood where she could see it. Her shirt was clean, her jacket zipped high. Beneath it, the dressing pressed against her side with each movement.

On the table lay a printed photograph from the dead man's phone in Arnhem.

The photograph showed a text message chain. Six messages, sent over the past twelve hours, coordinating the team that had been sent to kill her. The messages were terse, professional, and included operational details: her hotel, her room number, the time she was expected to be there, and a phrase that made Raven's fingers tighten around her coffee cup.

Asset has completed final deliverable. Proceed with cleanup.

Cleanup. She was cleanup. A tool used and discarded. A line item on Harrison Polk's operational budget, categorized and filed under "loose ends."

The council had hired her to kill Sebastian Hale. She had killed Sebastian Hale. And then, within hours, they had sent a team to ensure that the person who knew what they had authorized would never be able to testify to it.

Efficient. She could appreciate the logic even as she calculated the appropriate response. In their position, she might have done the same thing. Eliminate the contractor. Remove the evidence chain. Protect the principals.

But they had failed. And Raven did not fail.

She studied the text messages again. The sending number was encrypted, routed through a series of VoIP relays that would take a competent forensic analyst days to trace. But the language patterns were distinctive. The operational terminology. The way instructions were structured: target, method, timeline, contingency. She'd seen this style before. Harrison Polk's private security consultancy used a specific lexicon, developed from Polk's years as a Washington power broker who had learned operational security from the intelligence community contacts he had cultivated for decades.

Polk had given the order. Polk, or someone very close to him.

But Raven wasn't interested in Polk. Not yet. Polk was the mechanism. The council was the engine. And the council, right now, was distracted, every company in crisis, every engineer working around the clock, every boardroom in panic mode. The men who had tried to kill her were focused inward, fighting the digital fire that the movement's dying AI had set.

They weren't watching for her. They thought she was dead.

That was useful.

Raven opened the spare burner from her go bag. Its SIM had been bought in Arnhem before the Hale contract. She composed a message to a Berlin intelligence broker, a former colleague who sold current locations and movement patterns.

Need current locations and movement patterns for seven individuals. Full names to follow. Payment in crypto within 24 hours. Standard rates.

She sent the message, then typed the names.

Not the oligarchs. She knew where the oligarchs were; they were in their fortresses, surrounded by security teams, fighting fires. They would be accessible later.

Seven different names. Names she'd heard in the course of her work for the council. Names from the intelligence briefings Polk had provided when assigning her targets. Names the council had identified as the surviving core of the movement, the people executing whatever plan the dead activist had set in motion.

Adrian Marsh. Nadia Osei. Ines Brandt. Andrei Lepp. Tomás Ferreira. Sophie Richter. Ólafur Sigurdsson.

Raven sent the names. Then she sat back and waited.

She wasn't hunting them for the council. The council had tried to kill her. The council had forfeited any claim to her services, her loyalty, and her restraint.

The surviving operation was leverage. Her employers wanted it stopped. If she found its core before their damaged surveillance did, she could decide what to deliver, what to destroy, and what price they would pay for either. The old list was the route she knew. Rage was already pulling her back along it.

Raven didn't care about democracy. She didn't care about transparency or accountability or the restoration of representative institutions. She cared about the people who had broken a contract and sent men to put bullets in her while she was in a bathtub.

She wanted her former employers to lose control. The movement offered a way to take it from them, but she would not depend on its goodwill or wait for its plan to succeed. First she would find the people who held it together. Then she would choose what they were allowed to finish.

The decision sounded orderly in her head. Her grip on the phone was not.

Her phone buzzed. The intelligence broker in Berlin.

Preliminary locations within 6 hours. Three subjects already identified in transit. Details incoming.

Raven finished her coffee, stood, and adjusted her jacket. The ribs hurt. Her left hand could not grip the wheel properly. She set the route south before starting the engine.

She would reach Munich later that morning. Her contact would have preliminary locations by then. Until that message arrived, distance was the only useful thing she could gain.

The wolf was hunting. But not for the reasons anyone expected.
