Chapter Seventy.

Monday, 5:17 p.m. CET — nineteen hours remaining

Morrow found the houseboat.

Not because he was a better detective than the people hiding Adrian Marsh. Not because his investigative skills had overcome the analog security measures that someone, the journalist, Nadia Osei, he was almost certain, had put in place. He found it because someone wanted him to find it.

The tip had reached his Zurich hotel at 7:45 a.m.: a plain envelope, an Amsterdam address, and the words He arrives this morning. Morrow checked the paper, called the liaison, and asked where it had come from. Nobody would say.

Morrow had stared at the paper for fifteen minutes before deciding to act on it. Every instinct screamed setup. Every instinct screamed trap. But he was a detective, and detectives followed leads, even when the leads followed them first.

He left Zurich at 8:17 a.m. Santos rode beside him, newly arrived from San Francisco, reading the source-record requests and the evasive replies. The road journey took nine hours, including two fuel stops. They argued through the dossier while the miles accumulated.

At 5:17 p.m., they parked near the Prinsengracht, where they could see the listed houseboat. Nineteen hours remained before the deadline they had heard in the liaison's latest briefing. Morrow still did not know why that particular hour mattered.

The houseboat's curtain was drawn. Through a gap he could see a desk lamp and a man bent over papers.

Morrow sat in the Volkswagen and watched. Santos watched too, from the passenger seat, with the stillness of a man who could sit in a car for eight hours and never once reach for his phone.

He had a warrant. Not a local one; Dutch warrants required Dutch judicial authority, and the process for an American detective to obtain one was measured in weeks, not hours. But he had a US warrant, issued by a federal judge in the Northern District of California, based on the evidence file he'd been building for three months. The warrant was for the arrest of Adrian Marsh on suspicion of conspiracy to commit computer fraud and unauthorized access to protected systems.

The warrant was solid. The evidence supporting it was overwhelming.

And James Morrow did not trust a single piece of it.

Santos set the file aside. "They won't tell us who collected it."

"No."

"We ask Marsh, then."

"We watch first." Morrow looked at the boat. The warrant in his file was American. It gave him a case to take to the Dutch authorities, not permission to conduct an arrest here. His liaison wanted him to make that request immediately.

Santos waited.

"They gave us an address they shouldn't have," Morrow said. "And a deadline they won't explain. I want to know what they're trying to interrupt before I help them do it."

Morrow looked at the houseboat. The desk lamp through the half-drawn curtain. A man inside, working on the plan the liaison wanted stopped.

He could ask the Dutch authorities to detain Marsh while they reviewed the US warrant. That request could begin now. Or he could approach the boat and ask for a conversation first. Neither choice would make the evidence less troubling.

And then what?

Then the movement loses a key operative at the worst possible moment. The oligarchs, if they were the ones feeding Morrow, get exactly what they want. And Morrow becomes the instrument that powerful people used to crush the last organized opposition to their control.

Or, alternative theory: the evidence is real, Marsh is guilty, the movement is a criminal conspiracy, and Morrow's hesitation is letting a dangerous man continue to do dangerous things.

Both theories fit the evidence. That was the problem. The evidence was compatible with either narrative, and Morrow had no way to determine which was true without more information than he currently possessed.

He needed to look Marsh in the eye.

A conversation. A question the dossier had not answered. He would listen, then check the answer against something he could verify. Instinct had brought him this far; it could not finish the investigation for him.

Santos picked up his notebook. "Then let's hear it."

Morrow switched off the engine. They would watch long enough to see whether anyone else was following the same tip, then approach.

And then he would look Adrian Marsh in the eye and make the most important judgment call of his career.

The late afternoon light thinned over the canal. Bicycles crossed the bridge. A baker carried empty trays into his shop. Morrow watched the reflection of the street behind them in his mirror.

No second surveillance car appeared. No one approached the houseboat.

That proved very little. He waited another minute anyway, then nodded to Santos.

Nineteen hours. Somewhere inside the boat, Marsh was working toward a deadline someone wanted Morrow to stop. It was time to ask him why.
