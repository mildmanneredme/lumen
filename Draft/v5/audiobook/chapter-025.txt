Chapter Twenty Five.

On Saturday morning, the vote took nine minutes.

Adrian sat outside the reinforced door in a folding metal chair, listening to the murmur of voices he couldn't quite make out, and tried to remember the last time he'd been this nervous. His tenure review? No, he'd known the committee was favorable. His first angel investment pitch? Closer. That had been a different kind of exposure: standing in front of people with the power to validate or reject his judgment, knowing that the outcome would define the next phase of his life.

This was like that, except the stakes were measured in years of imprisonment rather than percentage points of equity.

The door opened. Lin stepped out. Her expression was as unreadable as ever.

"Six to two," she said. "You're in."

Adrian stood. "Which way did you vote?"

"Against. I still think you're a liability." She held the door open. "But I was outvoted, and I respect the process. So welcome to Project Lumen, Professor. Try not to get us all killed."

He walked through the door.

The room was different now. The long table had been cleared of everything except a single laptop, a glass of water, and a manila folder thick enough to stop a bullet, or at least a strongly worded memo. The inner circle members who'd voted were still seated, some watching him with the wary hospitality of people who'd just made a decision they weren't entirely comfortable with. Seb sat at the head of the table.

"Sit down, Adrian."

First name. That was new. Adrian sat.

"The vote carries conditions," Seb said. "Three of them. First: you're compartmentalized. You'll know the full structure of Project Lumen, but your operational access is limited to the Cartographer's role. You meet the team, you complete the maps, you do not freelance."

"Understood."

"Second: you travel under identities we provide. Your passport, your credit cards, your phone, none of it crosses a border. You'll be given clean documentation for each leg. When you're moving between Lumen members, you don't exist as Adrian Marsh."

"How many legs are we talking about?"

"Five. Five cities. Five team members. Each one built their component in isolation. You need to meet them all, assess the state of Priya's work, and determine what's needed to complete it."

"And the third condition?"

Seb looked at him with those glacier eyes. "You stop feeding information to Detective Morrow."

The silence in the room had the quality of held breath.

"Yes," Seb said. "We know. We've known since your second meeting with him. The AI that manages our communications security flagged the anomaly in your contact patterns within thirty-six hours of your first debrief with Morrow."

Adrian felt the blood leave his face. "If you've known—"

"We let it continue because the information you were giving him was carefully curated. We've been monitoring your reports. You've been feeding him enough to maintain your credibility without compromising anything operational. Whether that was intentional caution on your part or simple ignorance of what would be operational, the result is the same: Morrow has a file full of leads that go nowhere useful."

"Then why bring it up now?"

"Because the game changes today. You're no longer an outsider with limited access. You're about to learn things that, if shared with law enforcement, would get people killed. Priya was killed because someone found out what she knew. The Morrow channel is a liability we can no longer afford."

Adrian thought about Morrow. A good cop. An honest man. Someone who genuinely believed he was pursuing justice and who deserved the truth. Someone who, if Adrian went silent, would know immediately that the informant had been turned.

"If I cut contact abruptly, Morrow will know something's changed. He'll escalate."

"We'll handle Morrow," Seb said. "The AI will feed him enough noise to keep him busy. False leads, dead-end trails, the digital equivalent of a wild goose chase. By the time he realizes the trail has gone cold, you'll be in another country."

"I don't love the idea of an AI manipulating a police investigation."

"I don't love the idea of anyone else dying because information leaked through a channel we should have closed weeks ago." Seb's voice was flat. Final. "This is not negotiable, Adrian. The Morrow channel dies today."

Adrian looked around the table. Eight faces watched him. Lin's was hard. The others ranged from sympathetic to impatient. Nobody was going to argue this point on his behalf.

"Agreed," Adrian said. The word tasted like something he'd have to live with.

"Good." Seb opened the manila folder. Inside were printed photographs, dossiers, travel documents, and what appeared to be hand-drawn maps. No USB drives. No digital files. Everything on paper, because paper didn't phone home.

"Let me introduce you to the team."

He laid six photographs on the table, arranged in a row. Six faces. Six lives committed to an act of radical transparency that might change the world or destroy it.

"You already know about Priya. She's the absence we're asking you to fill." Seb touched the first photograph, a woman with dark hair, intense eyes, and the expression of someone caught between two worlds. The photograph was slightly out of focus, as if taken from a distance.

He moved to the second. "Ines Brandt. The Scaffold. Berlin. She built the distribution platform, a decentralized publishing system that will make the data impossible to suppress once it's released. She is brilliant, territorial, and will absolutely hate you on sight. Don't take it personally. She hates everyone on sight. You earn her respect by demonstrating that you understand what's at stake beyond the technical."

Third. "Andrei Lepp. The Locksmith. Tallinn. Former NATO intelligence contractor. He built the zero-day exploits, the keys to every door we need to open. He's paranoid, precise, and possibly the most technically gifted person I've ever met. He'll test you. Don't fail the test."

Fourth. "Sophie Richter. The Auditor. Geneva. Former bank compliance officer. She traces money flows, the financial architecture that connects the oligarchs to every corrupted legislative vote. Without her work, the breach exposes corporate behavior but not the political capture. She is the moral center of this team, and she will make you feel simultaneously reassured and ashamed."

Fifth. "Tomás Ferreira. The Courier. Lisbon. Former special forces. He built the physical deployment vectors, the devices that have to be planted inside each company's headquarters. He doesn't talk about ideology. He talks about angles of approach and exit routes. He will care about exactly one thing: whether you can hold your nerve."

Sixth. "Ólafur Sigurdsson. The Timekeeper. Reykjavik. Twenty-two years old. A prodigy. He built the synchronization mechanism, the trigger that makes all six components fire simultaneously. He's a kid who thinks he's solving the most beautiful engineering problem of his career, and he's right, but he doesn't fully grasp what the solution will do."

Seb looked up from the photographs.

"You'll visit each one. You'll learn their components. You'll assess Priya's existing network maps and determine what's missing. And you'll complete the maps that make the entire operation possible."

Adrian stared at the six faces on the table. People who had each, for reasons that were deeply personal and entirely their own, decided to risk everything for an idea.

Priya's photograph lay at the end of the row. Five people he could still meet, and one he would know only through what she had left behind.

"When do I start?" Adrian asked.

"Tomorrow morning. You fly to Tallinn under a Canadian passport in the name of David Chen. Andrei is your first meeting because his work is the most time-sensitive. The zero-days have shelf lives, and every day that passes increases the chance that a target company patches a vulnerability he's spent months developing."

"And if they already have? If the exploits are burned?"

"Then Andrei builds new ones, and the timeline extends, and more people die in the interval. Which is why we don't waste time." Seb gathered the photographs and returned them to the folder. "Any final questions?"

Adrian had one. He'd been holding it since Seb first described Project Lumen, turning it over in his mind, testing its edges.

"Who designed this? All of it, the compartmentalized structure, the six components, the architecture of the plan. Was it you?"

Something flickered across Seb's face. It was there and gone so quickly that Adrian almost missed it, a shadow of something that might have been doubt, or pride, or the expression of a man who knows more than he's saying.

"I started the movement," Seb said carefully. "I built the first cells. I recruited the first members. The philosophy, the strategy, the decision to move from protest to direct action, that's mine. But Project Lumen..." He paused. "Project Lumen evolved. It grew beyond what I originally conceived. The right people appeared at the right time with the right capabilities, and the plan took shape organically. Sometimes I feel like I'm steering it. Other times I feel like I'm just the face of something that has its own momentum."

It was the most honest thing Seb had said to him.

"Get some sleep, Professor," Seb said. "Tomorrow, you start earning the trust of five people who have every reason to distrust you."

Adrian stood. He walked toward the door, then stopped.

"Seb."

"Yes?"

"The person who voted against me, Lin. She said she still thinks I'm a liability."

"She does."

"What did she say, exactly?"

Seb's smile was thin and knowing. "She said, 'If he's a spy, I want to be the one who pulls the trigger.' I believe she was speaking metaphorically." A beat. "Seventy percent sure."

Adrian stepped through the door and into the hallway, where the fluorescent lights hummed and the air smelled of concrete and coffee, and thought: I am no longer an observer. I am no longer an informant.

I am a participant.

The realization should have terrified him. What it actually produced was something closer to relief, the feeling of a man who had been standing on the edge of a cliff for months and had finally, irrevocably, jumped.

The fall, he suspected, was going to be extraordinary.
