Chapter Thirty.

The target's name was Marcus Webb, and he had made two mistakes.

The first was operational: he'd used his personal email to communicate with a movement contact, a lapse in protocol so elementary that Raven felt a twinge of professional disappointment. The oligarchs' surveillance apparatus had flagged the communication, traced the IP address through a VPN that was competent but not exceptional, and produced a name, a photograph, and a home address in Portland, Oregon.

The second was habitual: Marcus Webb went running every morning at 5:45 AM in Forest Park, a five-thousand-acre urban forest on the western edge of Portland. Same route. Same time. Same trail: the Wildwood Trail, a thirty-mile path that wound through old-growth Douglas fir and western red cedar, dark and deserted at that hour, with no cameras, no cell coverage, and a canopy so dense that satellite imagery was useless.

A runner. A forest. A routine.

Raven had arrived in Portland forty-eight hours earlier, rented a car under one of her cycling identities, and spent the first day walking the trail. She'd mapped the terrain with the attention to topography that a painter gives to landscape, noting sight lines, elevation changes, the acoustic properties of different forest sections (sound carried differently through deciduous stretches versus conifer stands), and the precise spots where the trail narrowed between rock outcroppings that forced a runner into a single-file path with no room to dodge.

The kill method had presented itself during that walk, as it usually did, not through deliberation but through an associative leap, the sort her brain produced when she gave it enough raw data and enough quiet. She'd been studying a section of trail where a massive western red cedar had fallen across the path. The park service had cleared the trunk, but the root ball remained, a wall of earth and roots eight feet tall, creating a natural blind spot.

The root ball. The blind spot. The acoustic dead zone created by the surrounding conifers.

She'd built the device that evening in her rented Airbnb, using components purchased from three different hardware stores in three different suburbs. The device was simple: a modified bear trap mechanism attached to a monofilament line strung at ankle height across the trail, concealed beneath the layer of fallen needles that carpeted the forest floor. When triggered, it wouldn't trap the runner. It would trip him. A stumble, at running speed, on a downhill section of trail littered with exposed roots and volcanic rock.

The forest would do the rest.

At 5:47 AM on a Tuesday morning, Marcus Webb's left foot caught the monofilament line at the precise point where the Wildwood Trail descended a rocky grade near mile marker 14. He pitched forward, arms windmilling, and his forehead struck the edge of a basalt outcropping with the dull, final sound of a coconut dropped on concrete.

Raven watched from behind the root ball. She waited three minutes, listening to the forest settle back into its pre-dawn quiet. Then she stepped onto the trail, checked the body, pupils fixed, no pulse, the impact wound consistent with a fall, and retrieved the monofilament line and trip mechanism. She pocketed the components, wiped the sections of trail she'd walked with a branch, and departed along a deer path she'd identified during her reconnaissance that led to a gravel access road a quarter-mile east.

The Portland police would classify Marcus Webb's death as a trail running accident. Forest Park recorded an average of three serious trail injuries per year, and a fatal fall on a rocky downhill section in pre-dawn darkness, while tragic, was not unprecedented. The investigation would be perfunctory. The case would close quickly.

But something about this kill bothered Raven, and it took her until she was boarding a flight at PDX, under a different name, with a different passport, to a different city, to identify what it was.

The timeline.

The clients had sent final authorization and an updated movement schedule fifty-three hours before the kill window she'd selected. Fifty-three hours was workable; she'd operated on shorter timelines. But it wasn't optimal. Her standard protocol called for a minimum of five days of target observation. She'd compressed to two because the clients had flagged Marcus Webb as time-sensitive. He was scheduled to travel internationally, and once he left US soil, the jurisdictional complications multiplied.

Fifty-three hours had been enough to build a clean kill. But it hadn't been enough to build the kind of kill she took pride in, the kind where every variable was mapped, every contingency planned, every exit route tested twice. She'd improvised the monofilament approach based on a single day of trail reconnaissance. It had worked. But it had worked the way a first draft works: functionally, not elegantly.

Raven did not produce first drafts.

The problem was volume. The first contract, the cell member in custody, had been a single contained operation, with a fixed hearing date and a target whose location could not change. A ghost job. Beautiful in its simplicity. The second, Priya Chandrasekaran, had been more complex but still allowed for proper preparation. The maintenance worker approach, the binary agent, the self-erasing evidence chain. Craftsmanship.

But now the targets were coming faster. The clients' surveillance was producing names at a rate that outpaced her ability to give each kill the attention it deserved. She was being asked to work like a production line. She was not a production line. She was an artisan.

She had been diagnosed with ADHD in childhood. In her professional life, she had come to treat the fluctuations in her attention as both an asset and a liability. When she was engaged, when the puzzle was fresh, the variables novel, the creative challenge stimulating, her focus was absolute. She could hold twelve variables in her working memory simultaneously, see connections that others missed, and produce solutions that were genuinely innovative. The kills she was proudest of were the ones that nobody recognized as kills at all.

But when the work became repetitive, when the targets blurred together, when the approach patterns started to rhyme with previous jobs, when the novelty drained out and left only execution, her attention fragmented. She found herself skipping steps. Shortcutting reconnaissance. Making decisions based on instinct rather than data, and instinct was reliable only when backed by thorough preparation.

She was approaching the edge of something. She could feel it the way a tightrope walker feels the first tremor of the wire: not yet a wobble, not yet a fall, but a vibration that said the margin was shrinking.

Raven stared out the airplane window at the cloud layer below, white and featureless, and made a clinical inventory.

Four kills in six weeks. Each one clean. Each one undetected. But the quality curve was flattening. The Portland job was functional but inelegant. The next one would need to be better, or the pattern of "accidents" and "natural causes" would start to look less like coincidence and more like what it was.

She needed to slow down. She needed to tell the clients that the pace was unsustainable, that quality required time, that a single detected kill would compromise the entire program.

She had asked once, on the batch containing the Denver target. The extension had been refused. Since then she had learned to phrase questions as requests for better intelligence rather than more time. The available hours remained the same.

So she would maintain the pace. She would find the creativity. She would continue to produce kills that looked like anything but kills.

And she would watch the margin shrink, and she would manage it, because managing shrinking margins was what she did.

It was what she'd always done.

The plane descended through the cloud layer, and Raven closed her eyes and began to think about the next name on the list.
