Chapter Fifty Nine.

At 2:17 a.m., an hour after they gathered, they scattered like seeds thrown into a high wind.

Nadia had arranged the departures with the precision of a symphony conductor and the paranoia of a Cold War spymaster, which, Adrian reflected, were essentially the same skill set applied to different genres. No two team members left through the same exit. No two traveled by the same method. No two knew anyone else's route.

The assignments had been given in individual briefings, each team member pulled aside by Adrian and Nadia in a corner of the bookshop basement, given their instructions in whispered conversations that lasted no more than five minutes. No paper. No recordings. Memory only.

Tomás was first to leave. His assignment was the most physically demanding and arguably the most dangerous: retrieve the cached hardware devices from Lisbon, test each one, then deploy them to three target locations across the United States. Cole's headquarters in Austin. Strutt's complex in Menlo Park. Venn's fortress in Seattle. Three devices, three buildings, three of the most heavily secured corporate campuses in the world.

"You'll have new papers waiting in Lisbon," Nadia told him. "American passport, business consultant cover. Flights are booked under the alias, paid cash through a travel agent in Faro who doesn't ask questions."

Tomás nodded. "Equipment?"

"The Lisbon courier will meet you at the airport. The devices are coming out of the cache now."

"Checked?"

"Storage seals intact. You do the final inspection before the westbound flight."

Tomás's face betrayed nothing. "I've traveled worse," he said, and Adrian believed him completely. A man who'd spent a decade in Portuguese special forces and another decade installing security systems for the oligarchs themselves did not complain about the smell of fish. Tomás knew every building he needed to penetrate because he had helped design the security for two of them. He knew the camera angles, the guard rotations, the maintenance windows. He knew which doors were electromagnetic and which were mechanical. He knew where the server rooms were because he had installed the access control systems that protected them.

The poacher had once been the gamekeeper, and now he was poaching again.

Ines left second, heading to Berlin to activate the Scaffold. Her distribution platform, the decentralized, peer-to-peer publishing network she'd built over two years in her Kreuzberg factory, was the delivery mechanism for everything. Without it, the stolen data had nowhere to go. Without it, the breaches were just vandalism.

"I'll bring the surviving clusters up from Berlin," she told Adrian. "The activation is public, but the control path is separate. A watcher can see the signal without getting access to the nodes."

"How do you activate nodes without digital communication?"

"They monitor public code repositories," Ines said. "A specific pattern in a signed commit wakes them. I don't have to connect to each one directly."

"That's... elegant."

"Berlin," she said, shouldering her bag. "I'll report the count when I have it."

Andrei left third, heading back to Tallinn to retrieve the exploit drives and run final checks on each zero-day. He was the palest Adrian had ever seen him, which was saying something for a man who appeared to subsist on black coffee and existential dread.

"Each exploit is loaded into a deployment package," Andrei explained, his voice barely above a whisper. "The packages are delivered through Tomás's hardware devices. When a device is planted, it checks in with a dormant relay and verifies its staged payload. That does not execute the exploit. It waits for the authenticated activation command. Ólafur synchronizes those commands; then the exploits run and extraction begins." He paused. "In theory."

"In theory?"

"I've never tested them against live systems. I couldn't, without tripping the target companies' intrusion detection. Each exploit was built from intelligence gathered over months: vulnerability analysis, patch schedules, security architecture mapping. The intelligence was..." He trailed off. "It was too good. I always wondered where it came from."

From inside the council, Adrian thought. Laine, perhaps. Or someone they hadn't considered. He still had no evidence he could test, only the precision of information delivered through layers of separation.

"They'll work," Adrian told Andrei, with more confidence than he felt.

"One chance," Andrei said. "First attempt. If any exploit fails, the target company patches and we lose that breach forever." He met Adrian's eyes. "My hands are shaking."

"Mine too," Adrian admitted.

Andrei almost smiled. It was the first time Adrian had seen anything approaching warmth from the man. Then he was gone.

Sophie departed for Geneva. Her financial forensics tools were stored on air-gapped systems in a rented vault, actual physical vault space in a building that had been storing other people's secrets for four hundred years. The tools would parse the financial data extracted during the breach, mapping money flows in real-time, connecting oligarch accounts to political donations to legislative outcomes. Without Sophie's tools, the breach would produce a flood of raw data. With them, it would produce evidence.

"I need the data streams routed to my systems the moment the breaches are active," Sophie told Adrian. "The parsing begins immediately. Within two hours of activation, I can produce a complete financial map showing every dollar that flowed from the council to every compromised legislator in the US, UK, and EU."

"Two hours?"

"I'm very good at my job, Adrian. I've been very good at my job for thirty years. The only thing that's changed is that now the job matters."

Ólafur was heading to London, not Iceland, where his original setup had been compromised, but London, where Nadia had arranged a safehouse with the hardware he'd need to rebuild the trigger mechanism. The synchronization system, the component that ensured all breaches fired simultaneously, was the linchpin. Without it, the operation was a series of isolated attacks, each one giving the remaining targets time to lock down.

"The architecture is in my head," Ólafur said. "The synchronization logic runs on ordinary hardware. I need servers, stable power, and measured routes to each implant. The calibration takes time."

"Nadia's arranging the hardware. It'll be waiting at the London safehouse."

Ólafur nodded. Then: "Adrian? I want you to know that the trigger system is the most beautiful piece of engineering I've ever built. The timing precision... it's like conducting an orchestra where every musician is on a different continent and the piece has to begin on exactly the same beat."

Despite everything, the fear, the grief, the crushing weight of what they were attempting, Adrian felt a flicker of something almost like admiration. The boy was twenty-two. He was scared. He'd been crying an hour ago. And he was still, beneath all of it, in love with the elegance of his own creation.

That's what Seb saw in him, Adrian realized. Not just talent. Wonder.

"It is beautiful," Adrian told him. "Make it work."

Nadia would coordinate from London, setting up in a separate safehouse from Ólafur's. She would be the central switchboard, the human node through which all communication flowed. Burner phones, couriers, dead drops, hand-delivered messages. Every update from every team member would route through Nadia. Every instruction from Adrian would route through Nadia. She was the replacement for the AI, running on trust instead of algorithms.

"If I go dark," she told Adrian, "it means I'm compromised. If that happens, the fallback is Sophie's vault in Geneva. She has a direct relay to each team member. Sophie becomes the switchboard."

"And if Sophie goes dark?"

"Then we're in considerably more trouble than a backup plan can fix."

Which left Adrian himself.

His assignment was twofold. First: complete Priya's network maps. The internal architectures of each target company, the digital blueprints showing where source code was stored, where communications lived, where financial records flowed. Priya had mapped four of the six targets before her death. Adrian had been filling in the gaps using his own knowledge from years as a tech investor, knowledge of infrastructure, of server architecture, of how companies organized their most sensitive data.

He was good at it. Better than he'd expected. The years he'd spent evaluating tech companies for investment had given him an intuitive sense of how digital empires were structured, and the mapping work tapped into skills he'd forgotten he possessed.

Second: coordinate the overall operation. Timing, sequencing, contingency management. The job nobody wanted and everybody needed someone to do.

He would work from a safe house in, he paused as Nadia handed him the details, Amsterdam. Close enough to European transport networks. Far enough from any target to avoid suspicion. Anonymous enough, in a city full of tourists.

"Amsterdam?" he said.

"You'll be in a houseboat on the Prinsengracht," Nadia said. "Belongs to a retired Dutch intelligence officer who spent twenty years watching the Soviets and now grows tulips. He'll leave the key under a ceramic frog on the deck."

"A ceramic frog."

"Dutch intelligence officers have a particular sense of humor."

Adrian looked at the empty basement. The wine bottles. Seb's notes. The chair nobody had sat in. Nadia was checking the last route on her list. His driver was outside.

Thirty-four hours remained.

Adrian picked up his bag, a single backpack containing two changes of clothes, a canvas folder of handwritten notes, three burner phones, four thousand euros in cash, and a dog-eared copy of Priya's network diagrams rendered in her meticulous hand on paper so thin he could see the light through it.

He walked out into the Zurich night. A courier waited beside a car. Amsterdam was eight hours away.

Seven people. Thirty-four hours. Separate routes and a shared deadline.

If this was what democracy looked like when it fought back, Adrian thought, it could really use a better logistics department.

But it was what they had. And it would have to be enough.
