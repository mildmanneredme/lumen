Chapter Twenty Two.

Three days after the meeting in the basement, Adrian found himself sitting across from Sebastian Hale in a different location: a rented house in the Oakland hills with a view of the Bay Bridge and the kind of aggressive normalcy that made its use as a revolutionary safe house almost funny. There was a fruit bowl on the kitchen counter. Someone had put up curtains.

"I told you I'd show you everything," Seb said. "I meant it."

He opened a laptop, air-gapped, running a custom Linux distribution, the sort of machine that made Adrian's university-issued MacBook look like a children's toy, and turned it to face Adrian. On the screen was a map. Not a geographic map, though geography was part of it. A network map. Nodes and connections spanning continents, linked by lines of varying thickness and color. A timestamp in the corner dated the snapshot to the previous night. There was no live connection.

"What am I looking at?" Adrian asked, though some part of him already knew.

"You're looking at Project Lumen."

Adrian recognized the outline from the document sent to his anonymous phone. Six components, built separately, intended to converge. Seeing the locations attached to them changed the scale of it: Berlin, Tallinn, Silicon Valley, Lisbon, Geneva, Reykjavik. Places where people were sitting at desks, making choices, expecting to survive them.

"I know the outline," he said. "What I don't know is how far you've got."

"Far enough that delay is becoming dangerous. Not far enough to proceed." Seb selected the Silicon Valley node. "The Cartographer. Her name is Priya Chandrasekaran. She's a senior infrastructure engineer inside one of the target companies. She maintains the systems by day and maps them for us by night."

"She maps all six companies?"

"Her own, in detail. For the others, she has contacts. Former colleagues, people she trusts. Some know what she's doing. Most don't."

Adrian watched a set of incomplete diagrams fill the screen. "Then she's also mapping the limits of what they can tell her."

"Exactly. Four targets are complete. Two aren't. Priya has asked for help with those gaps. She can't keep approaching her contacts without making the pattern visible."

"Does she know you brought me here?"

"She knows I'm looking for someone. I haven't given her your name."

Seb slid a sheet of paper across the table. Two network sketches, several blank spaces, a note in handwriting Adrian did not recognize: Don't assume these services share an authentication boundary.

He read the note twice. "She's right. They may have integrated the business without integrating the permissions."

"That's the answer I needed to hear. You spent seven years reviewing architectures for due diligence, sitting on advisory boards, asking where the data went when a company bought another company. Priya has access you don't. You have a view across the industry she can't obtain from inside one employer. I want you to work with her."

"You want an investor's memory to finish an engineer's maps."

"I want you to tell her which assumptions are justified and which ones could get someone killed. She'll check the details. You won't be working alone."

Adrian set the sheet down. The request had become more frightening as it became more plausible.

"And the synchronization?" he asked. "The outline treats 'simultaneous' as if putting it on a diagram makes it happen."

"The hardware is installed and armed before activation. The Timekeeper is designing the schedule so the activation commands reach all six targets within four hundred milliseconds of one another."

"Arrival spread. Not the time it takes to break into a system."

"Right. Each local operation still needs seconds to complete. But once activated, it doesn't wait for another instruction from us. The fastest warning from one company to another, and a lockdown that could stop the second company, takes at least five seconds. All six operations have to be committed before that warning arrives."

Adrian studied the incomplete maps again. "So these gaps are part of the timing problem too. A wrong route inside a target, an unexpected permission check—"

"And the local operation stalls. Which is why I need you to help her."

Seb closed the laptop.

"You're asking me to join a conspiracy to commit the largest corporate espionage in history."

"I'm asking you to help restore democracy. The framing is your choice."

"The framing is a courtroom's choice, and courts tend to use words like 'espionage' and 'conspiracy.'"

"They do. And when the data is public, I suspect the court of public opinion will use different words."

"Priya is putting her freedom on that suspicion."

Seb did not answer immediately. "She's putting it on the work. You should ask her yourself, if you decide to join us."

Adrian was quiet for a long time.

"I need to think," he said.

"Three days," Seb replied. "Then I need an answer. Priya can't hold those gaps open indefinitely."

He showed Adrian to the door.

On the drive home, no blindfold this time, which Adrian interpreted as either a gesture of trust or an acknowledgment that he was already too deep to matter, he stared out the window at the San Francisco skyline and tried to remember the last time he'd felt certain about anything.

It had been a while.
