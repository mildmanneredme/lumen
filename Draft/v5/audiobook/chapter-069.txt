Chapter Sixty Nine.

Monday, 9:17 a.m. Austin time — twenty hours remaining

At 9:17 a.m., Austin was fully awake. Traffic backed up along the roads into the corporate campuses. Delivery trucks waited at service gates. Contractors in work shirts moved between buildings carrying equipment for systems damaged the day before. Tomás joined them.

Magnus Cole's headquarters occupied a campus on the northeast side of the city, a sprawling complex that Cole had designed, or at least claimed to have designed, as a "temple of innovation." In practice, it looked like someone had given an architect unlimited money and a brief that consisted entirely of the words "make it look like the future." The result was a collection of angular glass-and-steel buildings connected by covered walkways, surrounded by manicured landscaping, and protected by a security apparatus that was, in Tomás Ferreira's professional estimation, approximately 70 percent theater and 30 percent substance.

He had reached Austin little more than an hour earlier on the direct charter from Lisbon. Under the name James Pereira, he was a Portuguese-American facilities consultant assigned to emergency remediation. Nadia's travel contact had booked the reserve flight before the breach. The packet described a real maintenance window; his presence in it was the forged part.

He had used the interval to check the service entrance, identify the contractor check-in desk, and compare the morning's procedures with the access material in his packet. The emergency had added guards. It had also added workers whose names the guards did not know.

The security had changed. Cole had upgraded the visible elements: the gate barriers were heavier, the guard count was higher, the cameras were newer. But the backend hadn't changed. The server building, a low, windowless structure at the northeast corner of the campus, still used the same access control system Tomás had evaluated and recommended replacing four years ago. He'd submitted a report identifying the system's vulnerabilities. Cole's facilities team had read the report, acknowledged the vulnerabilities, and decided that replacing the system was "not a current budget priority."

Budget priorities. The most dangerous words in security.

He parked at the contractor lot and put on a gray work shirt. The bag over his shoulder held the device and tools. At the check-in desk, a guard compared his reserve passport with the service order, called a facilities supervisor, and gave him a temporary visitor badge.

The supervisor wanted the damaged power monitoring unit in the server building checked before noon. Tomás asked where he could find the mechanical room. The guard pointed him toward it.

Inside, he checked the approved unit, wrote two measurements on the form, then moved to the adjacent maintenance passage. It connected to the server room's cable run. The old panel lock was where he remembered it. He opened it with the cloned key in his packet.

The passage was narrow. He stayed low beneath the cable bundles and reached the inner access panel. This was the boundary his work order did not authorize him to cross. Four bolts held it in place. He loosened them from the service side and eased the panel inward.

A door opened somewhere beyond the room. Tomás stopped. Footsteps crossed the corridor and faded. He waited another ten seconds before putting his bag through.

At 9:41 a.m., he stood inside the server building's mechanical room. The authorized maintenance task had brought him close. The rest would leave no entry on the visitor log.

The server room was on the other side of a steel door that required badge access. Tomás reached into his bag and produced a cloned access badge, fabricated from specifications he'd documented during his security consultation, updated with current encryption parameters that Andrei had extracted from the intelligence data. He held it to the reader.

The light turned green. The lock clicked.

Thank you for not updating your access control system, Mr. Cole. Budget priorities.

The server room was a cathedral of technology, row after row of racks, each one humming with processors and blinking with status LEDs, the combined output creating a low, continuous drone that Tomás felt in his chest more than heard with his ears. The temperature was precisely 18 degrees Celsius. The air tasted of filtered nothing.

He moved between the racks with the economy of motion that two decades of tactical training had encoded into his nervous system. No wasted steps. No unnecessary noise. Each movement planned two steps ahead, each corner checked with the fiberoptic camera before he committed his body.

The target rack was in row seven, the corporate communications infrastructure. Tomás identified it by the cable management labels and the rack position that matched Adrian's deployment map. He knelt, opened his bag, and removed the device.

It was beautiful, in its way. A matte-black box, indistinguishable from the legitimate networking equipment surrounding it. Custom electronics inside a cloned shell. Tomás connected it to the rack's power distribution unit, then ran an Ethernet cable from the device to an open port on the network switch above it.

The device's status LED blinked once. Twice. Then settled into a steady green, the same color and cadence as every other device on the rack.

Invisible.

Tomás checked the connection. Solid. He cable-tied the Ethernet cable to the existing cable bundle, ensuring it followed the same routing as the legitimate cables. He positioned the device behind a cable management panel that would make it invisible to anyone who wasn't specifically searching rack by rack, port by port.

Total time in the server room: four minutes and thirty-seven seconds.

He returned through the access panel, replaced its bolts, and followed the maintenance passage back to the power monitoring unit.

He locked the panel and finished the service form. A loose lock or an unfinished task would invite someone else to follow his route. He handed the measurements to the supervisor and signed out at the contractor desk.

Details. Tomás lived in the details. The details were where operations succeeded or failed, where the difference between a clean extraction and a catastrophe lived, where the training that had been beaten into him over twenty years of military and security work expressed itself as the quiet, meticulous discipline of a man who left no trace.

Back in the sedan, he drove toward the airfield. His reserve connection to California was already scheduled. There would be time to change clothes there.

One device planted. Two still with him: Menlo Park and Seattle. The secondary operatives handling Laine, Barrington, and Nakamura had their installation windows and separate courier chains. Nadia would confirm each plant.

He picked up the burner phone. Dialed the relay.

"First delivery complete. Austin. Clean. Moving to Menlo Park."

The relay acknowledged. He removed the SIM and returned the phone to his bag.

Then he ate the last of Dona Maria's pasteis de nata, cold now, the custard firm, the pastry softened, and thought of Ana. His sister, who had followed the truth into a company's supply chain and died for it. His sister, whose death had transformed a competent security professional into a weapon aimed at the people who had killed her.

He missed her. He missed her with a constancy that had become its own kind of companion, a presence inside his chest that never dimmed and never spoke and never needed to, because the missing was the message, and the message was: I am still here because you are not, and every door I open is a door you should have walked through.

One device down. The Showman's fortress had a parasite in its veins.

In the airport waiting room he set an alarm for the boarding call and closed his eyes. Twenty minutes, if nothing interrupted. He slept when he could.

Austin carried on outside. In Cole's server building, a small black box blinked green among the legitimate switches, waiting for the command.
