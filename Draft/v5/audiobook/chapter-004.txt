Chapter Four.

Harrison Polk had three phones. The first was for his wife, his children, and the handful of Washington acquaintances he permitted to believe they were his friends. The second was for the lobbying firm: six hundred employees across four offices, a client list that read like the Fortune 10, and a billing rate that would have made a cardiac surgeon feel underpaid. The third phone was for the calls that mattered.

He used the third phone now, standing in the kitchen of his Georgetown townhouse at six in the morning, wearing a cashmere robe he'd bought in Milan and slippers his daughter had given him for Christmas that said WORLD'S OKAYEST DAD. He'd found the slippers funny. Harrison Polk had a sense of humor about himself, a survival adaptation more valuable, in Washington, than intelligence.

The number he dialed belonged to no contact list. It existed in his memory alone, a string of digits he'd committed to the same mental vault where he stored the real reasons behind every major piece of legislation that had passed through Congress in the last two decades.

Two rings. Then a voice like gravel poured over ice.

"It's early for you, Harrison."

"Clive, when I call you at six a.m., it means I have a reason to call you at six a.m."

Clive Barrington was in London, where it was eleven in the morning, which meant he was exactly two Scotches into his day and therefore at peak cognitive function. Barrington's media empire spanned three continents: cable news networks, newspaper chains, digital platforms, and a streaming service that had convinced sixty million subscribers to pay monthly for the privilege of being told what to think. He was seventy-three years old and operated with the metabolic energy of a man saving every last morsel of energy in case it extends his life.

"The arrest in San Francisco," Polk said. "NovaMind."

"I've seen it. My people are covering it. Corporate espionage, dull stuff."

"It's not dull, Clive. And it's not corporate espionage. Not only corporate espionage."

A pause. Barrington listening rather than waiting to speak — a distinction lost on most people, but not on Harrison Polk, who had built a career on reading the silences of powerful men.

"I've spoken with Elias," Polk continued. Elias Strutt, whose social networking empire generated more data about human behavior than every intelligence agency in the Western world combined. "His people have been tracking something. Chatter. Encrypted, decentralized, unusually sophisticated. They've been seeing fragments for months. Patterns that suggest coordination without a center, like a nervous system without a brain."

"Or a brain that's very good at hiding," Barrington said.

"Exactly. Elias thinks the arrest is connected. The suspect wasn't stealing corporate secrets for a competitor. He was mapping NovaMind's internal systems, their architecture, their vulnerabilities, the layout of their most sensitive infrastructure. The kind of reconnaissance you do before an invasion."

"An invasion of what?"

"That's the question, isn't it?"

Polk could hear Barrington thinking. The old man processed information like a newsroom: rapidly, through the lens of narrative, always asking the same question — what's the story?

"How concerned should I be?" Barrington asked.

"Concerned enough to clear your calendar for a trip to Seattle next week."

Another silence. Longer this time. A trip to Seattle meant Arthur Venn's territory, and Venn didn't host social gatherings. If Polk was suggesting a meeting at Venn's estate, the situation had moved beyond phone calls.

"Who else?" Barrington asked.

"Everyone."

The word landed with the weight Polk intended. In twenty years of managing the delicate, fractious, occasionally homicidal relationships between the men who actually ran the Western world, Harrison Polk had never once called them all together. There had been bilateral meetings — Strutt and Venn coordinating on cloud policy, Barrington and Polk managing a congressional investigation, Magnus Cole alienating everyone at a private dinner and then buying something that made them need him again. But all of them, in one room? That was a card you played once.

Polk was playing it.

"Even Cole?" Barrington asked, and the distaste in his voice was audible even through encrypted satellite transmission. Magnus Cole was the member of their informal fraternity that the others tolerated the way a body tolerates a low-grade fever: unpleasant, occasionally useful, and impossible to eliminate without consequences worse than the disease. His empire had been built on a single breakthrough — an algorithm that dramatically increased the speed of internet connections, a piece of intellectual property so fundamental that every major tech company on the planet licensed it. Cole's protocol was the plumbing beneath the data economy, the unlock that had made the rest of their empires possible. Which made him both indispensable and insufferable.

"Even Cole. And Laine." Geoffrey Laine, one of the first tech founders — his company had been building operating systems when the rest of them were still in school. He'd stepped back from the industry years ago and now spent more time at the United Nations than in any boardroom. What had started as a transparent publicity stunt to rehabilitate his reputation had turned, against everyone's expectations, into a genuine ambition. Laine wanted to broker agreements between nations that had stopped talking to each other, to build bridges that seasoned diplomats said couldn't be built. He was surprisingly good at it. He was also the most likely to object to anything that sounded illegal, not because he was naive, but because he'd staked his second act on being taken seriously by people who cared about international law.

"And the young one? Nakamura?"

"Kai will be there," Polk said. "He needs to be. His platform is the largest distribution channel for the demographic most likely to be sympathetic to whatever this movement is. If there's a coordinated threat to our interests, his algorithm is either part of the problem or part of the solution."

The truth was simpler. Kai Nakamura was the youngest member of their circle by five years, the least established, and the most underestimated. His short-form content platform, NovaTok, had grown from a novelty app into the dominant information ecosystem for an entire generation in less than a decade. The other oligarchs had watched this happen with the bemused condescension of men who had built their empires on hardware and infrastructure watching a child build a castle out of fifteen-second videos. They didn't understand his power. Polk did. You didn't need to own the roads if you owned the conversations happening inside every car.

"Next Thursday," Polk said. "Venn's estate. Dinner, then a private session afterward. I'll send details through the usual channel."

"And if I have scheduling conflicts?"

"Cancel them."

Polk hung up. He stood in his kitchen, the morning light of Georgetown filtering through plantation shutters selected by a decorator his wife had hired, and made two more calls. Then he set the third phone on the counter and poured himself coffee from a French press.

He was thinking about the arrest. About Daniel Yoon, twenty-six years old, sitting in a cell in San Francisco, who had somehow penetrated one of the most secure technology companies in the world with the precision of a surgeon and the planning horizon of someone who thought in years, not weeks. About the chatter Strutt's systems had been tracking: decentralized, encrypted, patterned like a living organism.

About the phrase Strutt had used, the one that had made Polk pick up his third phone before dawn: organized at a scale that suggests institutional backing.

Not a lone hacker. Not corporate espionage. Something else.

In twenty years of managing power at its most concentrated level, Harrison Polk had developed an instinct for threat detection that was almost preternatural. It was the reason the most powerful people in the Western world took his calls. He could feel shifts in the tides of influence — not through any single data point, but through a sub-conscious mind, finely tuned from years of experience on the Hill.

The pattern he was feeling now was unfamiliar. And that, more than anything, was what frightened him.

Because Harrison Polk was afraid of very little. But he was deeply, professionally afraid of the things he could not recognize.

Something was moving beneath the surface of the world he controlled. Something organized, patient, and invisible.

And it had just made its first mistake.
